Before he drew a roofline, Eero Saarinen timed passengers with a stopwatch. He was not timing construction crews. He was timing travelers — how long they walked, where they bunched, how far the gate sat from the curb. His first question about Dulles, by his own account, was not *what will it look like* but *how do we solve the passengers walking for miles* [Source: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/eero-saarinens-revolutionary-design-dulles-airport/7853/]. His answer was the most radical de-sequencing bet in the history of American aviation: nineteen custom Chrysler-Budd mobile lounges that carried travelers directly from a single check-in hall to aircraft parked as far as a mile out on the apron, eliminating fixed concourses entirely [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport]. No gate holds. No pier walk. No sequence. The passenger boarded a lounge and the lounge became the gate.

The concept was dead in twelve years. Hub-and-spoke throughput economics — banks of flights arriving and departing in tight waves, connecting passengers who needed to move plane-to-plane fast — made a fleet of slow rolling rooms unworkable [Source: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/eero-saarinens-revolutionary-design-dulles-airport/7853/]. The $22 billion plan MWAA is now advancing finishes the burial: it extends the AeroTrain to the concourses and eliminates the mobile lounges from regular operation entirely [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. And yet the thing Saarinen actually built — the soaring catenary-roofed headhouse — is a National Register structure at sixty-four years old, and the new program keeps it and extends it three hundred feet in each direction [Source: https://byerussell.substack.com/p/a-22-billion-program-for-dulles].

That is the whole argument, written into the airport's own concrete. The *processing concept* died in twelve years. The *shell* is protected at sixty-four.

Here is the thesis, narrower and harder than the one that is usually posed. The danger in a terminal designed today is not that its planners will fail to forecast biometrics or eVTOL or off-airport check-in. The danger is that they will pour fifty-year concrete around *any* passenger-processing sequence — the centralized one they know, or the distributed one they are tempted to bet on — and thereby convert a five-to-fifteen-year decision into a fifty-year liability. The move that survives to 2050 is not a prediction. It is a refusal to predict: lock the durable geometry hard and oversized, keep the processing layer deliberately soft and richly provisioned, and treat every sequence commitment as reversible. Dulles's own history is the best evidence for that discipline, because Dulles has already paid — twice — to rip out a processing model it had hard-wired into the building.

## Executive summary

1. **Dulles is spending its cheapest asset to buy the most expensive kind of certainty.** The airport ran a cost-per-enplanement of $9.56 in FY2025, cheaper for airlines than all but a handful of US large hubs, where the range runs from $3.94 at Atlanta to $30.16 at LAX [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad] [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. The $22 billion program — $21.8 billion in new bonds — proposes to issue four to five times MWAA's entire existing $4.7–4.9 billion aviation-enterprise debt against that advantage [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Any future-proofing move that permanently raises CPE is not future-proofing. It is spending the airport's core commercial asset. (The $9.56 figure is compiled from MWAA filings by a secondary aggregator, not read from the audited ACFR, and should be verified against MWAA's own statements before it is quoted to a board [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad].)

2. **The cheap-hub advantage is already eroding before the first program bond is issued.** Signatory CPE is rising from $9.56 in FY2025 toward $12.77 in the first half of 2026 on the ~$5.5 billion of debt MWAA already plans through 2028 — pushing coverage down toward 1.3x and debt-per-enplanement toward $400, from $223 in 2024 — before the $22 billion program adds a dollar [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. The program does not start the clock on Dulles's cost problem; it accelerates a clock already running.

3. **The $22 billion figure is a political anchor, not an engineering estimate.** Responses to the same DOT RFI priced the work from $14.4 billion (Ferrovial, for a rebuild of today's airport) to $35–50 billion (Phoenix/Ironbridge, for a 50-million-passenger expansion — roughly double 2025 volume) [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. The scopes differ, so the spread is not a clean apples-to-apples disagreement — but even bracketed for scope, the uncertainty is wide enough that the headline should be read at ±50 percent, and the program's own materials concede it excludes "any potential delays or cost overruns" [Source: https://byerussell.substack.com/p/a-22-billion-program-for-dulles].

4. **The mega-terminal overrun record is the base rate, and it almost always breaks at the same place: the novel integrated system on the critical path, not the poured structure.** Berlin Brandenburg opened nine years late at 2.3–2.6x budget on a bespoke smoke-extraction system [Source: https://www.euronews.com/2020/10/31/berlin-airport-opens-10-years-late-and-three-times-over-budget]; Denver's automated baggage ran ~$560 million over and was decommissioned in 2005 [Source: https://sebokwiki.org/wiki/Denver_Airport_Baggage_Handling_System]; the LAX people mover is +76 percent and three years late [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report]; Heathrow T5 came in on time and on budget and still lost more than 42,000 bags in its first ten days [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Terminal_5]. Kansas City delivered forty gates for $1.5 billion on time and on budget — with no bespoke system on its critical path [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date]. The one recurring exception proves the rule about where money should go: Denver's Great Hall renovation blew up partly on weak 1990s concrete found under the existing terminal — an unknown-existing-conditions risk that lives in the renovation packages, not the new-build ones [Source: https://www.constructiondive.com/news/great-hall-claims-dias-lack-of-engagement-contributed-to-airport-renovatio/567441/].

5. **For a decade, coordination has beaten concrete.** FAA wake-turbulence recategorization gave Memphis a ~20 percent capacity gain — up to 22 more arrivals an hour — with no new pavement [Source: https://www.mobilityengineeringtech.com/component/content/article/25559-recat-wake-turbulence-recategorization]; airport collaborative decision-making cut European taxi-out times 10–20 percent with a data protocol, not a taxiway [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making]; and legacy hubs run 30–50 percent of latent gate capacity unused because they work each gate 3–6 times a day where a low-cost carrier works one 10.3 times [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. The building is rarely the binding constraint until the coordination levers are exhausted — and most airports have not touched them.

6. **The durable commitments are geometric; the volatile ones are procedural — and the split is knowable now.** Walking distance, apron and taxilane clearance, baggage-system topology, and curb geometry are fifty-year physics operations can never fix after the pour [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/] [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-03-12/pickups-at-kansas-citys-new-airport-terminal-are-a-nightmare-heres-how-officials-say-to-fix-it]. Checkpoint lane count, gate assignment, turn discipline, check-in fit-out, and every operational-intelligence product are five-to-fifteen-year layers that software and rule changes keep improving — and are scheduled to be replaced two to five times inside the building's life while it operates [Source: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-298.pdf].

7. **The genuinely open design questions are federal control questions with no schedule, and the last time federal politics moved on Dulles, it moved against MWAA.** Distributed screening is one PreCheck-only DHS prototype at Las Vegas with no announced deployment timeline [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]; the 58-year-old DCA perimeter rule that built Dulles's long-haul franchise was eroded in 2024 over the unanimous objection of both home-state Senate delegations [Source: https://thehill.com/homenews/4655051-senate-passes-faa-reauthorization-after-dca-slots-fight/]. And the money to provision for these futures is itself constrained: the PFC cap has been frozen at $4.50 since 2000, and the BIL terminal grants sunset September 30, 2026, before the first concourse is poured [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/pfc] [Source: https://aaae.org/uscta-alerts/appropriations-leaders-unveil-final-fy26-dot/faa-and-dhs/tsa/cbp-spending-bills]. Provision for the open futures; do not draw the building around them.

8. **The building cannot buy back United's network math.** United carries roughly 68 percent of Dulles traffic and is investing in the airport right now — a >$500 million, 14-gate Concourse E opens late 2026 [Source: https://simpleflying.com/where-united-airlines-washington-dulles-passengers-go/] [Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/more-than-half-a-billion-dollar-expansion--modernization-coming-to-uniteds-washington-dulles-hub-302320375.html]. But Pittsburgh built to US Airways' specification and was dehubbed at a CPE of $6; Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Cleveland were each left carrying hub-scale infrastructure when the carrier's math turned — and each sat at the top of the carrier's investment list a decade before it was cut [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport] [Source: https://simpleflying.com/united-airlines-cleveland-hub/]. Size the connecting-hub scope soft and convertible, and condition it on a matched-duration carrier commitment, because no terminal design solves a dehubbing.

## The argument

### Start with the one number that disciplines every other decision

Before the renderings, there is a single figure that should sit at the head of the table: $9.56. That is what it cost an airline, per enplaned passenger, to operate at Dulles in FY2025 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. Across US large hubs, cost-per-enplanement runs from $3.94 at Atlanta to $30.16 at LAX and $29.56 at Chicago O'Hare [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. Dulles sits near the cheap end. That is not an accident of accounting. It is the airport's defining commercial asset — the reason it remains, in United's own capital math, worth flying.

One honest caveat travels with that number. It is compiled from MWAA filings by a secondary aggregator rather than read from the audited ACFR, and MWAA's own finance staff will know their statements better than any outside source; verify it before it goes in front of the board [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. The point survives verification either way, because the number is already moving in the wrong direction. Signatory CPE is climbing from $9.56 in FY2025 toward $12.77 in the first half of 2026, on roughly $5.5 billion of debt MWAA plans through 2028 — before the $22 billion program adds anything — driving coverage down toward 1.3x and debt-per-enplanement toward $400, up from $223 in 2024 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. The cheap-hub advantage is eroding on its own schedule. The program does not create the cost problem; it accelerates one already in motion.

Now set the program against it. The $22 billion plan proposes $21.8 billion in new bonds plus $1.1 billion in fees [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. MWAA today carries roughly $4.7–4.9 billion of principal across its aviation enterprise [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. The program therefore proposes to issue four to five times the airport's entire existing debt — at a hub whose whole advantage is that it is cheap. Under MWAA's residual rate structure, with "no contractual cap on the cost level that can be recovered through the rate formula," incremental debt service passes mechanically to the airlines [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Every dollar of the program either protects the CPE advantage or spends it.

That is the frame that turns "future-proofing" from a design aesthetic into a capital-allocation problem. The question is not how to make the building impressive in 2050. It is which dollars buy something that survives, and which buy something that will be obsolete before the bonds are half-retired.

### The building itself already answered the question, in 1962 and again in 1972

The temptation in any future-proofing exercise is to treat the future as unprecedented. Dulles cannot afford that flattery, because it has run this experiment before and the tape is still readable.

Saarinen's mobile-lounge terminal was a distributed, de-sequenced processing model — exactly the direction toward which processing is said to be heading now. It died not because Saarinen forecast badly but because the throughput logic underneath it changed: hub banking rewarded fixed concourses and fast plane-to-plane connection, and a fleet of rolling rooms could not keep up [Source: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/eero-saarinens-revolutionary-design-dulles-airport/7853/]. The concept was obsolete in twelve years. The shell endured for sixty-four and counting. Dulles has since paid twice to unwind that hard-wired sequence: first the ~$1.4–1.5 billion AeroTrain that opened in 2010 to begin replacing the lounges [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AeroTrain_(Dulles_International_Airport)], and now the $3.75 billion AeroTrain extension in the current program that retires them for good [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/]. Two payments, one processing bet, gone.

Kansas City tells the same story on a different clock. Its 1972 three-terminal cloverleaf let passengers drive right up to their gate — a design "before security and before the hub-and-spoke system" [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2023/03/02/kansas-citys-new-terminal-opens-trading-gate-proximity-for-making-everything-else-better/]. Then, on a single morning in September 2001, the processing sequence the whole building was organized around became illegal. Federalized screening under ATSA forced consolidated, sterile-side checkpoints at 429 airports [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_and_Transportation_Security_Act]. The thin circular geometry that made drive-to-gate elegant made post-9/11 screening nearly unworkable, and it took twenty-two years and a $1.5 billion single terminal to undo [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date].

Two buildings, two eras, one lesson. When a terminal is organized around a processing sequence, it is the sequence that dates — the check-in hall depth, the checkpoint location, the drive-to-gate access — while the structural bones outlast it by decades. None of these buildings dated at the span or the column grid.

### The failure taxonomy, run through the airports the program should study

Catalog the recent large-hub terminals and disposition each by the single design decision that dated or detonated fastest. The pattern is almost monotonous: it is the novel, integrated, single-vendor system on the critical path — almost never the poured concrete.

- **Berlin Brandenburg (2020):** the fastest-dating design decision was a counter-based check-in hall drawn for an era in which online and mobile check-in had already hollowed out that function — dated on opening day — while the schedule was detonated by a bespoke smoke-extraction system "not built according to the construction permit" and signed off by a man qualified as a draughtsman, not an engineer [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport].
- **Heathrow Terminal 5 (2008):** the structure aged fine; the baggage-system integration failed on day one — a test software filter never removed cost more than 42,000 bags and 500 flights in ten days — and the baggage layer was still failing in 2026, long after the concrete was signed off [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Terminal_5] [Source: https://spectrum.ieee.org/baggage-problem-hits-heathrow-terminal-5-].
- **Denver automated baggage (1995):** the canonical case — a $193 million contract past $400 million, sixteen months late, never run at scale, decommissioned by 2005 in favor of a manual system [Source: https://sebokwiki.org/wiki/Denver_Airport_Baggage_Handling_System]. And the Great Hall renovation two decades later is the one genuine structural exception: weak 1990s concrete found under the existing terminal, an unknown-existing-conditions failure directly relevant to Dulles's own renovation of 1960s–1980s concourses [Source: https://www.constructiondive.com/news/great-hall-claims-dias-lack-of-engagement-contributed-to-airport-renovatio/567441/].
- **Istanbul (2018):** the fastest-dating decision was sheer geometry — 20–30 minute walks to the farthest gates, locked on day one and un-coordinatable [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/]. That is a geometry decision, which is exactly the layer the thesis says you must get right, because you cannot fix it later.
- **Beijing Daxing (2019):** the counter-example. Its committed radial geometry — farthest gate under eight minutes and 600 meters from center — aged into praise, not regret [Source: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/zaha-hadid-daxing-international-airport-beijing-09-26-2019/]. Hard commitment to durable geometry was the right bet.
- **LaGuardia Terminal B (2020):** a headhouse-plus-concourse typology with fully centralized processing, delivered on time and on budget within an $8 billion program and named the best new terminal in the world [Source: https://dbia.org/project/a-whole-new-laguardia-terminal-b-redevelopment/]. The conventional typology, superbly executed, has not dated.
- **Kansas City (2023):** forty gates, $1.5 billion, on time and on budget, with no bespoke system on its critical path [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date]. The distinguishing variable was scope discipline, not square footage.
- **Changi Jewel:** the one bet this Council did not research and will not pretend to. Jewel's wager was that dwell and destination retail could become the terminal's reason for being. Whether that revenue model is durable is an open question the program team should own, and it belongs in the commercial section below, not in a taxonomy the evidence cannot support.

For a capital planner, this is not a horror-story recitation. It is a spending instruction. The systems that dated and detonated everywhere — automated baggage, bespoke life-safety, first-of-kind people movers, counter-based check-in — are precisely the volatile layer. The structures that quietly outlasted them are the durable layer. Overbuild the durable, unretrofittable layer — structural bays, apron geometry, power and comms conduit — and keep the security regime, the sensing, and the fit-out deliberately soft, because those are the exact systems that dated fastest everywhere else.

### Coordination has been beating concrete, and Dulles has not exhausted it

There is a quieter body of evidence that should unsettle anyone about to triple a gate count. Over the last decade, the largest measured capacity gains in US and European aviation came not from new pavement but from new rules and better information-sharing.

The cleanest natural experiments are the ones where nothing physical changed. FAA wake-turbulence recategorization reclassified aircraft separation by wing design and approach speed rather than weight alone, letting planes fly closer together. At Memphis this produced roughly a 20 percent capacity boost and up to 22 additional arrivals per hour — no runway, no taxiway, a rule [Source: https://www.mobilityengineeringtech.com/component/content/article/25559-recat-wake-turbulence-recategorization]. Airport collaborative decision-making tells the same story on the surface: EUROCONTROL credits A-CDM with cutting taxi-out time 10–20 percent across European airports, the product of a shared, accurate off-block time rather than any concrete [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making].

The gate data is more pointed — and it comes with a caveat the honest version of this argument must carry. "Full" gate utilization is conventionally 6–8 turns per gate per day. Delta's hubs ran 2.9 (Memphis) to 6.3 (Atlanta) daily turns; a low-cost carrier's focus cities ran 7.2 (Phoenix) to 10.3 (Baltimore) [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. That comparison is not apples-to-apples: a connecting hub deliberately *banks* flights into arrival and departure waves to manufacture connections, so its gates sit idle between banks by design, and comparing it to point-to-point low-cost flying overstates the gap. The source is also dated — a circa-2012 deck, and Delta left Memphis in 2013 — so treat the exact figures as directional. The recoverable margin is real but bounded: the operations evidence puts latent legacy-hub capacity at 30–50 percent, not an unlimited multiple, and it is recoverable *within* a banked operation through common-use gating and tighter turn discipline, not by pretending a hub can run like Southwest [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. Dulles today has 139 gates serving 29 million passengers against a master-plan ambition toward 90 million [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport] [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2025/07/17/new-master-plan-for-dulles-airport-envisions-growth-to-90-million-annual-passengers/]. The instinct is to multiply gates. The utilization data says look harder first.

And gate count, properly understood, is a function of turn *variance*, not turn *time*. A full A320 turn runs 35–45 minutes, but even top-quartile short-haul carriers average 8 minutes of ground delay per turn, with observed variance near 19 percent [Source: https://simpleflying.com/narrowbody-turnaround-times-time-differences-guide/]. Planners size gates for the bad day, not the average one. Every minute of variance you fail to squeeze out is a fraction of a gate you must build as pure buffer. Buy down the variance with coordination and you buy down the gate count.

None of this argues that Dulles needs no new concrete. It argues *sequence*: pull the coordination levers first, so you build the right, smaller amount of the expensive thing. A $22 billion program sized to today's uncoordinated peak is funding coordination debt in structural steel.

### What operations can never fix is where the money must go

The coordination argument has a hard boundary, and honesty requires naming it, because it is exactly where Dulles must not skimp. Some constraints are physics, and when they are wrong no software rescues them.

Walking distance is physics. Istanbul imposes 20–30 minute walks to its farthest gates; Beijing Daxing's radial starfish caps the farthest gate under eight minutes and 600 meters [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/] [Source: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/zaha-hadid-daxing-international-airport-beijing-09-26-2019/]. No scheduling app closes that gap; concourse length and vertical-circulation placement are locked on day one. Baggage topology is physics: once the conveyor and sortation geometry are poured, you live with them for decades, and under-provisioning them is an error operations cannot undo. The curb is physics too — Kansas City's celebrated new terminal traded gate proximity for a better building and then watched its pickup roadway back up to the highway because curb and cell-lot capacity were mismatched to demand [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-03-12/pickups-at-kansas-citys-new-airport-terminal-are-a-nightmare-heres-how-officials-say-to-fix-it].

These are the commitments to overbuild now, because retrofit is either impossible or ruinous. They are also, tellingly, the least glamorous line items — apron clearances, baggage halls, roadway geometry — the first things sacrificed to fund a signature interior. That inversion is the recurring failure mode, and it is exactly backwards.

### Provision the substrate; never marry the product

Every operational-intelligence system Dulles might buy — A-CDM, predictive turnaround, an integrated ops center, a digital twin, biometric flow analytics — has a useful life of five to fifteen years and gets re-platformed on that cadence. None belongs to the fifty-year spine.

The honest read on this category is that its independently measured returns are small-but-compounding and its large returns are all modeled. A-CDM's audited benefit is minutes per departure; the widely quoted "$900 million per airline" perfect-turn figure is a vendor projection authored by its own CEO with no independent auditor named [Source: https://www.assaia.com/resources/ai-optimised-aircraft-turnarounds-are-unlocking-billions-in-long-term-value-for-airports-and-airlines]. Digital twins have a wide deployment gap: only DFW operates an established US large-hub twin, and 17 of 31 large hubs remain in pilot or planning [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/twin]. And the dominant failure mode across the category is organizational — more than half of analytics pilots are abandoned after proof-of-concept, and deployments without executive sponsorship are three times likelier to stall [Source: https://intuitionlabs.ai/pdfs/enterprise-ai-rollout-failures-causes-and-case-studies.pdf].

The design implication is clean. Do not organize the terminal around any specific product; every one of them will be replaced twice before the building is fifty. Do organize it to be instrumentable to the teeth: dense conduit and power at the apron and in the terminal, camera and sensor sightlines with dedicated data paths, generous comms risers, and — the one genuinely durable software decision — a clean, open operational-data architecture any future vendor can plug into. Schiphol's operational intelligence runs on more than 80,000 sensors; that instrumentation layer is a built-form decision, not a software one [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/twin]. Retrofitting sightlines and conduit into a finished mega-terminal is the expense you cannot undo. The software you can always re-buy.

### The renewal tail: $22 billion is not the cost of ownership

There is a further reason the volatile layer deserves this discipline: it is not merely replaceable, it is *scheduled* to be replaced, and the schedule runs inside the operating building. The $22 billion headline is capital plus financing, not the thirty-year cost of ownership. Baggage systems, HVAC and chillers, elevators and escalators and moving walkways, apron and airfield lighting, and the AeroTrain rolling stock all reach end-of-life on staggered 5-to-25-year cycles and must be renewed under traffic [Source: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-298.pdf]. A sponsor that capitalizes glamorous new construction and underfunds the renewal cadence is manufacturing its own next crisis: GAO found that deferring investment "increased construction times and costs," and that preventive maintenance returns $4 to $10 for every $1 spent on time [Source: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-298.pdf]. The 2050 story the operator actually lives is not the ribbon-cutting; it is the renewal cycle. Budget the cadence into the durable layer's provisioning, or pay for its absence in emergency retrofits.

### The synthesis: refuse to hard-wire the sequence

Put the evidence together and it converges on a single discipline from more directions than any one lens could reach. Overbuild the conduit and the column grid; keep the security regime soft. The durable layer's electrical, structural, and spatial reserve is the spend you cannot recover later at any price. Spend on the geometry operations can never fix; stay cheap on everything a rule change keeps improving. Provision the substrate and re-buy the software. Lock the fifty-year bones and treat every processing commitment as reversible — because Dulles already paid twice to rip out a hard-wired one.

That is the thesis in its defensible form. Not *the terminal will open dated because processing is decentralizing* — that is a forecast, and forecasts are how you strand a building. The defensible claim is that pouring fifty-year concrete around any processing sequence, today's or tomorrow's, converts a fast-moving decision into a slow-moving liability. The move that survives is to build the durable geometry hard and oversized and leave the volatile layer genuinely, cheaply soft.

## The counter-case, honestly presented

There is a serious argument that the thesis, even in this disciplined form, buys expensive optionality against changes that are unproven, slow, and — where they have been available for decades — chronically failing to scale. A planner who believed it would build the robust conventional thing superbly and pocket the difference. The case deserves its strongest statement.

Start with screening, because the thesis leans on it hardest, and the trend runs the other way. Screening distributes only if you are willing to multiply the scarcest, most regulated resource in the building: certified officers. Every credible operator move of the last five years has run toward consolidation to maximize officer productivity, not away from it. Kansas City's new terminal centralized its checkpoint specifically "to handle larger crowds," consolidating standard, PreCheck, ADA, and Touchless ID lines into one location [Source: https://www.kctv5.com/2026/04/10/kci-centralizes-security-checkpoint-handle-larger-crowds/]. TSA's own 2025 solicitation seeks to "reduce total operating costs, workforce requirements, and manual labor" — fewer officers per passenger, which distributed gate-level screening structurally opposes [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2025/07/25/tsa-seeks-private-sector-solutions-enhance-airport-security-and]. The checkpoint persists because labor is the binding constraint and consolidation is how you economize on it.

The federal structure hardens this from a trend into a control question. Distributed screening is not a design choice MWAA gets to make. Under 49 U.S.C. §44901, TSA controls the checkpoint, and self-service screening exists as exactly one PreCheck-only DHS prototype at Harry Reid in Las Vegas, with TSA stating it will study results before announcing any deployment timeline [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]. Betting built form on decentralized screening by 2035 is betting on an unscheduled federal rulemaking. And CT scanners — the actual near-term certainty — are larger, heavier, and more power-hungry than the units they replace, pushing the *centralized* checkpoint to grow before anything distributes [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/].

Off-airport processing is a thirty-year-old idea that never became the dominant mode. Hong Kong's Airport Express in-town check-in and Vienna's City Air Terminal have offered curb-to-city baggage and check-in since the 1990s; both remain optional, airline-dependent, rail-dependent, and partial, with some facilities suspended [Source: https://www.hongkongairport.com/en/baggage/airport-express-check-in.page] [Source: https://viennaairport.com/en/passengers/arrival__departure/city_check-in]. Three decades of availability without a tipping point is the single strongest data point against the claim that processing is about to migrate off-site inside a 10–25 year window.

The failure taxonomy the thesis leans on may be a category error. BER, Heathrow T5, and Denver did not fail because they froze a processing sequence. They failed on governance, fire-suppression engineering, baggage integration, truncated testing, and scope creep. Attributing execution disasters to "built form sized for the wrong decade" launders a management story into an architecture story. The lesson those projects actually teach is *build the conventional thing competently* — and the proof is LaGuardia Terminal B, a headhouse-plus-concourse typology with fully centralized processing, delivered on time and on budget and named the best new terminal in the world [Source: https://dbia.org/project/a-whole-new-laguardia-terminal-b-redevelopment/].

Flexibility, meanwhile, is an option you pay for up front and usually never exercise. The real-options terminal-design literature's own headline case — Zurich — found the flexible alternative worth "approximately 5% higher than the best conventional phased plan," and that is the optimized result under favorable assumptions, not the median [Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23249935.2023.2287502]. Soft space, flexible column grids, and over-provisioned services carry real capital and floor-area cost today against a payoff that arrives only if a specific future materializes on schedule. Most soft space gets leased, walled, and forgotten. A $22 billion program funded by $21.8 billion in new bonds must survive rating agencies, an airline use-and-lease negotiation, and a board — and "we left it soft in case screening decentralizes" is the first line item cut when the budget tightens and the first thing blamed if the terminal underperforms.

And there is a decisive geometry bet that aged beautifully by refusing to stay soft. Beijing Daxing committed hard to a radial starfish sized so the farthest gate sits about eight minutes from the center — an irreversible structural decision now praised for minimizing walking across the world's largest single-building terminal [Source: https://www.businesstraveller.com/features/phoenix-rising-beijings-new-daxing-international-airport/]. Commitment, not softness, produced the durable win.

Then there is induced demand, the objection most likely to be raised across the program table. Air travel grows 4–5 percent a year, and expanded capacity reliably fills; runway and apron geometry, not software, is the binding constraint at mature hubs [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191261523000231]. On this reading, sizing durable assets to genuine peak is the decision that ages *best*, because demand rises to meet whatever you build — and the "pull coordination levers first, then build smaller" instruction risks under-building a hub that will fill anything it is given.

And beneath all of it, an even more basic reframe: this is not a bet on any processing paradigm at all. It is depreciation-driven renewal of a worn-out 1962 plant. Structure carries a ~50-year design life; the mobile lounges are a 60-year-old artifact; Dulles is spending $22 billion because the physical plant is obsolete [Source: https://www.lfspaceframe.com/news/structural-design-of-airport-terminal-building-part-1/]. Renewal decisions should be governed by lifecycle and throughput certainty, not by speculative de-sequencing. If that framing is right, the thesis is answering a question the program is not asking.

The sharpest counter comes from the airline side of the table: the real risk at Dulles is not a dated design; it is a departed carrier. United carries roughly 68 percent of Dulles traffic, and it has stood at the edge of dehubbing IAD once already — stopped not by anything MWAA built but by the fact that Newark could not physically absorb the traffic [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/united-going-kill-washington-dulles-hub/]. Pittsburgh built a ~$1 billion midfield terminal to US Airways' specification and was dehubbed in 2004 at a cost-per-enplanement of just $6 [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport]. Cincinnati fell from 22.7 million passengers in 2005 to under 6 million by 2013 and demolished a concourse [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966692315001970]. Against risks of that magnitude, arguing about checkpoint softness is a small matter. Build the robust, financeable, legible thing — runways, aprons, structural bays, a working AeroTrain — and stop hedging bets the security economics and thirty years of non-adoption say will not pay.

## Why the counter-case is insufficient

The counter-case is not wrong. It is, in several places, more right than the reflexive version of the thesis, and the honest move is to concede those places by name before explaining why the thesis still holds.

**Concede screening entirely.** Do not distribute the checkpoint. The labor economics, the TSA solicitations, and the single-prototype status of self-service screening all point one direction, and it is consolidation. The screening regime has ratcheted toward centralization for a quarter-century and has never once reversed [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_and_Transportation_Security_Act]. Drawing the building around distributed gate-level screening is a bet against the clearest regulatory grain in the industry, and it should not be made. But note the second half of the operations finding, which the counter-case skips: do not *oversize* the central checkpoint either. CT throughput gains and self-service both raise passengers-per-square-foot, so the hall the 2026 team draws risks being too big by 2040 even as it stays central [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/]. The instruction is not "distribute." It is "lock the checkpoint's location and secure-boundary logic; leave its lane count and machine mix soft." That is the thesis, not the counter-case.

**Concede off-airport processing as a design driver.** Thirty years without a tipping point is real evidence, and no responsible plan organizes a building around curb-to-city check-in arriving by 2035. But the concession has a floor. "It never scaled in 30 years" can be a phase-change setup rather than a verdict — off-airport check-in stayed niche partly for want of the rail and identity infrastructure that is now arriving, and Dulles has Metro rail that the Hong Kong and Vienna models always required [Source: https://viennaairport.com/en/passengers/arrival__departure/city_check-in]. Induction breaks at inflection points. The correct treatment is neither to build for it nor to foreclose it: keep it an unfunded option, provisioned through the same soft check-in halls and clean data architecture the thesis already recommends for other reasons.

**Concede that the mega-terminal failures were execution failures.** BER's smoke system, Denver's baggage, T5's software filter — every one traces to governance, testing, and bespoke systems, not to a frozen processing paradigm. The counter-case is correct that these are management stories. But it draws the wrong conclusion. The thesis never claimed those buildings failed *because* they hard-wired a sequence. It claims what the record confirms: the fastest-dating, most-detonating layer is always the novel integrated system, and the durable structure quietly outlives it. That is the *same* observation the counter-case makes when it praises Kansas City's scope discipline and LaGuardia's clean execution. Both sides agree the concrete is not the problem. The only disagreement is whether you should therefore leave the volatile layer soft — and the discipline the counter-case admires (keep novel systems off the critical path, build conventional structure superbly) *is* the durable/volatile split under a different name.

**Concede that flexibility's financial payoff is modest — and then reject the framing.** The 5 percent Zurich figure is real, and any recommendation resting on financial option value alone would be weak [Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23249935.2023.2287502]. But the thesis does not rest there. There are two different things called "flexibility," and they must be separated. *Program and fit-out flexibility* — movable walls, soft space held vacant — is the 5 percent bet, and it often does get leased and forgotten. *Deep-service provisioning* — conduit, power density, riser capacity, floor loading, structural spare, floor-to-floor height — is a different animal. It is cheap to install in new slab and structure and ruinously expensive to retrofit into an occupied terminal. Even the strongest opponent of flexibility concedes this outright: overbuilding power and riser capacity for electrified ground equipment and unknown IT load "is not speculation — it is the cheapest insurance in the building." Electrified ground-support equipment is a substation-and-conduit decision in the durable layer, and Sea-Tac has already installed 576 electric charging locations across its concourses [Source: https://xantrex.com/about-xantrex/blog/work-truck/evaluating-gse-electrification-strategies-choosing-the-right-approach-for-airports/]. Apron-edge power and transformer-vault space are cheap to pour now and brutal to add to a finished, operating apron. The provisioning claim is not an options-pricing argument. It is an asymmetry-of-retrofit-cost argument, and on that ground even the opposition surrenders.

**Concede Daxing — and read it correctly.** Yes, a hard geometry bet aged beautifully. But look at *what* Daxing committed to: apron geometry, walking distance, radial circulation — the fifty-year physical bones. It did not hard-wire a check-in typology or a screening regime into that starfish. Daxing is not evidence against the thesis; it is the thesis executed. Commit hard to the durable geometry, stay soft on the processing layer. That is precisely the split.

**Concede the induced-demand point in general — and deny it locally.** Nationally, capacity does fill; the growth curve is real. But Dulles specifically has a lived record that cuts the other way, and it is sitting unused in its own files. The 1997 expansion nearly tripled handling capacity to ~40 million a year — and the airport then *lost* roughly 200,000 passengers to Reagan National between 2011 and 2013 when the perimeter rule loosened [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport]. Dulles has built ahead of demand that then failed to arrive on schedule, because at this airport demand is not set by the size of the building; it is set by federal slot policy and one carrier's network math. "Demand fills whatever we build" is a reasonable prior at a constrained gateway. It is a dangerous one at a hub that has already watched capacity sit while traffic left for a downtown competitor.

**Concede the asset-renewal reframe — and note it makes the framework more urgent, not less.** The program *is* depreciation-driven renewal of a worn-out plant; that is largely true, and it is a healthier framing than "a bet on de-sequencing." But renewal is the *only* moment the durable/volatile split can be acted on. You do not get to re-pour the column grid or re-cut the apron geometry in a mid-life refresh; you get to decide those things exactly once, when you rebuild. That makes the discipline more load-bearing, not less: the renewal is precisely when you choose what to lock for fifty years and what to leave soft, and getting that choice wrong is what strands a building for the next fifty.

**Concede the dehubbing risk is larger than the design risk — and watch it reinforce the thesis rather than defeat it.** This is the counter-case's most important point and its biggest strategic error. No terminal design solves a United departure; the building cannot buy back network math; Pittsburgh and Cincinnati are the relevant precedents. United's live investment — the >$500 million, 14-gate Concourse E opening late 2026, +25 percent departures versus 2023, 13 new routes in 2024 — is real and recent [Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/more-than-half-a-billion-dollar-expansion--modernization-coming-to-uniteds-washington-dulles-hub-302320375.html]. But Cleveland and Cincinnati sat at the top of the carrier's investment list a decade before they were cut; commitment is a snapshot, not a covenant [Source: https://simpleflying.com/united-airlines-cleveland-hub/]. So ask what the *building* should do given that risk. If the connecting-hub complex is sized to today's peak in fixed, single-tenant geometry — a 33-gate regional concourse hard-built to United's specification — then a dehubbing strands structure, exactly as it stranded Pittsburgh's Concourse E and Cincinnati's demolished Concourse C. If instead that same complex is built to *convert to common use* — gates that can be shed or re-tenanted without moving columns, apron clearances that swing between one widebody and two narrowbodies via MARS stands — then a dehubbing costs the airport traffic but not stranded concrete [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. The dehubbing risk is the strongest possible argument *for* soft, convertible processing geometry. The counter-case names the risk correctly and then recommends the response — "build the robust committed thing" — that maximizes the damage when the risk lands.

So the concessions are real and they are large. Do not distribute screening. Do not oversize the central hall. Do not organize the building around off-airport processing. Build the conventional typology and execute it superbly. Every one of those is now in the recommendation. What survives all of them is the core: the durable geometry gets locked hard and oversized, the processing sequence stays soft and convertible, the substrate gets over-provisioned because retrofit is unaffordable, and single-tenant scope gets built to convert. Of the counter-case's strongest objections, only induced demand is a genuinely adverse argument — and Dulles's own DCA leakage answers it. The rest, read closely, are either concessions the recommendation now absorbs or arguments for the thesis wearing the opposition's colors.

## Two dated snapshots: the terminal in 2035 and in 2050

Leadership needs the ten-year and twenty-five-year pictures distinct, not blurred into an average.

**2035 — the operator's day.** Check-in counters are half gone; most passengers cleared identity on their phone and dropped bags at a biometric belt in under a minute. The central checkpoint still exists but runs fewer, faster CT and self-service lanes at higher throughput per square foot, so the hall is smaller than it was drawn in 2026. Coordination has squeezed turn variance, so gates run closer to 7–8 productive turns. MARS-capable stands flex daily between one widebody and two regional jets as United's fleet mix shifts. The bag system, not the checkpoint, is the peak-hour constraint. The building feels efficient because the volatile layers were left soft and have already been upgraded twice [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/] [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/].

**2050 — the operator's day.** Screening may be partially distributed to secure sub-zones, and the central hall the 2026 team refused to hard-wire has been re-partitioned twice. Some gates now serve mixed fleets — single-aisle long-haul, regional, and whatever the small-aircraft question resolves into — off the same apron geometry, because the clearances were poured wide in 2028. Former check-in halls are dwell, lounge, and revenue space. What still constrains the airport in 2050 is exactly what constrained it in 2026: apron geometry, walking distance, baggage topology, and the curb [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/]. The airports that dated badly are the ones that locked the volatile layer and skimped on the durable geometry. Dulles's job is to invert that.

## The commercial model, and the honest limits of the evidence

A $21.8 billion bond program is serviced by aeronautical *and* non-aeronautical revenue, and the durability of the revenue model — retail, dwell, parking — is a structural question at Dulles precisely because the debt is multi-decade [Source: https://reason.org/wp-content/uploads/modernizing-passenger-facility-charge.pdf]. If processing time compresses and journeys personalize, dwell does not vanish; it relocates. The check-in halls that empty as counters disappear are the most convertible revenue space in the building — dwell, lounge, and concession floor that can be captured if the geometry allows it and stranded if it does not. That is one more reason to leave the landside processing zone soft rather than hard-walled.

Two exposures deserve naming. First, parking and curb revenue are hostage to ground-transport shifts; the same rail and ride-hail trends that might one day feed off-airport check-in also erode the parking revenue the building is partly sized around. Second, the destination-retail bet — Changi Jewel's wager that the terminal itself becomes the reason to come — is unproven at a US connecting hub and outside this Council's evidence base.

This is the weakest-supported section of the report, and it is more honest to say so than to manufacture confidence. No brief modeled dwell-spend elasticity or the landside/airside revenue split for Dulles. The program team should treat the revenue architecture as an open question with real money attached, not a settled assumption — and should note that "leave the check-in halls convertible" is a revenue hedge as much as an operational one.

## Sustainability as a physical constraint, not an aspiration

Decarbonization makes physical demands of a terminal expected to run in 2050, and the cheap-now/ruinous-later asymmetry applies with full force. Electrified ground-support equipment is not an operations line item — it is a substation, transformer-vault, and conduit decision in the durable layer, and real deployments are large: Sea-Tac installed 576 electric charging locations across all concourses [Source: https://xantrex.com/about-xantrex/blog/work-truck/evaluating-gse-electrification-strategies-choosing-the-right-approach-for-airports/]. Two specifics matter for design. Fully electrified three-shift GSE implies charging windows that today's Level 2 chargers — roughly eight hours — cannot serve, so the apron must be provisioned for fast-charge *power density*, not merely outlets [Source: https://xantrex.com/about-xantrex/blog/work-truck/evaluating-gse-electrification-strategies-choosing-the-right-approach-for-airports/]. And thermal-load headroom in the electrical backbone is among the cheapest things to overbuild in new structure and among the most expensive to add to a finished apron.

The evidence thins beyond GSE, and the report should say so. SAF logistics is fundamentally a fuel-farm and hydrant decision on the airfield, and on-site generation is a substation-and-roof-structural-load decision — both durable-layer commitments that are cheap to provision and costly to retrofit, but neither was modeled in depth by the Council's evidence base. Name them as durable-layer provisioning questions for the program team, and do not pretend to more precision than the sources support.

## The gate and apron future, and a benchmark the program team must run

The 1:1 gate-aircraft relationship is already dissolving where airports chose to dissolve it. MARS stands let one position serve either a widebody or two narrowbodies, and remote-stand operations let Frankfurt add capacity without lengthening the pier [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. The apron geometry and taxilane clearances that make those swings possible are fifty-year physics; the decision to use them on any given day is soft. Provision the geometry now; leave the operating mode adjustable.

The run's benchmark question — how ICN, DOH, Changi T5, and the ORD Global Terminal are hedging stand flexibility today, and what it costs them — the Council can only partly answer. The mechanism is well supported: swing and MARS stands are real, deployed hedges, and the industry confirms them in use at Incheon, Doha, and O'Hare. The per-airport *cost* of those hedges is not in the evidence base, and this report will not invent it. It belongs on the program team's desk as a targeted study: pull the stand-flexibility cost data from those four programs directly before sizing Dulles's apron, because the answer bears on how many convertible stands the connecting complex should carry.

## Implications for MWAA and the Dulles program

This is not a generic terminal. It is a specific program with named packages, a named anchor tenant, a named federal sponsor, and a compressed schedule, and the framework has to survive contact with all of them. The moves below are sorted and, within each bucket, ranked — with the top ones anchored to the program's own decision calendar.

**The phasing is the risk, and it is already visible in the schedule.** The program compresses a roughly two-decade master plan into eight years, 2026–2034, with three major packages running concurrently around a live United hub: a new concourse plus Concourse A renovation (~$6.2 billion, construction from about April 2027), demolition of Concourse C/D and a new 33-gate Concourse B for United regional flying (~$2.26 billion, from about January 2028), and a $3.75 billion AeroTrain extension (from about January 2028, targeted December 2033) [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/]. Concurrency at one airport is multiplicative, not additive; every interface between two simultaneous programs is a claim waiting to happen. And eliminating the mobile lounges *requires* the AeroTrain extension complete before the lounge fleet can retire — so the passenger-access system has zero schedule slack. If the tunnel slips, the whole gate program's usability slips with it [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. Build the Heathrow T5 risk-holding governance and the LaGuardia build-new-before-you-demolish phasing in now, or pay for their absence in claims later.

**Design FOR now (ranked).** First, and binding at the April 2027 construction start: lock the CBP-driven international geometry — one-way sterile corridors, mandatory separation of arriving passengers, the FIS baggage hall — because the Airport Technical Design Standards revise on a stable three-year cycle and are a genuine fifty-year durable constraint [Source: https://www.sskies.org/images/uploads/subpage/PARAS_0002.CBPATDSCompanionGuide.FinalReport.pdf]. But build the FIS *geometry* for the 2050 ceiling while phasing FIS *fit-out and gate count* against CBP staffing reality: CBP officers are appropriated annually, the Reimbursable Services Program rents only overtime, and a concourse designed for international growth CBP will not staff is a stranded asset in the most expensive part of the building [Source: https://www.cbp.gov/border-security/public-private-partnerships/reimbursable-services-program/faqs]. Second: lock the central checkpoint's location and secure boundary. Third: lock wide column grids, generous floor-to-floor heights, apron and taxilane geometry, and the vertical-circulation and utility spines. This is the widebody international gateway function — anchored by United's immunized Atlantic joint venture and by DCA's perimeter and slot cage — which is the least-reversible part of United's commitment and the part with the longest half-life [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2024/10/21/uniteds-atlantic-joint-venture-guides-how-united-grows/].

**Accommodate LATER (ranked by retrofit-cost asymmetry).** First, the substrate that is cheapest now and most ruinous later: apron-edge power, transformer-vault space, conduit density, fast-charge power density, generous comms risers, and a clean open operational-data architecture. Second: MARS and swing-stand clearances so one position serves a widebody or two narrowbodies as United's fleet mix shifts [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. Third: checkpoint structural bays, floor loading, and boundary flexibility for a checkpoint that can both grow with CT and later thin with self-service. Fourth: check-in halls convertible to dwell, lounge, and revenue space as counters vanish. Above all, build Concourse B's 33 United regional gates to *convert to common use* — the Pittsburgh risk in modern form is a single-tenant concourse that strands when the tenant's math turns, and MARS-capable, re-tenantable geometry is the hedge [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport].

**Bet AGAINST, explicitly.** Sizing the building for today's uncoordinated peak before the coordination levers are pulled. Distributed or off-airport screening as a built-form organizing principle — provision for it, do not pour concrete around it, because TSA has scheduled nothing [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]. eVTOL-at-scale apron geometry as a load-bearing assumption. Any single-vendor, first-of-a-kind life-safety or baggage system with no operational fallback — the BER and Denver failure mode, and avoidable. And a permanent, hard-walled row of check-in counters, the one piece of processing geometry already known to be dying.

**A note on the financing tension the provisioning argument creates.** Every provisioning dollar is a bonded dollar that raises the CPE the opening of this report defends, and the self-help revenue that might soften that is frozen: the PFC cap has sat at $4.50 since 2000 (a fourth failed raise in 2024), the BIL terminal money sunsets September 30, 2026 — before the first concourse is poured — and the next FAA reauthorization is not until 2028 [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/pfc] [Source: https://aaae.org/uscta-alerts/appropriations-leaders-unveil-final-fy26-dot/faa-and-dhs/tsa/cbp-spending-bills]. The resolution is not to abandon provisioning; it is to recognize that the durable substrate is a small fraction of the program's cost carrying the highest retrofit-cost asymmetry in the building. That makes it the *last* scope to cut when the budget tightens, not the first — the opposite of its usual fate.

**Two decisions sit above the architecture, and the design cannot solve either.** First, the financial one: $21.8 billion of new bonds on a 14-million-enplanement base, backstopped by a residual agreement with one 68 percent carrier, would recreate something close to the 2013 cost basis — CPE peaked at $26.47 that year [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. The last time IAD sat near this cost basis, United weighed leaving, and what saved the hub was Newark's physical constraint, not anything MWAA built or priced [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/united-going-kill-washington-dulles-hub/]. If United will not sign a use-and-lease term extending materially beyond the current 2039 horizon and co-underwrite the connecting-hub scope through majority-in-interest, the program is building hub-scale infrastructure on a hub commitment the anchor tenant has declined to guarantee. The mitigation is contractual and phased, not architectural: condition the connecting-hub scope on a matched-duration carrier commitment, and stress-test bond coverage against a 30 percent United pull-down.

Second, the political one: this program is federally sponsored and federally timed. DOT holds the title, extended the lease to 2100, and launched the RFI that produced the $22 billion figure and the 2034 date [Source: https://www.transportation.gov/briefing-room/trumps-transportation-secretary-sean-p-duffy-launches-new-initiative-revitalize]. The sponsor's mandate ends with the administration; a 2034 ribbon-cutting must survive a 2029 transition. Design the durable moves — the geometry, the provisioning — to be defensible even if the federal push evaporates and MWAA is left carrying the debt at a CPE the airlines can contest through 2039. The perimeter rule that built Dulles's long-haul franchise took 58 years to move and then moved against MWAA over the unanimous objection of both home-state delegations [Source: https://thehill.com/homenews/4655051-senate-passes-faa-reauthorization-after-dca-slots-fight/]. Plan the durable form around the regulation. Hedge the politics. Never plan around a favorable act of Congress.

Saarinen timed the passengers with a stopwatch and built them a way to skip the walk entirely. He was wrong about the sequence and right about the shell — the concept lasted twelve years, the roof is protected at sixty-four. The team now drawing the terminal that replaces his lounges faces the identical asymmetry, and it has the rare advantage of being able to read the answer in its own concrete. The question in the program meeting is not what the terminal will look like in 2050. It is which lines on the drawing the airport will still be able to move when the sequence changes again — and it will change again — and which ones it is about to pour into the ground for fifty years. Draw that boundary first. Everything else is fit-out.
