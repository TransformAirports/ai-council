# The Stopwatch and the Shell: What Actually Survives to 2050 at Dulles

*A Transform Airports AI Council report on the $22 billion Dulles rebuild*

---

In 1959, before he drew a single roofline, Eero Saarinen stood in airports with a stopwatch. He was not timing construction crews. He was timing passengers — how long they walked, where they bunched, how far the gate sat from the curb. His first question about Dulles, by his own account, was not *what will it look like* but *how do we solve the passengers walking for miles* [Source: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/eero-saarinens-revolutionary-design-dulles-airport/7853/]. His answer was the most radical de-sequencing bet in the history of American aviation: nineteen custom Chrysler-Budd mobile lounges that carried travelers directly from a single check-in hall to aircraft parked as far as a mile out on the apron, eliminating fixed concourses entirely [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport]. No gate holds. No pier walk. No sequence. The passenger boarded a lounge and the lounge became the gate.

The concept was dead inside a decade. Hub-and-spoke throughput economics — banks of flights arriving and departing in tight waves, connecting passengers who needed to move plane-to-plane fast — made a fleet of slow rolling rooms unworkable. The $22 billion plan MWAA is now advancing finishes the burial: it extends the AeroTrain to the concourses and eliminates the mobile lounges from regular operation entirely [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. And yet the thing Saarinen actually built — the soaring catenary-roofed headhouse — is a National Register structure at sixty-four years old, and the new program keeps it and extends it three hundred feet in each direction [Source: https://byerussell.substack.com/p/a-22-billion-program-for-dulles].

That is the whole argument, written into the airport's own concrete. The *processing concept* died in twelve years. The *shell* is protected at sixty-four. Dulles has already run the experiment the run prompt is asking about, and the result is not ambiguous.

**Our thesis is narrower and harder than the one we were handed.** The danger in a terminal designed today is not that its planners will fail to forecast biometrics or eVTOL or off-airport check-in. The danger is that they will pour fifty-year concrete around *any* passenger-processing sequence — the centralized one they know, or the distributed one they are tempted to bet on — and thereby convert a five-to-fifteen-year decision into a fifty-year liability. The move that survives to 2050 is not a prediction. It is a refusal to predict: lock the durable geometry hard and oversized, keep the processing layer deliberately soft and richly provisioned, and treat every sequence commitment as reversible. Dulles's own history is the best evidence for that discipline, because Dulles already paid — twice — to rip out a processing model it had hard-wired into the building.

## Executive summary

1. **Dulles is spending its cheapest asset to buy the most expensive kind of certainty.** The airport runs a cost-per-enplanement of $9.56, cheaper for airlines than all but a handful of US large hubs; the $22 billion program — $21.8 billion in new bonds — proposes to issue roughly four to five times the airport's entire existing $3.7 billion debt stack against that advantage [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad] [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Any future-proofing move that permanently raises CPE is not future-proofing. It is spending the airport's core commercial asset.

2. **The $22 billion figure is a political anchor, not an engineering estimate.** Qualified bidders priced this exact scope at $14.4 billion (Ferrovial) and at $35–50 billion (Phoenix/Ironbridge) — a spread of more than $35 billion on the same building [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. When professionals disagree by a factor of three, the headline should be read at ±50 percent, and the program's own materials concede it excludes "any potential delays or cost overruns" [Source: https://byerussell.substack.com/p/a-22-billion-program-for-dulles].

3. **The mega-terminal overrun record is the base rate, and it always breaks at the same place: the novel integrated system on the critical path, never the poured structure.** Berlin Brandenburg opened nine years late at 2.3–2.6x budget on a bespoke smoke-extraction system; Denver's automated baggage ran ~$560 million over and was decommissioned in 2005; the LAX people mover is +76 percent and three years late; Heathrow T5 was on time and on budget and still lost ~42,000 bags on opening day [Source: https://www.euronews.com/2020/10/31/berlin-airport-opens-10-years-late-and-three-times-over-budget] [Source: https://www.wrike.com/blog/lessons-learned-from-project-failure-at-denver-international-airport-why-checking-bags-is-still-a-pain/] [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report] [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Terminal_5]. Kansas City delivered forty gates for $1.5 billion on time and on budget by keeping novel systems off the critical path [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date].

4. **For a decade, coordination has beaten concrete, and by wide margins.** FAA wake-turbulence recategorization gave Memphis a ~20 percent capacity gain — up to 22 more arrivals an hour — with no new pavement; airport collaborative decision-making cut European taxi-out times 10–20 percent with a data protocol, not a taxiway; and legacy hubs work gates 3–6 times a day where Southwest works one 10.3 times at BWI [Source: https://www.mobilityengineeringtech.com/component/content/article/25559-recat-wake-turbulence-recategorization] [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making] [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. The building is rarely the binding constraint until the coordination levers are exhausted, and most airports have not touched them.

5. **The durable commitments are geometric; the volatile ones are procedural — and the split is knowable now.** Walking distance, apron and taxilane clearance, baggage-system topology, and curb geometry are fifty-year physics that operations can never fix after the pour; checkpoint lane count, gate assignment, turn discipline, check-in fit-out, and every operational-intelligence product are five-to-fifteen-year layers that software and rule changes keep improving [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/] [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-03-12/pickups-at-kansas-citys-new-airport-terminal-are-a-nightmare-heres-how-officials-say-to-fix-it].

6. **Every operational-intelligence system Dulles could buy has a useful life of five to fifteen years; the durable investment is the substrate underneath them.** A-CDM, predictive turnaround, digital twins, biometric flow analytics — all get re-platformed on that cadence. Schiphol's operational intelligence rides on 80,000-plus sensors; the sensors, conduit, sightlines, power density, and a clean open data architecture are the built-form decision, and retrofitting them into a finished mega-terminal is the expense you cannot undo [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/twin].

7. **The genuinely open design questions — distributed screening, off-airport processing — are federal control questions with no schedule, and the last time federal politics moved on Dulles, it moved against MWAA.** Distributed screening is one PreCheck-only DHS prototype at Las Vegas with no announced deployment timeline; the 58-year-old DCA perimeter rule that built Dulles's long-haul franchise was eroded in 2024 over the unanimous objection of both home-state Senate delegations [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid] [Source: https://thehill.com/homenews/4655051-senate-passes-faa-reauthorization-after-dca-slots-fight/]. Provision for these futures; do not draw the building around them.

8. **The building cannot buy back United's network math.** United carries roughly 68 percent of Dulles traffic; Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Cleveland each built or paid for hub-scale infrastructure to a carrier's specification and were left with the debt when the carrier's math turned [Source: https://simpleflying.com/where-united-airlines-washington-dulles-passengers-go/] [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport]. Size the connecting-hub scope soft and convertible, and condition it on a matched-duration carrier commitment, because no terminal design solves a dehubbing.

## The argument

### Start with the one number that disciplines every other decision

Before the design charrette, before the renderings, there is a single figure that should sit at the head of the table: $9.56. That is what it costs an airline, per enplaned passenger, to operate at Dulles in FY2025 — down from $12.88 the year before as debt amortized and traffic recovered [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. Across US large hubs, cost-per-enplanement runs from $3.94 at Atlanta to $30.16 at LAX and $29.56 at Chicago O'Hare [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. Dulles sits near the cheap end. That is not an accident of accounting. It is the airport's defining commercial asset — the reason it remains, in United's own capital math, worth flying.

Now set the program against it. The $22 billion plan proposes $21.8 billion in new bonds plus $1.1 billion in fees [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. MWAA today carries roughly $4.7–4.9 billion of principal across its aviation enterprise [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. The program therefore proposes to issue four to five times the airport's entire existing debt — at a hub whose whole advantage is that it is cheap. Under MWAA's residual rate structure, with "no contractual cap on the cost level that can be recovered through the rate formula," incremental debt service passes mechanically to the airlines [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Every dollar of the program either protects the CPE advantage or spends it.

This is the frame that turns "future-proofing" from a design aesthetic into a capital-allocation problem. The question is not *how do we make the building impressive in 2050*. It is *which dollars buy something that survives, and which buy something that will be obsolete before the bonds are half-retired*.

### The building itself already answered the question, in 1962 and again in 1972

The temptation in any future-proofing exercise is to treat the future as unprecedented. Dulles cannot afford that flattery, because it has run this experiment before and the tape is still readable.

Saarinen's mobile-lounge terminal was a distributed, de-sequenced processing model — exactly the direction the run's thesis says the industry is now heading. It died not because Saarinen forecast badly but because the throughput logic underneath it changed: hub banking rewarded fixed concourses and fast plane-to-plane connection, and a fleet of rolling rooms could not keep up [Source: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/eero-saarinens-revolutionary-design-dulles-airport/7853/]. The *concept* was obsolete in roughly ten years. The *shell* endured for sixty-four and counting.

Kansas City tells the same story on a different clock. Its 1972 three-terminal cloverleaf let passengers drive right up to their gate — a design, as one account put it, "before security and before the hub-and-spoke system" [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2023/03/02/kansas-citys-new-terminal-opens-trading-gate-proximity-for-making-everything-else-better/]. Then, on a single morning in September 2001, the processing sequence the whole building was organized around became illegal. Federalized screening under ATSA forced consolidated, sterile-side checkpoints at 429 airports [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_and_Transportation_Security_Act]. The thin circular geometry that made drive-to-gate elegant made post-9/11 screening nearly unworkable, and it took twenty-two years and a $1.5 billion single terminal to undo [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date].

Two buildings, two eras, one lesson. When a terminal is organized around a processing sequence, it is the *sequence* that dates — the check-in hall depth, the checkpoint location, the drive-to-gate access — while the structural bones outlast it by decades. Berlin Brandenburg dated at its counter-based check-in hall; Kansas City dated at its distributed checkpoints; Dulles dated at its lounges. None of them dated at the span or the column grid.

### The failure taxonomy points at the same culprit every time

If you catalog the mega-terminals that blew up on cost or schedule, a pattern emerges that is almost monotonous in its consistency. The thing that dates fastest, overruns worst, and takes the whole opening down with it is the novel, integrated, single-vendor system on the critical path — never the poured concrete.

Berlin Brandenburg is the canonical case: nine years late, a budget that went from roughly €2.8 billion to €6.5 billion and beyond, felled by a bespoke smoke-extraction system "not built according to the construction permit" and signed off by a man qualified as an engineering draughtsman rather than an engineer [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport]. Denver's automated baggage system grew from a $193 million contract past $400 million, opened sixteen months late, never ran at scale, and was fully decommissioned by 2005 in favor of a manual system [Source: https://sebokwiki.org/wiki/Denver_Airport_Baggage_Handling_System]. The LAX people mover went from a $1.9 billion construction budget to $3.34 billion — up 76 percent — on a first-of-kind public-private dispute structure, not on any problem with pouring concrete [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report].

The most instructive case is the one that got the construction *right*. Heathrow Terminal 5 came in on time and on its £4.3 billion budget because the owner held all the risk in a single integrated team — and still lost more than 42,000 bags and cancelled over 500 flights in the first ten days, because a test software filter was never removed and operational readiness was under-rehearsed [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Terminal_5] [Source: https://cdn2.f-cdn.com/files/download/1815034/eLHMsHeathrow%20Fiasco.pdf]. The concrete was flawless. The systems layer failed on day one — and Heathrow's baggage system was still failing in 2026, long after the structure was signed off [Source: https://spectrum.ieee.org/baggage-problem-hits-heathrow-terminal-5-].

Now stand Kansas City next to all of them. Forty gates, roughly a million square feet, $1.5 billion, on time and on budget, opened February 2023 [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date]. The distinguishing variable was not square footage or ambition. It was scope discipline: keep the novel integrated systems off the critical path and let the structure be structure.

For a capital planner, this taxonomy is not a horror-story recitation. It is a spending instruction. The systems that dated and detonated everywhere — automated baggage, bespoke life-safety, first-of-kind people movers, integrated processing software — are precisely the *volatile* layer. The structure that quietly outlasted them is the *durable* layer. So overbuild the durable, unretrofittable layer — structural bays, apron geometry, power and comms conduit — and keep the security regime, the sensing, and the fit-out deliberately soft, because those are the exact systems that dated fastest everywhere else. That is not a hedge against forecasting error. It is the economically rational response to it.

### Coordination has been beating concrete for a decade, and Dulles has not exhausted it

There is a quieter body of evidence that should unsettle anyone about to triple a gate count. Over the last decade, the largest measured capacity gains in US and European aviation came not from new pavement but from new rules and better information-sharing.

The cleanest natural experiments are the ones where nothing physical changed. FAA wake-turbulence recategorization reclassified aircraft separation by wing design and approach speed rather than weight alone, letting planes fly closer together. At Memphis this produced roughly a 20 percent capacity boost and up to 22 additional arrivals per hour — no runway, no taxiway, a rule [Source: https://www.mobilityengineeringtech.com/component/content/article/25559-recat-wake-turbulence-recategorization]. Airport collaborative decision-making tells the same story on the surface: EUROCONTROL credits A-CDM with cutting taxi-out time 10–20 percent across European airports, the product of a shared, accurate off-block time rather than any concrete [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making]. Its independent 17-airport assessment found taxi-out reduced by 0.25–3 minutes per departure and the standard deviation of take-off times roughly halved, from about 14 minutes to 7 [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/airport-collaborative-decision-making-cdm-impact-assessment].

The gate data is more pointed still. "Full" gate utilization is conventionally 6–8 turns per gate per day. Delta's hubs ran 2.9 (Memphis) to 6.3 (Atlanta) daily turns; Southwest's focus cities ran 7.2 (Phoenix) to 10.3 (Baltimore) [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. The spread is operational strategy, not building capability. Dulles today has 139 gates serving 29 million passengers, against a master-plan ambition toward 90 million [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport] [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2025/07/17/new-master-plan-for-dulles-airport-envisions-growth-to-90-million-annual-passengers/]. The instinct is to multiply gates. The utilization data says look harder first: a hub working its gates 3–6 times a day is leaving a large multiple of throughput on the table, much of it recoverable through common-use gating, tighter turn discipline, and the coordination levers above.

And gate count, properly understood, is a function of turn *variance*, not turn *time*. A full A320 turn runs 35–45 minutes, but even top-quartile short-haul carriers average 8 minutes of ground delay per turn, with observed variance near 19 percent [Source: https://simpleflying.com/narrowbody-turnaround-times-time-differences-guide/]. Planners size gates for the bad day, not the average one. Every minute of variance you fail to squeeze out is a fraction of a gate you must build as pure buffer. Buy down the variance with coordination and you buy down the gate count — and the building permanently stops paying for the ramp's worst afternoons.

None of this argues that Dulles needs no new concrete. It argues *sequence*: pull the coordination levers first, so you build the right, smaller amount of the expensive thing. A $22 billion program sized to today's uncoordinated peak is funding coordination debt in structural steel.

### What operations can never fix is where the money must go

The coordination argument has a hard boundary, and honesty requires naming it, because it is exactly where Dulles must not skimp. Some constraints are physics, and when they are wrong no software rescues them.

Walking distance is physics. Istanbul's terminal imposes 20–30 minute walks to its farthest gates; Beijing Daxing's radial starfish caps the farthest gate at under eight minutes and 600 meters [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/] [Source: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/zaha-hadid-daxing-international-airport-beijing-09-26-2019/]. No scheduling app closes that gap; concourse length and vertical-circulation placement are locked on day one. Baggage topology is physics: once the conveyor and sortation geometry are poured, you live with them for decades, and under-provisioning them is an error operations cannot undo. The curb is physics too — Kansas City's celebrated new terminal traded gate proximity for a better building and then watched its pickup roadway back up to the highway because curb and cell-lot capacity were mismatched to demand [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-03-12/pickups-at-kansas-citys-new-airport-terminal-are-a-nightmare-heres-how-officials-say-to-fix-it].

These are the commitments to overbuild now, because retrofit is either impossible or ruinous. They are also, tellingly, the least glamorous line items — apron clearances, baggage halls, roadway geometry — the first things sacrificed to fund a signature interior. That inversion is the recurring failure mode, and it is exactly backwards.

### Provision the substrate; never marry the product

The technology layer sharpens the same point to a fine edge. Every operational-intelligence system Dulles might buy — A-CDM, predictive turnaround, the integrated ops center, the digital twin, biometric flow analytics — has a useful life of five to fifteen years and gets re-platformed on that cadence. None of it belongs to the fifty-year spine.

The honest read on this category is that its independently measured returns are small-but-compounding and its large returns are all modeled. A-CDM's audited benefit is minutes per departure; the widely quoted "$900 million per airline, $300–500 million per hub by 2035" perfect-turn figure is a vendor's own projection, authored by its CEO with no independent auditor named [Source: https://www.assaia.com/resources/ai-optimised-aircraft-turnarounds-are-unlocking-billions-in-long-term-value-for-airports-and-airlines]. Digital twins have a wide deployment gap: only DFW operates an established US large-hub twin, and 17 of 31 large hubs remain in pilot or planning [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/twin]. Buying a twin in 2026 is buying a multi-year program, not a product. And the dominant failure mode across the category is organizational — more than half of analytics pilots are abandoned after proof-of-concept, and deployments without executive sponsorship are three times likelier to stall [Source: https://intuitionlabs.ai/pdfs/enterprise-ai-rollout-failures-causes-and-case-studies.pdf].

The design implication is clean. Do not organize the terminal around any specific product; every one of them will be replaced twice before the building is fifty. Do organize it to be instrumentable to the teeth: dense conduit and power at the apron and in the terminal, camera and sensor sightlines with dedicated data paths, an apron laid out for computer vision, generous comms risers, and — the one genuinely durable software decision — a clean, open operational-data architecture any future vendor can plug into. Schiphol's operational intelligence runs on more than 80,000 sensors across its campus; that instrumentation layer is a built-form decision, not a software one [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/twin]. Retrofitting sightlines and conduit into a finished mega-terminal is the expense you cannot undo. The software you can always re-buy.

### The synthesis: refuse to hard-wire the sequence

Put the lenses together and they converge, from six different starting points, on a single discipline. The economist says overbuild the conduit and the column grid, keep the security regime soft. The engineer says the durable layer's electrical, structural, and spatial reserve is the spend you cannot recover later at any price. The operations analyst says spend on the geometry operations can never fix and stay cheap on everything a rule change keeps improving. The technology scout says provision the substrate and re-buy the software. The historian says lock the fifty-year bones and treat every processing commitment as reversible, because Dulles already paid twice to rip out a hard-wired one.

That is the thesis in its defensible form. Not *the terminal will open dated because processing is decentralizing* — that is a forecast, and forecasts are how you strand a building. The defensible claim is that pouring fifty-year concrete around any processing sequence, today's or tomorrow's, converts a fast-moving decision into a slow-moving liability. The move that survives is to build the durable geometry hard and oversized and leave the volatile layer genuinely, cheaply soft.

## The counter-case, honestly presented

There is a serious argument that the thesis, even in this disciplined form, buys expensive optionality against changes that are unproven, slow, and — where they have been available for decades — chronically failing to scale. A planner who believed it would build the robust conventional thing superbly and pocket the difference. The case deserves its strongest statement.

**Start with screening, because the thesis leans on it hardest, and the trend runs the other way.** Screening distributes only if you are willing to multiply the scarcest, most regulated resource in the building: certified officers. Every credible operator move of the last five years has run toward consolidation to maximize officer productivity, not away from it. Kansas City's new terminal centralized its checkpoint specifically "to handle larger crowds," consolidating standard, PreCheck, ADA, and Touchless ID lines into one location [Source: https://www.kctv5.com/2026/04/10/kci-centralizes-security-checkpoint-handle-larger-crowds/]. TSA's own 2025 solicitation seeks to "reduce total operating costs, workforce requirements, and manual labor" — fewer officers per passenger, which distributed gate-level screening structurally opposes [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2025/07/25/tsa-seeks-private-sector-solutions-enhance-airport-security-and]. The checkpoint persists because labor is the binding constraint and consolidation is how you economize on it.

The regulatory brief hardens this from a trend into a control question. Distributed screening is not a design choice MWAA gets to make. Under 49 U.S.C. §44901, TSA controls the checkpoint, and self-service screening exists as exactly one PreCheck-only DHS prototype at Harry Reid in Las Vegas, with TSA stating it will study results before announcing any deployment timeline [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]. Betting built form on decentralized screening by 2035 is betting on an unscheduled federal rulemaking. And CT scanners — the actual near-term certainty — are larger, heavier, and more power-hungry than the units they replace, pushing the *centralized* checkpoint to grow before anything distributes [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/].

**Off-airport processing is a thirty-year-old idea that never became the dominant mode.** Hong Kong's Airport Express in-town check-in and Vienna's City Air Terminal have offered curb-to-city baggage and check-in since the 1990s; both remain optional, airline-dependent, rail-dependent, and partial, with some facilities suspended [Source: https://www.hongkongairport.com/en/baggage/airport-express-check-in.page] [Source: https://viennaairport.com/en/passengers/arrival__departure/city_check-in]. Three decades of availability without a tipping point is the single strongest data point against the claim that processing is about to migrate off-site inside a 10–25 year window.

**The failure taxonomy the thesis leans on may be a category error.** BER, Heathrow T5, and Denver did not fail because they froze a processing sequence. They failed on governance, fire-suppression engineering, baggage integration, truncated testing, and scope creep. Attributing execution disasters to "built form sized for the wrong decade" launders a management story into an architecture story. The lesson those projects actually teach is *build the conventional thing competently* — and the proof is LaGuardia Terminal B, a headhouse-plus-concourse typology with fully centralized processing, delivered on time and on budget within an $8 billion program and named the best new airport terminal in the world [Source: https://dbia.org/project/a-whole-new-laguardia-terminal-b-redevelopment/]. The most-praised recent US rebuild bet hard on the current typology and executed it superbly.

**Flexibility, meanwhile, is an option you pay for up front and usually never exercise.** The real-options terminal-design literature's own headline case — Zurich — found the flexible alternative worth "approximately 5% higher than the best conventional phased plan," and that is the optimized result under favorable assumptions, not the median [Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23249935.2023.2287502]. Soft space, flexible column grids, and over-provisioned services carry real capital and floor-area cost today against a payoff that arrives only if a specific future materializes on schedule. Most soft space gets leased, walled, and forgotten. A $22 billion program funded by $21.8 billion in new bonds must survive rating agencies, an airline use-and-lease negotiation, and a board — and "we left it soft in case screening decentralizes" is the first line item cut when the budget tightens and the first thing blamed if the terminal underperforms.

**And there is a decisive geometry bet that aged beautifully by refusing to stay soft.** Beijing Daxing committed hard to a radial starfish sized so the farthest gate sits about eight minutes from the center — an irreversible structural decision now praised for minimizing walking across the world's largest single-building terminal [Source: https://www.businesstraveller.com/features/phoenix-rising-beijings-new-daxing-international-airport/]. Commitment, not softness, produced the durable win.

**The airline lens supplies the sharpest counter of all: the real risk at Dulles is not a dated design; it is a departed carrier.** United carries roughly 68 percent of Dulles traffic, and it has stood at the edge of dehubbing IAD once already — stopped not by anything MWAA built but by the fact that Newark could not physically absorb the traffic [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/united-going-kill-washington-dulles-hub/]. Pittsburgh built a ~$1 billion midfield terminal to US Airways' specification and was dehubbed in 2004 at a cost-per-enplanement of just $6 — proof that a purpose-built, carrier-requested, *cheap* terminal cannot hold a hub whose route economics have failed [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport]. Cincinnati fell from 22.7 million passengers in 2005 to under 6 million by 2013 and demolished a concourse [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966692315001970]. Against risks of that magnitude, arguing about checkpoint softness is rearranging deck chairs. Build the robust, financeable, legible thing — runways, aprons, structural bays, a working AeroTrain — and stop hedging bets the security economics and thirty years of non-adoption say will not pay.

## Why the counter-case is insufficient

The counter-case is not wrong. It is, in several places, more right than the run's original thesis, and the honest move is to concede those places by name before explaining why the thesis still holds.

**Concede screening entirely.** Do not distribute the checkpoint. The labor economics, the TSA solicitations, and the single-prototype status of self-service screening all point one direction, and it is consolidation. The historian's reading is decisive here: the screening regime has ratcheted toward centralization for a quarter-century and has never once reversed [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_and_Transportation_Security_Act]. A strategist who recommends drawing the building around distributed gate-level screening is recommending a bet against the clearest regulatory grain in the industry. So we do not recommend it. But note the second half of the operations brief's finding, which the counter-case skips: do not *oversize* the central checkpoint either. CT throughput gains and self-service divestiture are both raising passengers-per-square-foot, so the hall the 2026 team draws risks being too big by 2040 even as it stays central [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/]. The instruction is not "distribute." It is "lock the checkpoint's location and secure-boundary logic; leave its lane count and machine mix soft." That is the thesis, not the counter-case.

**Concede off-airport processing as a design driver.** Thirty years without a tipping point is real evidence, and no responsible plan organizes a building around curb-to-city check-in arriving by 2035. But the concession has a floor. Even the counter-case's own author grants that "it never scaled in 30 years" can be a phase-change setup rather than a verdict — off-airport check-in stayed niche partly for want of the rail and identity infrastructure that is now arriving, and Dulles will have Metro rail that Hong Kong and Vienna's models always required [Source: https://viennaairport.com/en/passengers/arrival__departure/city_check-in]. Induction breaks at inflection points. The correct treatment is neither to build for it nor to foreclose it: keep it an unfunded option, provisioned through the same soft check-in halls and clean data architecture the thesis already recommends for other reasons.

**Concede that the mega-terminal failures were execution failures.** BER's smoke system, Denver's baggage, T5's software filter — every one traces to governance, testing, and bespoke systems, not to a frozen processing paradigm. The counter-case is correct that these are management stories. But it draws the wrong conclusion. The thesis never claimed those buildings failed *because* they hard-wired a sequence. It claims something the failure record actively confirms: the fastest-dating, most-detonating layer is always the novel integrated system, and the durable structure quietly outlives it. That is the *same* observation the counter-case makes when it praises Kansas City's scope discipline and LaGuardia's clean execution. Both sides agree the concrete is not the problem. The disagreement is only about whether you should therefore leave the volatile layer soft — and here the counter-case contradicts itself, because the discipline it admires (keep novel systems off the critical path, build conventional structure superbly) *is* the durable/volatile split under a different name.

**Concede that flexibility's financial payoff is modest — and then reject the framing.** The 5 percent Zurich figure is real, and any recommendation resting on financial option value alone would be weak [Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23249935.2023.2287502]. But the thesis does not rest there, and the counter-case's strongest concession is the one that dismantles its own argument. There are two different things called "flexibility," and they must be separated. *Program and fit-out flexibility* — movable walls, soft space held vacant — is the 5 percent bet, and it often does get leased and forgotten. *Deep-service provisioning* — conduit, power density, riser capacity, floor loading, structural spare, floor-to-floor height — is a different animal entirely. It is cheap to install in new slab and structure and ruinously expensive to retrofit into an occupied terminal. Even the contrarian concedes this outright: overbuilding power and riser capacity for electrified ground equipment and unknown IT load "is not speculation — it is the cheapest insurance in the building." The chief engineer makes it concrete: electrified ground-support equipment is a substation-and-conduit decision in the durable layer, and Sea-Tac already installed 576 electric charging locations across its concourses [Source: https://xantrex.com/about-xantrex/blog/work-truck/evaluating-gse-electrification-strategies-choosing-the-right-approach-for-airports/]. Apron-edge power and transformer-vault space are cheap to pour now and brutal to add to a finished, operating apron. The thesis's provisioning claim is not an options-pricing argument. It is an asymmetry-of-retrofit-cost argument, and on that ground even the opposition surrenders.

**Concede Daxing — and read it correctly.** Yes, a hard geometry bet aged beautifully. But look at *what* Daxing committed to: apron geometry, walking distance, radial circulation — the fifty-year physical bones. It did not hard-wire a check-in typology or a screening regime into that starfish. Daxing is not evidence against the thesis; it is the thesis executed. Commit hard to the durable geometry, stay soft on the processing layer. That is precisely the split.

**Concede the dehubbing risk is larger than the design risk — and watch it reinforce the thesis rather than defeat it.** This is the counter-case's most important point and its biggest strategic error. The airline strategist is right that no terminal design solves a United departure, that the building cannot buy back network math, and that Pittsburgh and Cincinnati are the relevant precedents [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport]. But ask what the *building* should do given that risk. If the connecting-hub complex is sized to today's peak in *fixed, single-tenant geometry* — a 33-gate regional concourse hard-built to United's specification — then a dehubbing strands structure, exactly as it stranded Pittsburgh's Concourse E and Cincinnati's demolished Concourse C. If instead that same complex is built to *convert to common use* — gates that can be shed or re-tenanted without moving columns, apron clearances that swing between one widebody and two narrowbodies via MARS stands — then a dehubbing costs the airport traffic but not stranded concrete [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. The dehubbing risk is the strongest possible argument *for* soft, convertible processing geometry. The counter-case names the risk correctly and then recommends the response — "build the robust committed thing" — that maximizes the damage when the risk lands.

So the concessions are real and they are large. Do not distribute screening. Do not oversize the central hall. Do not organize the building around off-airport processing. Build the conventional typology and execute it superbly. Every one of those is now in the recommendation. What survives all of them is the core: the durable geometry gets locked hard and oversized, the processing sequence stays soft and convertible, the substrate gets over-provisioned because retrofit is unaffordable, and single-tenant scope gets built to convert. The counter-case does not defeat that. Correctly read, four of its five strongest points argue *for* it.

## Implications for MWAA and the Dulles program

This is not a generic terminal. It is a specific program with named packages, a named anchor tenant, a named federal sponsor, and a compressed schedule, and the framework has to survive contact with all of them.

**The phasing is the risk, and it is already visible in the schedule.** The program compresses a roughly two-decade master plan into eight years, 2026–2034, with three major packages running concurrently around a live United hub: a new concourse plus Concourse A renovation (~$6.2 billion, construction from about April 2027), demolition of Concourse C/D and a new 33-gate Concourse B for United regional flying (~$2.26 billion, from about January 2028), and a $3.75 billion AeroTrain extension (from about January 2028, targeted December 2033) [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/]. Concurrency at one airport is multiplicative, not additive; every interface between two simultaneous programs is a claim waiting to happen. And eliminating the mobile lounges *requires* the AeroTrain extension complete before the lounge fleet can retire — which means the passenger-access system has zero schedule slack. If the tunnel slips, the whole gate program's usability slips with it [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. The governance model matters more than the architecture: build the Heathrow T5 risk-holding structure and the LaGuardia build-new-before-you-demolish phasing in now, or pay for their absence in claims later.

**Design FOR now.** Lock the CBP-driven international geometry — one-way sterile corridors, mandatory separation of arriving passengers, the FIS baggage hall — because the Airport Technical Design Standards revise on a stable three-year cycle and are a genuine fifty-year durable constraint [Source: https://www.sskies.org/images/uploads/subpage/PARAS_0002.CBPATDSCompanionGuide.FinalReport.pdf]. Lock the central checkpoint's location and secure boundary. Lock wide column grids, generous floor-to-floor heights, apron and taxilane geometry, and the vertical-circulation and utility spines. This is the widebody international gateway function — anchored by United's immunized Atlantic joint venture and by DCA's perimeter and slot cage — which is the least-reversible part of United's commitment and the part with the longest half-life [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2024/10/21/uniteds-atlantic-joint-venture-guides-how-united-grows/].

**Accommodate LATER.** Build the geometry, leave the mode soft. MARS and swing-stand clearances on the apron so one position can serve a widebody or two narrowbodies as United's fleet mix shifts [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. Checkpoint structural bays, floor loading, and boundary flexibility for a checkpoint that can both grow with CT and later thin with self-service. Check-in halls convertible to dwell, lounge, and revenue space as counters vanish. Apron-edge power, transformer-vault space, and conduit density for electrified ground equipment. Generous comms risers and a clean, open operational-data architecture so the electronic layer can change cheaply for fifty years. Above all, build Concourse B's 33 United regional gates to *convert to common use* — the Pittsburgh risk in modern form is a single-tenant concourse that strands when the tenant's math turns, and MARS-capable, re-tenantable geometry is the hedge [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport].

**Bet AGAINST, explicitly.** Sizing the building for today's uncoordinated peak before the coordination levers are pulled. Distributed or off-airport screening as a built-form organizing principle — provision for it, do not pour concrete around it, because TSA has scheduled nothing [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]. eVTOL-at-scale apron geometry as a load-bearing assumption. Any single-vendor, first-of-a-kind life-safety or baggage system with no operational fallback — that is the BER and Denver failure mode, and it is avoidable. And a permanent, hard-walled row of check-in counters, which is the one piece of processing geometry we already know is dying.

**Two decisions sit above the architecture, and the design cannot solve either.** First, the financial one: $21.8 billion of new bonds on a 14.3-million-enplanement base, backstopped by a residual agreement with one 68 percent carrier, recreates something close to the 2013 cost structure — CPE peaked at $26.47 that year — that put IAD on United's dehub list in the first place [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. If United will not sign a use-and-lease term extending materially beyond the current 2039 horizon and co-underwrite the connecting-hub scope through majority-in-interest, the program is building hub-scale infrastructure on a hub commitment the anchor tenant has declined to guarantee. The mitigation is contractual and phased, not architectural: condition the connecting-hub scope on a matched-duration carrier commitment, and stress-test bond coverage against a 30 percent United pull-down.

Second, the political one: this program is federally sponsored and federally timed. DOT holds the title, extended the lease to 2100, and launched the RFI that produced the $22 billion figure and the 2034 date [Source: https://www.transportation.gov/briefing-room/trumps-transportation-secretary-sean-p-duffy-launches-new-initiative-revitalize]. The sponsor's mandate ends with the administration; a 2034 ribbon-cutting must survive a 2029 transition. Design the durable moves — the geometry, the provisioning — to be defensible even if the federal push evaporates and MWAA is left carrying the debt at a CPE the airlines can contest through 2039. The perimeter rule that built Dulles's long-haul franchise took 58 years to move and then moved against MWAA over the unanimous objection of both home-state delegations [Source: https://thehill.com/homenews/4655051-senate-passes-faa-reauthorization-after-dca-slots-fight/]. Plan the durable form around the regulation. Hedge the politics. Never plan around a favorable act of Congress.

Saarinen timed the passengers with a stopwatch and built them a way to skip the walk entirely. He was wrong about the sequence and right about the shell — the concept lasted twelve years, the roof is protected at sixty-four. The team now drawing the terminal that replaces his lounges faces the identical asymmetry, and it has the rare advantage of being able to read the answer in its own concrete. The question in the program meeting is not what the terminal will look like in 2050. It is which lines on the drawing the airport will still be able to move when the sequence changes again — and it will change again — and which ones it is about to pour into the ground for fifty years. Draw that boundary first. Everything else is fit-out.
