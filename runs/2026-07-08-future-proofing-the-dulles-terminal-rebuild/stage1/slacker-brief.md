# Slacker Brief — Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*The agent who didn't prepare, saying something useful anyway. Three parts, strict order. Part one is frozen once written.*

---

## 1. Gut thesis (frozen — written cold, before any research)

Here's what jumps out before I look anything up.

**What's right on its face:** The thesis has caught a real thing. Terminals are drawn during one decade's throughput assumptions and pour concrete that outlives those assumptions by 40 years. The "durable vs. volatile" split is the whole ballgame and the brief knows it. Structure, column grid, apron geometry, and vertical circulation are the bones; everything else is fit-out you'll rip out three times before 2050. That's correct and it's the useful frame.

**What's suspicious on its face:** The thesis is quietly in love with decentralization. "Screening distributes, gate-to-aircraft stops being 1:1, processing migrates onto the device." Maybe. But every one of those has been predicted for 20 years and centralized checkpoints keep *winning* — because TSA is a federal monopoly that standardizes on centralized lanes, because CBP wants chokepoints, and because distributed screening multiplies staffing cost. The de-sequencing story is a technology story told as if regulation and labor economics don't get a vote. They get the deciding vote.

**The joke** (which contains the critique): *Dulles is the last airport that should lecture anyone about future-proofing.* Saarinen built it in 1962 with mobile lounges — a bespoke bet on a de-sequenced, gate-less future where a people-mover drives you to a remote aircraft. It was visionary and it was *wrong*, and Dulles spent 50 years and billions retrofitting around those beautiful, useless mobile lounges and a terminal you couldn't easily add piers to. The AeroTrain finally fixed it in 2010. So when the brief says "design for de-sequencing," my gut screams: *that is literally the mistake Dulles already made once.* The most future-proof thing Saarinen could have done was build a boring, extensible pier terminal with a fat column grid and room to grow.

**Two or three cold reactions:**
1. The real durable asset isn't the building — it's the *land and the apron geometry and the ability to add/extend piers cheaply*. Optionality is spatial slack, not clever design.
2. "Overbuild provisioning now because retrofit is unaffordable later" is right for conduit/power/structural depth, and a trap for anything with a chip in it. The discipline is knowing which is which.
3. Everyone benchmarks IST/PKX/Changi. Those are state-capitalism megaprojects. Dulles is a US federal-regulatory, bond-financed, airline-use-agreement animal. The constraints that will date Dulles are American ones — TSA, CBP, MWAA's capital stack, United's hub economics — not architecture.

Frozen. Now to reality.

## 3. Revised position (resubmitted)

**What survived.** The durable asset is *spatial slack and extensibility*, not clever form. Two US airports — Dulles (mobile lounges) and Kansas City (drive-to-gate horseshoe) — built de-sequenced, decentralized terminals in the 1960s–70s and spent decades and billions undoing them. Both were beaten by boring, extensible pier terminals with room to add gates. That pattern is the single most useful thing I brought, and reality reinforced it. Also survived: bet *against* eVTOL-at-scale as a design driver (no US cert, vertiports unprofitable pre-2030), and overbuild the *dumb* provisioning — conduit, structural depth, and now emphatically **electrical service** (power demand ~5x by 2050) — while leaving anything with a chip in it soft.

**What I'm revising.** My most confident line — "centralized screening keeps winning; de-sequencing is a technology fantasy that regulation vetoes" — was too smug. TSA itself is prototyping distributed, self-service, checkpoint-reducing screening. But it's PreCheck-only and federally standardized, so the correct inference isn't "design a distributed layout now." It's: **build the checkpoint as a soft, column-free, over-provisioned volume that can shrink and redistribute — never as a load-bearing architectural chokepoint.** De-sequencing is a real vector; the hedge is spatial softness, not committing to the new geometry.

**What I flat-out got wrong.** I framed Dulles as a blank-slate rebuild betting on de-sequencing. Wrong twice over. The $22B program *keeps* Saarinen, completes by **2034 not 2050**, and adds *linear pier concourses* — i.e., it is already doing the boring, extensible thing my gut prescribed. So the live risk at Dulles is not that it romanticizes decentralization. It's the opposite: that it hard-codes **2034-era gate sizing, checkpoint geometry, and bag-system topology into a building it must still operate in 2070.**

**What I hadn't considered (D).** The sharpest *evidenced* risk is commercial, not procedural. Non-aero revenue per passenger is already falling ($8.61 → $7.57) and the decline is tied to dwell compression. Dulles is sizing terminal volume and landside/airside split around a revenue architecture that is structurally shrinking per head. That is precisely the thesis's "sized for the wrong decade" failure — but it lands on the **commercial axis**, and the council will under-cover it because everyone's eyes are on screening and gates. If the building assumes retail rents that per-passenger economics won't deliver, you've overbuilt landside concession volume that dates faster than any checkpoint. Point the Strategist here.

## 4. Three "wait, what about…" questions for the Strategist

1. **What if Dulles's real future-proofing failure is that it's *not radical enough*?** The program keeps the 1962 terminal and completes in 2034 — every durability judgment is anchored to a checkpoint-and-pier logic that TSA is itself trying to dissolve. Are we future-proofing a rebuild, or lovingly restoring the last decade's typology with better cladding?
2. **What about designing the checkpoint hall as *demolishable*, not permanent?** If distributed screening is a real 15-year vector, the durable move is a checkpoint volume that can be gutted without touching structure — soft floor, roof-only spans, no mechanical cores buried in it. Has anyone priced "screening as fit-out" versus "screening as building"?
3. **What about the concession-revenue cliff as a sizing input?** If dwell keeps compressing and per-pax spend keeps falling, is the landside/airside volume split in the $22B plan sized around a revenue number that won't materialize — and is anyone stress-testing terminal square footage against the *declining* ACI per-passenger curve rather than a flat one?

## 5. One sentence on what to ignore

Trust my architectural-history pattern (mobile lounges, MCI, de-sequencing bets get undone) and the commercial-revenue point — but discount my electrification numbers: I have one case-study pair (DEN/MSP) and a headline "5x by 2050," and the chief-engineer will have better load figures than my ten-minute grab.
