# Airline Commercial Strategist — Evidence Brief

## Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

**The lens:** A $22B terminal is a 50-year bet on a 5-year decision cycle. Dulles does not control its own throughput logic — United does. The run prompt asks which structural decisions "still hold" through 2050. My answer, from the commercial side of the table: none of them hold if United's network math turns, and the building cannot buy that math back. Every design move in this program should be stress-tested against one question — *what will United do in response?* That question is my veto.

---

## 1. Key Findings

1. **IAD is a single-carrier hub with all the fragility that implies.** United carried ~16.6M passengers to/from/via Dulles in the 12 months to June 2024 — roughly **68% of airport traffic**. Of United's IAD passengers, ~54% are local O&D and ~40% connect [Source: https://simpleflying.com/where-united-airlines-washington-dulles-passengers-go/]. A terminal sized for United's connecting bank is sized for a decision United re-underwrites every planning cycle.

2. **United has already stood at the edge of dehubbing IAD once.** Post-Continental merger, Jeff Smisek reportedly weighed dehubbing Dulles; what stopped him was not the airport — it was that **Newark (EWR) could not physically absorb the traffic** [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/united-going-kill-washington-dulles-hub/]. IAD's survival was a function of constraint at *another* United hub, not of anything MWAA built. That is the real moat, and it is not a building.

3. **The $22B program will reverse a decade of CPE discipline — and CPE is the variable that moves carrier behavior most predictably.** IAD CPE peaked at **$26.47 in 2013** (roughly double BWI and nearly double DCA), then fell to **$12.88 (FY2024) and ~$9.56–11.17 (FY2025)** as debt amortized and traffic recovered [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad] [Source: https://airlineweekly.skift.com/2021/07/united-airlines-may-finally-get-a-new-concourse-in-washington/]. A program funded by **$21.8B in new bonds** pushes CPE back toward and likely past that 2013 peak [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/].

4. **CPE is a necessary-but-not-sufficient trigger. Route economics dehub airports at *low* CPE too.** US Airways dehubbed Pittsburgh in 2004 at a CPE of just **$6** [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2012/07/17/across-the-aisle-from-pittsburgh-airports-executive-director-on-pittsburghs-rise-and-fall-as-a-hub-part-1/]. Cheap gates do not save a redundant hub. High CPE accelerates an exit that network redundancy already justifies.

5. **What actually anchors United at IAD is the Atlantic joint venture and DCA's perimeter/slot cage — not terminal quality.** United's A++ immunized JV (Lufthansa Group, Air Canada, now ITA) makes IAD a transatlantic and Africa/India gateway [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2024/10/21/uniteds-atlantic-joint-venture-guides-how-united-grows/]. DCA is perimeter- and slot-constrained; IAD is the region's only unconstrained long-haul field [Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/more-than-half-a-billion-dollar-expansion--modernization-coming-to-uniteds-washington-dulles-hub-302320375.html]. **Design the building around the widebody international gateway function, because that is the least-reversible part of United's commitment.**

6. **The residual/MII structure gives United a veto on capital but leaves MWAA holding the debt.** MWAA's Airline Use Agreement (Jan 2025–Dec 2039) is a residual-type deal with revenue-sharing (Transfers at DCA, a Plateau mechanism at IAD) [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Under residual + majority-in-interest, signatory airlines backstop the risk but also approve — or block — major capital, and the airport carries the bonds if traffic falls [Source: http://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/airport-capital-review].

7. **Every recent dehubbing shares one plot: the airport built the infrastructure the carrier requested, then the carrier's network math turned and the airport was left with the debt and the empty concourses.** CVG, PIT, STL, CLE, MEM. The building never saved any of them.

## 2. Evidence — Industry Structure and the Leverage It Creates

**The post-consolidation oligopoly.** The 2008–2016 merger wave (Delta/Northwest 2008, United/Continental 2010, Southwest/AirTran 2011, American/US Airways 2013, Alaska/Virgin America 2016) collapsed the mainline field to four carriers controlling ~80% of domestic capacity. Consolidation is what *created* redundant hubs — and redundant hubs are what get cut. United closed Cleveland precisely because, post-Continental, "every other hub was larger and served a specific role… the northern-Ohio city was redundant" [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2014/02/04/united-cuts-its-cleveland-hub-blames-connecting-passengers-and-the-faa/]. The strategist's takeaway: mergers convert a carrier's hub portfolio into a *ranked list*, and the airport at the bottom of the list is a capital-allocation casualty regardless of local demand.

**The ULCC segment is a different, weaker value proposition — and shrinking.** Spirit, Frontier, and Allegiant bring point-to-point local traffic, minimal gate commitment, and no willingness to sign long-dated residual agreements or backstop debt. The relevant IAD datapoint: DWU notes United is adding capacity "following Spirit Airlines' May 2026 shutdown" [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. The ULCC layer that a diversified airport might lean on as a hedge against hub concentration is contracting, not growing. IAD's hedge is thin.

**Hub-and-spoke economics.** A hub exists to manufacture connecting itineraries by banking flights into arrival/departure waves — trading passenger connection time and airport peaking for network breadth the local market alone cannot support. A "real hub" carries meaningful connecting traffic on top of local O&D; a "focus city" is mostly local point-to-point. IAD at 40% connecting is a genuine hub, but a *thinner* one than ATL or ORD — which is exactly why it was on Smisek's list. Thin connecting banks are the first to get de-banked when RJ economics deteriorate, because 50-seat regional feed is the most fuel- and cost-sensitive flying in the system.

**Cost-per-enplanement benchmarking.** Carriers benchmark CPE across their own network and divert *growth* (not necessarily existing service) away from high-CPE stations. Current large-hub CPE ranges: ATL ~$3.94–6.07, ORD ~$29.6, DFW ~$13.4–17.0, DEN ~$12.8, LAX ~$30–38, SFO ~$22.6–24.6 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. Note the spread: a carrier tolerates $30 at a slot-constrained gateway (LAX, ORD) it cannot replicate, but not at a substitutable connecting hub. **IAD's defensibility at high CPE depends entirely on being non-substitutable — which is the JV-gateway argument, not the connecting-hub argument.** As DWU puts it, "a CPE spike during a capital program peak followed by a decline as debt amortizes signals a different credit profile than persistently high CPE" [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. The 2013→2024 IAD curve is exactly that pattern; the $22B program restarts the spike.

**Use agreements and where leverage actually sits.** Residual agreements shift financial risk to signatory airlines in exchange for preferential rates; compensatory agreements leave the airport bearing revenue risk but with more autonomy [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-rate-methodologies]. The majority-in-interest clause is the operative constraint: "Airlines holding a majority of landed weight or enplanements must approve capital projects exceeding specified thresholds… MII provisions are negotiating leverage for airlines and constraints on airport management flexibility" [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-airport-relationships]. At a 68%-United airport, MII effectively means United co-signs the terminal — and can withhold its signature to reshape scope, phasing, or gate allocation. The $22B program's fate runs through United's capital committee as much as MWAA's board.

## 3. Case Studies — Carrier Responses to Airport Decisions

**Pittsburgh (US Airways, dehubbed Nov 2004) — the cautionary twin.** PIT built a midfield terminal at US Airways' request; the lease was signed 1987, it opened 1992. By 2003 US Airways ran a **~$40M/year loss** operating the hub while paying **~80% of the airport's $673M terminal debt** [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport]. When it dehubbed, CPE was only **$6** — proof that a *purpose-built, carrier-requested* terminal at *low cost* still could not hold a hub whose route economics had failed. Concourse E and part of A went dark. PIT spent the next two decades and $1.5B building a *smaller* terminal to fit the airport it actually had. **This is the single most relevant precedent for a $22B IAD program: infrastructure sized to a carrier's request, stranded by the carrier's network decision.**

**Cincinnati (Delta/Northwest).** Peak 2005: ~600 daily flights, ~150 destinations, 22.7M passengers, Delta's second-largest hub. By 2013: <180 departures, 77 destinations, <6M passengers [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966692315001970]. The mechanism: the Delta/Northwest merger (2008) made CVG redundant against ATL/DTW, and CVG's notoriously high fares (a symptom of monopoly hub pricing) hollowed the local base. Delta cut into 2010. Carrier framing was "network optimization"; the route economics said "redundant connecting complex with a small catchment."

**St. Louis (TWA→American).** American acquired TWA in 2001, initially *said* it would keep STL as a hub to relieve ORD congestion, then replaced mainline with RJs (from ~800 daily ops to ~200) and formally killed the hub in 2009, consolidating on ORD/DFW [Source: https://simpleflying.com/what-happened-american-airlines-st-louis-hub/]. **Named conflict:** American's public "we'll keep it" survived exactly as long as the route economics did. When O&D softened post-9/11, the stated commitment evaporated. Carrier statements are options, not obligations.

**Cleveland (Continental→United, 2014).** United announced closure Feb 1, 2014, saying the hub "hasn't been profitable for over a decade," and cut daily departures **>60%** by June, closing Concourse D [Source: https://simpleflying.com/united-airlines-cleveland-hub/]. Smisek's own frame was "shrink to profitability." **Named conflict:** United publicly blamed "connecting passengers and the FAA," but the operative reason was post-merger redundancy — CLE was the smallest of nine hubs [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2014/02/04/united-cuts-its-cleveland-hub-blames-connecting-passengers-and-the-faa/]. The airport's response infrastructure — gates, a concourse — became overhang overnight.

**The pattern that indicts a $22B program.** In all four, the airport had recently built or was still paying for hub-scale infrastructure; MII did not prevent the buildout (airlines want the gates when the hub is up); and residual structures transferred *rate* risk but not the *stranded-asset* risk that lands on the airport's balance sheet when the carrier leaves. Each carrier issued a network-optimization statement that trailed the route economics by 12–18 months.

**The counter-case — why IAD is not (yet) CLE.** United is currently *investing*: Concourse E, a 435,000 sq ft / 14-gate build opening late 2026 (>$500M), 250→280 daily departures (+25% vs 2023), and 13 new domestic routes in 2024 — "more than any other U.S. carrier added from any U.S. airport that year" — plus new long-haul to Dakar, Nice, Venice [Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/more-than-half-a-billion-dollar-expansion--modernization-coming-to-uniteds-washington-dulles-hub-302320375.html]. This is real and it is recent. But it is a bet on the *current* JV-gateway logic and on EWR staying constrained. The strategist's discipline: an airport at the top of a carrier's investment list in 2026 was, at CLE and CVG, at the top of the list a decade before it was cut. Commitment is a snapshot, not a covenant.

## 5. The Veto Applied to This Program

**What holds through 2050 is what United cannot substitute:** the transatlantic/Africa/India widebody gateway function, anchored by the A++ JV and by DCA's perimeter and slot cage. Design *for* that — deep widebody contact stands, apron geometry and taxiway fillets for the largest aircraft, international arrivals/FIS capacity, and gate flexibility that lets one stand swing between a widebody long-haul and two narrowbodies. That is the part of United's commitment with the longest half-life.

**What is a bet, not a given:** the domestic connecting bank. It rests on RJ economics, on EWR staying full, and on United not acquiring or being acquired into a network where IAD becomes the redundant node. A terminal whose *fixed geometry* is sized to today's connecting-bank peak is exposed exactly where CVG, STL, and CLE were exposed. Size the connecting complex soft — gates that can be shed or re-tenanted without stranding structure.

**The financial veto:** $21.8B of new bonds on a 14M-enplanement base, mostly backstopped by a residual agreement with one 68% carrier, recreates the 2013 cost structure that put IAD on United's dehub list. If United will not sign a use-and-lease term that extends materially beyond the current 2039 horizon *and* co-underwrite the connecting-hub scope through MII, the program is building hub-scale infrastructure on a hub commitment the anchor tenant has declined to guarantee. **No terminal design solves that. The mitigation is contractual and phased, not architectural.** Any Council recommendation that does not condition the connecting-hub scope on a matched-duration carrier commitment is incomplete.
