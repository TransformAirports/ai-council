# Machiavelli — Stage 1 Brief

## Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild: Who Actually Decides What Gets Locked In

*Independent lens for the Transform Airports AI Research Council. My job is not to forecast the 2050 terminal — the other agents will do that better. My job is to explain why the building will get built to satisfy incentives that have nothing to do with 2050, and what to do about it.*

---

### The thesis is right about the failure and wrong about the cause

The run-prompt's framing is sharp: mega-terminals open functionally dated because they fix built form around a passenger-processing sequence that is already decentralizing. But it locates the failure in the design brief — "designers fix geometry around the throughput logic of the decade they were drawn in." That is the polite, technical version. It treats the rigidity as a forecasting error, as if better planners with better models would have drawn a more flexible building.

They wouldn't have. The rigidity is not a forecasting failure. It is an **incentive-alignment failure**, and it is overdetermined. Every actor who touches a $22B terminal program is optimizing for something on a horizon far shorter than the asset's, and almost none of those incentives reward flexibility. Future-proofing loses not because nobody can see the future but because nobody in the room is paid to protect it. If the Council recommends design principles without redesigning who-decides-what-and-when, the principles will be value-engineered out in year three and the report will read, in 2050, like every other master plan that promised adaptability.

So this brief maps the incentive terrain first, then names the specific moves that make flexibility survive contact with the humans.

---

### Map the actors and their real horizons

Follow the calendar and the budget, not the vision statement.

**The political sponsor (short horizon, high leverage).** This program has a named federal patron: the reporting is explicit that the Trump administration wants Dulles "rebuilt into a world-class hub," and MWAA's board seats three presidential appointees confirmed by the Senate alongside seven Virginia and four D.C. appointees. A White House–backed megaproject has a horizon measured in one to three years — the groundbreaking, the visible cranes, the ribbon. The accelerated eight-year build (construction 2026, complete ~2034) is itself a political artifact: speed *is* the deliverable. Nobody sponsoring this program will be in office when the 2050 gate-flexibility bet pays off. The asset's most consequential design decisions will be signed off by people whose personal payoff is the announcement, not the operation.

**The MWAA board (medium horizon, rotating).** Six-year terms, one reappointment, chair elected annually. Across a program that spans 2026–2034 and an asset that must work to 2050, the board that approves Phase 1 geometry is not the board that lives with Phase 3, and neither is the board that operates the finished building. Governance discontinuity is structural. Whoever controls the *early* phases controls the column grid, the level changes, and the apron everyone else must build against — and they will be gone before the constraint bites.

**United Airlines (long operational horizon, narrow interest).** IAD is a United hub; United is the residual signatory whose cost-per-enplanement pays for this. IAD's CPE has historically run high and was only pulled below ~$20 with outside help. That is the single most important fact about who controls this building. Under a residual-rate use-and-lease agreement with a majority-in-interest clause, the anchor carrier gets an effective veto over capital spend it does not want to fund. United's horizon is genuinely long — but its interest is narrow: *its* network economics, *its* gates, *its* CPE. United will not pay today for structural over-provisioning that benefits a hypothetical 2045 competitor, an off-airport processing model that erodes its lounge-and-dwell economics, or apron flexibility sized for a fleet it doesn't fly. The carrier's rational self-interest and the airport's option value diverge exactly on the questions the thesis cares about most.

**TSA and CBP (immovable, status-quo-weighted).** Decentralized screening is not MWAA's decision to make. TSA owns the checkpoint concept of operations, the staffing model, and the liability. Its institutional incentives — uniform national standards, centralized supervision, minimized labor cost, minimized blame surface — all point toward the centralized checkpoint that exists today. The airport can provision *for* distributed screening; it cannot *deliver* it unilaterally. Any design that assumes gate-level or off-airport screening is a bet on a federal agency changing behavior against its own incentive gradient. (More on how to hedge that below.)

**The design-and-construction complex (paid on what's fixed, not what's soft).** Architects win awards and repeat commissions for legible, photographable form — not for empty, provisioned, deliberately-unfinished shell. Engineers and contractors bill against a defined program; "soft space we're choosing not to design yet" generates no fee and carries schedule risk. The entire delivery apparatus has a gentle, honest, structural bias toward resolving ambiguity into concrete now. Nobody is corrupt. Everybody is responding to how they're paid.

**Grounding note.** None of this requires villains. The board members are public servants; United is running a hub honestly; TSA is protecting the flying public; the architects want a building they're proud of. The rigidity is the *emergent* product of ordinary, defensible incentives pointing the same direction. That is worse than villainy, because there is no one to fire — you have to redesign the incentives themselves.

### The gap between stated and revealed preferences

Every megaterminal program says the same three words: *flexible, adaptable, future-proof.* The budget, the RFP scoring, and the schedule reveal what is actually valued.

- **What gets measured** in the delivery contract: on-time, on-budget, program-complete, and — because there's a political sponsor — fast. Flexibility appears in the vision deck and in no line of the incentive contract. What is unmeasured is unmanaged, and what is unmanaged is unfunded.
- **The revealed test** is what happens in the value-engineering meeting that *will* arrive around 2029–2030 when the program is over budget (it will be; $21.8B of the $22B is bonded debt including inflation and interest — the nominal number is already carrying assumptions that won't hold). In that meeting, spare structural capacity, extra conduit, deeper utility spines, apron depth held for stands that don't exist yet, and soft space with no assigned program are the *first* things cut. They have no operational constituency, no tenant demanding them, no line item screaming when they vanish. They are, precisely, the things the thesis argues are most valuable and least recoverable.

This is the crux. **The incentive structure systematically under-provides exactly the durable, cheap-now/unaffordable-later provisioning that is the whole point of future-proofing.** The 50-year moves (structure, column grid, level changes, apron geometry, vertical-circulation and utility spines, power/comms/conduit headroom) are cheap as a share of $22B and impossible to retrofit — and they are invisible, defenseless, and unloved by every actor in the room. The 5–15-year moves (IT, sensing, security fit-out) are visible, championed, and easy to change later — and they get the attention. The program's natural gradient is exactly backwards from what the asset needs.

### The failure taxonomy is a taxonomy of political decisions wearing design costumes

The run-prompt asks which single design decision dated fastest in each recent megaterminal. Read the honest ones and the fastest-dating decision is almost always the one made to satisfy a *sponsor's timeline or image*, not a planner's forecast.

- **LaGuardia.** The most instructive case because it's the closest analog: a governor's signature program, merged from a design competition into a "shovel-ready" scheme unveiled in July 2015 and under construction eight months later — with most of the competition's *transportation* recommendations discarded to hit the political timeline. LGA's constrained footprint and dual-island geometry were locked to the speed of the announcement. The building is admired and already boxed in. Speed was the sponsor's product; flexibility was the price.
- **BER (Berlin).** Not a design-forecast failure — a governance failure. Politicians sat on the supervisory board, technical expertise was thin, and every change became a political debate in which political judgment overrode technical judgment. Fourteen years, 120,000 defects. The lesson for Dulles is not "avoid exotic smoke-extraction"; it's "a board of political appointees making technical calls is the failure mode itself."
- **IST / PKX.** State-driven prestige megaprojects where national image and opening-date symbolism dominated. Grandeur and singular geometry were the point; adaptability was never the brief.

The pattern: what dates fastest is whatever got fixed to serve a short-horizon actor's payoff. For Dulles — a White House–branded, speed-first program — that risk is not hypothetical. **The single biggest future-proofing threat is that Phase 1 geometry gets frozen to the political groundbreaking calendar before the uncertain elements are resolved.** Break ground before the blueprint is done and you inherit BER's disease with LGA's footprint.

---

### Screening, gates, and off-airport processing are bets on other institutions' behavior

The thesis correctly identifies screening decentralization, the collapse of the 1:1 gate–aircraft relationship, and on-device/off-airport processing as the volatile frontier. The Machiavellian read: **each of these depends on an actor MWAA does not control changing its behavior against its own incentives.**

- Distributed screening requires TSA to abandon centralized supervision — against its cost, standardization, and liability incentives.
- Off-airport / curb-to-gate processing requires TSA *and* CBP concessions and reallocates dwell revenue away from the terminal and, often, away from the anchor carrier's lounge economics — so United has reason to resist.
- Breaking the 1:1 gate relationship (common-use, remote stands, swing gates) reduces a hub carrier's territorial control of "its" gates — which carriers resist even when it's efficient, because exclusive-use gates are a competitive moat.

The correct posture toward a bet you don't control is not to *design around it* (techno-optimism on a schedule, which the run-prompt rightly bans) and not to *ignore it* (inertia). It is to **buy the option cheaply and refuse to pay for the commitment.** Provision the physical substrate — floor loading, power, comms, drainage, structural bays, and knock-out panels at gate lines and curb — that *would* let screening distribute or processing move, at pennies-on-the-dollar today, and commit the operational model to nothing. That converts a forecast you'll get wrong into an option you can exercise if and when the federal partner or the carrier moves. The building says "yes, later" without betting on a date.

### Recommendations: design FOR / accommodate LATER / bet AGAINST — and the governance to make it stick

The other agents will populate the technical content of these three buckets. My contribution is the sort *and* the enforcement mechanism, because a sorted list with no defender is just a nicer memo to ignore.

**Design FOR now (lock it — it's durable and the incentive gradient will under-supply it, so overbuild deliberately):**
1. Structural grid, floor-to-floor heights, and level changes generous enough to accept a screening hall that moves, shrinks, or distributes. The bones are the one thing 2050 cannot retrofit.
2. Apron geometry and stand depth sized for fleet *uncertainty* — swing capability between widebody and single-aisle, contact-to-remote convertibility — even though United won't ask for it.
3. Utility, power, comms, and vertical-circulation spines over-provisioned with spare capacity and physical headroom. Decarbonization load (electrified GSE charging, thermal, on-site generation) is a hard future demand; the conduit and switchgear space is cheap now and unaffordable to chase through an operating terminal later.

**Accommodate LATER (provision the substrate, commit to no operational model — buy options, not commitments):**
4. Knock-out panels, spare bays, and pre-run power/comms at gate lines, curb, and concourse ends for distributed screening and off-airport processing *if* TSA/CBP move.
5. Soft space with structural and MEP capacity but no fixed program, explicitly reserved for a commercial/dwell model that will relocate as processing time compresses.

**Bet AGAINST (do not spend to design around these; treat their arrival dates as unknown):**
6. eVTOL-at-scale, universal biometrics as a delivered reality, and full autonomous ground handling on a schedule. Provision structure that *could* host them; design no operation that *needs* them.

**The enforcement layer — without this, the above is decoration:**

- **Give flexibility a dollar value and a named owner.** Ring-fence a "durability/optionality reserve" the way a program rings-fences contingency, with a board-level defender who has standing to block its raid in the VE meeting. Unowned flexibility dies; owned flexibility survives.
- **Sequence the phasing so stability leads and volatility trails.** Put the durable, low-uncertainty bones — spines, grid, apron — in the early phases the political calendar wants to build anyway. Push the genuinely uncertain elements (the screening hall, the gate/dwell interface) into *later* phases where more information will exist. This aligns the sponsor's speed incentive with the asset's need to defer volatile decisions, instead of letting speed freeze the wrong things first. Do not let the groundbreaking calendar fix what should stay soft.
- **Write flexibility into the delivery and use-and-lease contracts, or it isn't real.** Score the design RFP on *measured* convertibility (bays that swing, shell provisioned and verified), not on image. And structure the United negotiation so that MWAA — not the anchor carrier's CPE-minimization — retains control of the durable bones. If the residual-rate/MII structure lets United veto over-provisioning, the airport must fund the durable substrate through a mechanism the carrier cannot strip, or accept that the building will be optimized for United's 2030 economics and no one's 2050 options.
- **Keep political appointees away from technical calls.** BER's lesson, stated plainly: insulate constructability and life-safety decisions from a board whose composition and horizon are political. Name who owns the technical veto and protect it.

---

### The one-line version for the program meeting

The Dulles terminal will not fail to be future-proof because its designers can't see 2050. It will fail because a White House–timed, United-funded, TSA-constrained, politically-governed $22B program has no actor whose payoff depends on 2050 — and flexibility, being invisible and unowned, is the first thing every one of those actors will trade away. Future-proofing this building is 20% design principle and 80% deciding *now*, in the governance and the contracts, who is paid to protect the empty space. Lock the bones, buy the options, bet against the schedules, and give the softness a defender with a veto. Everything else is a nicer master plan headed for the same value-engineering meeting.
