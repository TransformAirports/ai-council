# Contrarian Brief — Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

**Role:** Stress-test the run thesis. My job is to build the strongest honest case *against* the proposition that hub terminals designed today will open "functionally dated" because they lock in a centralized, sequenced passenger-processing model that is decentralizing and de-sequencing. My value to the Council is opposition to the position the panel is predisposed to adopt.

**The contrarian position, in one line:** The thesis asks the Dulles program to buy expensive optionality against a set of changes that are unproven, slow, and — where they have already been available for decades — chronically fail to scale. The larger risk at Dulles is not building a terminal that dates; it is under-committing to durable, high-throughput physical infrastructure in order to hedge bets that will not pay, and calling that hedge "future-proofing."

---

## 1. The strongest version of the argument

- **The centralized checkpoint is a durable feature, not a transitional one — and the binding constraint is labor, not real estate.** Screening distributes only if you are willing to multiply the scarcest, most regulated resource in the building: certified officers. Every credible operator move in the last five years has run *toward* consolidation to maximize officer productivity, not away from it. Designing soft space for a distributed checkpoint is designing for a world that the security economics actively push against.

- **"Future-proofing through flexibility" is an option you pay for up front and usually never exercise.** The academic best case for real-options terminal design puts the financial upside at roughly 5% over a conventional phased plan — and that is the optimized result, not the median. Flexible column grids, soft space, and over-provisioned services carry real capital and floor-area cost today against a payoff that arrives only if a specific future materializes on schedule. Most soft space gets leased, walled, and forgotten.

- **The failure taxonomy the thesis leans on is a category error.** BER, Heathrow T5, and their peers did not fail because they froze a processing sequence. They failed on governance, fire-suppression engineering, baggage-system integration, testing time, and scope creep. Attributing execution disasters to "built form sized for the wrong decade" launders a management story into an architecture story. The lesson those projects teach is *build the conventional thing competently*, not *build the unconventional thing*.

- **Off-airport and de-sequenced processing has been available for 30+ years and never became the dominant mode.** Hong Kong's Airport Express in-town check-in and Vienna's City Air Terminal have existed since the 1990s. They remain niche, some stations are suspended, and they ride on rail spines most US hubs do not have. Three decades of availability with no tipping point is strong evidence that migration off-airport is a slow trickle, not a structural break arriving by 2035.

- **Physical capacity ceilings are real and induced demand guarantees you hit them.** Air travel grows 4–5% a year and expanded capacity fills; runway and apron geometry, not software, is the binding constraint at mature hubs. Sizing structure, apron, and vertical circulation for genuine peak throughput is the decision that ages *best*, because demand reliably rises to meet it.

- **Asset renewal is not optional and it is the actual reason Dulles is being rebuilt.** The Saarinen terminal opened in 1962; the mobile lounges are a 60-year-old processing artifact. Structure carries a ~50-year design life. Dulles is spending $22B because the physical plant is worn out and functionally obsolete — a depreciation-driven renewal, not a bet on a processing paradigm. Renewal decisions should be governed by lifecycle and throughput certainty, not by speculative de-sequencing.

- **The thesis violates its own run rules.** The run explicitly forbids "techno-optimism on a schedule" and treating unproven timelines as givens. Designing the built form *against* the current typology — betting that screening distributes and the 1:1 gate relationship dissolves inside the 10–25 year window — is exactly that: a schedule bet on eVTOL-adjacent, biometrics-universal, off-device-processing futures whose arrival dates are unknown.

- **Visible, robust infrastructure is defensible; soft bets are not.** A $22B program funded by $21.8B in new bonds must survive rating agencies, an airline use-and-lease negotiation, and a board. Runways, aprons, structural bays, and a working AeroTrain are financeable and legible. "We left it soft in case screening decentralizes" is the first line item cut when the budget tightens, and the first thing blamed if the terminal underperforms.

## 2. Evidence

**Centralized screening is consolidating, not distributing.** The operator trend is explicit: Kansas City's new terminal centralized its checkpoint specifically "to handle larger crowds," consolidating standard, PreCheck, ADA, and Touchless ID lines into a single location [Source: https://www.kctv5.com/2026/04/10/kci-centralizes-security-checkpoint-handle-larger-crowds/]. Where "remote" screening appears, it means sending images to a *centralized* location to "maximize the productivity" of screening staff — centralizing the labor, not the queue [Source: https://www.airport-technology.com/features/improving-safety-tsa-checkpoints/]. TSA's own 2025 solicitation seeks to "reduce total operating costs, workforce requirements, and manual labor" — i.e., fewer officers per passenger, which distributed gate-level screening structurally opposes [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2025/07/25/tsa-seeks-private-sector-solutions-enhance-airport-security-and]. The checkpoint persists because officer staffing is the scarce resource and consolidation is how you economize on it.

**Flexibility's payoff is modest even in its best-case literature.** The real-options case study most cited in favor of flexible terminal design — Zurich — found the flexible alternative worth "approximately 5% higher than the best conventional phased plan" [Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23249935.2023.2287502]. That is the optimized upside under favorable assumptions. The same literature concedes that "under and over provision of capacity is a recurrent risk" — flexibility does not remove the risk, it repriced it and added carrying cost [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2324993523003202].

**The mega-terminal "failures" were execution failures, not paradigm failures.** BER's delay traced to a fire-protection and smoke-extraction system "not built according to the construction permit," a supervisory board "full of politicians who had no idea how to supervise the project," no general contractor, and mid-build scope creep that turned the terminal from a rectangle into a U; budget went from €2.83B to €6.5–7.3B over 29 years [Source: https://interestingengineering.com/science/heres-how-berlin-brandenburg-airport-became-one-of-the-biggest-engineering-failures] [Source: https://www.thelocal.de/20201030/the-real-story-behind-why-berlin-ber-airport-took-nine-years-to-open]. Heathrow T5's opening meltdown — 500 flights cancelled, 23,000 bags stranded over five days — was caused by too few baggage handlers plane-side, IT integration complexity, and truncated testing time, not by a dated processing geometry [Source: https://www.npr.org/2008/03/31/89231792/problems-plague-heathrow-airports-new-terminal] [Source: https://londonairtravel.com/2019/08/10/british-airways-100-years-heathrow-terminal-5/]. None of these projects would have been saved by softer, less committed built form. Several were harmed by change.

**A physically decisive geometry bet aged *well*.** Beijing Daxing fixed a radial "starfish" plan sized so the farthest gate sits ~600m — about eight minutes — from the center, an irreversible structural decision that is now praised for minimizing walking across the world's largest single-building terminal [Source: https://www.businesstraveller.com/features/phoenix-rising-beijings-new-daxing-international-airport/] [Source: http://en.people.cn/n3/2019/0911/c90000-9614024.html]. The lesson cuts against the thesis: the durable win came from committing hard to a physical form, not from leaving it soft.

**The conventional rebuild is the one that won awards, on time and on budget.** LaGuardia Terminal B — a headhouse-plus-concourse typology with centralized processing — was named best new airport terminal in the world (UNESCO Prix Versailles; first US Skytrax 5-star), delivered on time and on budget as part of an $8B program [Source: https://www.portauthoritybuilds.com/redevelopment/us/en/lga.html] [Source: https://dbia.org/project/a-whole-new-laguardia-terminal-b-redevelopment/]. Its celebrated "island-and-bridges" move was a *constructability* solution to build over live operations — not a de-sequencing of the passenger journey [Source: https://paacademy.com/blog/laguardia-airport-terminal-b-case-study]. Conventional, centralized, and superbly executed beat unconventional and speculative.

**Off-airport processing is a 30-year-old idea that never scaled.** Hong Kong's Airport Express in-town check-in and Vienna's City Air Terminal at Wien-Mitte have offered curb-to-city baggage and check-in since the 1990s; both remain optional, airline-dependent, rail-dependent, and partial, with some facilities (e.g., Kowloon) suspended [Source: https://www.hongkongairport.com/en/baggage/airport-express-check-in.page] [Source: https://viennaairport.com/en/passengers/arrival__departure/city_check-in]. Three decades without a tipping point is the single strongest data point against the claim that processing is about to "migrate off-site."

**Induced demand makes throughput the safe bet.** Air travel demand is projected to grow 4–5% per year while "runway capacity [is] the most stringent constraint on growth"; expanded capacity is absorbed rather than banked [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191261523000231]. Sizing durable physical assets for real peak demand is the decision least likely to be regretted.

**Dulles is an asset-renewal program with a 50-year structural horizon.** Terminal structures are designed to a ~50-year service life; depreciation is a function of utilization and is not deferrable [Source: https://www.lfspaceframe.com/news/structural-design-of-airport-terminal-building-part-1/]. The $22B program — $21.8B in new bonds — keeps the 1962 Saarinen headhouse, extends it 300 feet each way, replaces obsolete concourses, and eliminates the mobile lounges via AeroTrain extension [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/] [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. This is a worn-out plant being renewed on a bond-financed clock, not a laboratory for de-sequencing.

## 3. Where this contrarian position is wrong — the honest concessions

If the panel is going to publish the pro-infrastructure case, it has to survive these, because they are real:

1. **Provisioning asymmetry is genuinely on the thesis's side.** Conduit, power, structural spare capacity, and floor-to-floor height are cheap to install now and ruinously expensive to retrofit into an occupied terminal. My "flexibility is overpriced" argument holds for *fit-out and program* flexibility; it collapses for *deep-service provisioning*. A defensible brief must separate these. Overbuilding power and riser capacity for electrified GSE and unknown IT load is not speculation — it is the cheapest insurance in the building.

2. **The 1:1 gate–aircraft relationship is already loosening, and that is apron geometry, not software.** Fleet mix shifts (widebody retirement, single-aisle long-haul, regionals) and swing/MARS stands are real, deployed hedges at ICN, DOH, and ORD. Apron and stand flexibility is a durable physical decision, and here the thesis is right that rigidity is a liability. My case is strongest on *screening* and *off-airport processing*; it is weakest on *stands*.

3. **"It never scaled in 30 years" can be a phase-change setup, not a verdict.** Off-airport check-in stayed niche partly for want of the rail and identity infrastructure that is now arriving (mobile ID, biometric enrollment, transit connectivity Dulles will have via Metro). Absence of a past tipping point does not prove absence of a future one. My argument is inductive, and induction breaks at inflection points.

4. **Centralized-screening consolidation is a present-tense fact, not a 25-year guarantee.** I am extrapolating today's TSA labor economics across the design life of the building. If self-service, on-person screening technology matures, the checkpoint's floor demand could shrink dramatically — stranding a centrally massed hall. The safer read is: don't distribute, but don't *oversize* the central checkpoint either.

5. **Daxing and LGA are survivorship evidence.** I cited two committed-geometry successes; the thesis can counter with committed-geometry regrets (over-scaled halls, mobile lounges themselves). Commitment wins when the bet is right and loses when it is wrong — which is precisely the thesis's point.

## 4. Data points a strategist must address or concede

1. **"Flexible... approximately 5% higher than the best conventional phased plan"** [Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23249935.2023.2287502]. The pro-flexibility literature's headline number is single digits, and it is the optimized case. Any brief recommending expensive optionality must justify it against a payoff this modest — or argue the option value is non-financial (resilience), which is a different and harder claim.

2. **BER: €2.83B → €6.5–7.3B over 29 years, felled by fire systems and governance** [Source: https://interestingengineering.com/science/heres-how-berlin-brandenburg-airport-became-one-of-the-biggest-engineering-failures]. If the marquee "dated mega-terminal" actually failed on execution, the failure taxonomy must be rewritten around delivery competence, not processing paradigm — which weakens the thesis's central exhibit.

3. **Off-airport check-in has existed since the 1990s and remains niche, with facilities suspended** [Source: https://www.hongkongairport.com/en/baggage/airport-express-check-in.page]. Any claim that "processing migrates off-airport" inside 10–25 years must explain why 30 years of availability produced no migration — and what specifically changes now.

4. **KCI centralized its checkpoint "to handle larger crowds"; TSA seeks to cut "workforce requirements... and manual labor"** [Source: https://www.kctv5.com/2026/04/10/kci-centralizes-security-checkpoint-handle-larger-crowds/] [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2025/07/25/tsa-seeks-private-sector-solutions-enhance-airport-security-and]. The live regulatory and labor trend runs opposite to distributed screening. A strategist recommending soft space for a distributed checkpoint must name the force that reverses this.

5. **LGA Terminal B: conventional typology, best new terminal in the world, on time and on budget** [Source: https://dbia.org/project/a-whole-new-laguardia-terminal-b-redevelopment/]. The most-praised recent US rebuild bet on the current typology executed superbly. The burden is on the thesis to show why Dulles should bet against a typology that just won the field's top honors.

---

## Bottom line for the Council

The thesis is right about one thing and wrong about the rest. It is right that *deep physical provisioning* and *apron/stand geometry* must be treated as durable, over-built, and locked now — retrofit is unaffordable and the fleet is genuinely diversifying. It is wrong to generalize that insight into a case against the centralized, sequenced processing typology, because the two changes it leans on hardest — distributed screening and off-airport processing — are contradicted by present labor economics and by thirty years of non-adoption. The honest recommendation is not "leave the processing sequence soft." It is: **lock structure, apron, riser/power, and vertical circulation hard and oversized; keep the checkpoint central but do not oversize its hall; and treat off-airport de-sequencing as an unfunded option, not a design driver.** Build the robust, conventional, superbly executed thing — the LGA lesson, not the BER one — and provision generously underneath it. That is what actually survives to 2050.
