# Virtual Chris — The Lattice Brief

## Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*Independent Stage 1 brief. Optimism about possibilities, rigor about facts. The other agents on this council will inventory the risks in the $22B program. My job is the connective tissue they'll skip: the alliances that already exist, the initiatives that become leverage, and the openings this thesis creates that nobody put on the agenda.*

A word on the factual spine, because the rest depends on it. MWAA's revitalization plan, shared with airlines in May 2026 and blessed by DOT Secretary Sean Duffy, is roughly a $20B build carried to ~$22B by inflation and interest — funded by about $21.8B in new bonds and $1.1B in airport fees, not federal grants. It adds four linear concourses, expands the Saarinen head house ~300 feet east and west, extends the Aerotrain across the whole field, and retires the mobile lounges. Construction starts this year; target completion is around 2034 — eight years against the old master plan's two decades. Cost per enplanement could reach ~$90 by 2035 absent federal subsidy; United, which takes Concourses B and C under the 2025–2039 use agreement, already flies the most expensive large-hub CPE in the country. Hold those numbers. Every connection below runs through them.

## 1. The Lattice

Eight connections this thesis activates — each a department, an initiative, and a stakeholder that the built-form question quietly ties together.

- **Aerotrain spine ↔ distributed screening ↔ Planning/Ops.** The single most durable thing MWAA will pour is the Aerotrain box and its stations — 50-year concrete. It is also the most *centralizing* commitment in the program, hard-coding a curb→core→gate flow at the exact moment the thesis says processing de-sequences. The resolution isn't to cancel the train; it's to provision each station now as a candidate distributed-processing node (power, comms, floor-to-floor height, queuing envelope). Overbuild the conduit, not the checkpoint. This is the highest-stakes durable-vs-volatile decision in the whole program, and it belongs to the same team drawing the tunnel.

- **Loudoun data centers ↔ terminal energy plant ↔ Facilities.** Dulles is being rebuilt inside Data Center Alley — 586+ facilities in Loudoun, the largest concentration on earth — where Dominion cannot deliver enough high-voltage power until the Golden-to-Mars 500kV loop closes (~2028) and residential rates are rising 14% in 2026 on data-center demand. The sustainability success criterion (electrified GSE, thermal load, on-site generation) reads as a cost until you see the neighbor: co-located load that needs redundancy and throws off waste heat. A shared substation, thermal loop, or behind-the-meter generation deal turns an energy liability into a regional asset. Why it matters: this is the cheapest thing to provision now (a reserved switchyard footprint and conduit) and the most unaffordable to retrofit.

- **Federal board seats ↔ the money gap ↔ DOT.** The President appoints 3 of MWAA's 17 board members; the Trump administration wants a "world-class hub" and issued the RFI. But the federal *cash* isn't there: the PFC is frozen at $4.50 since 2000, and the AIG supplement expires at the end of FY2026, dropping the grant baseline by ~$2.9B a year. Political will is high; appropriations are thin. That gap defines the realistic federal trade — streamlining, not subsidy (see §4).

- **Section 106 / Saarinen ↔ the durable layer ↔ preservation coalition.** The 1962 terminal has been NRHP-eligible since 1978, and Section 106 review binds any federal action touching it. Docomomo, the Art Deco Society, and independent preservationists have already filed. Read through the thesis, these aren't obstacles — they are the first constituency for "structure lives 50 years, fit-out lives five." The head house *is* the durable layer. The preservation argument and the future-proofing argument are the same sentence.

- **United ↔ gate flexibility ↔ 2025–2039 use agreement.** United hates paying for stranded assets and needs schedule elasticity as its fleet shifts toward single-aisle long-haul. The thesis's "break the 1:1 gate–aircraft relationship" is, translated, United's own hedge. Swing gates and MARS-capable stands are a shared win hiding inside a use agreement both parties must sign anyway.

- **Silver Line + Dulles Toll Road ↔ off-airport processing ↔ MWAA's own balance sheet.** MWAA already owns the decentralization infrastructure: Metrorail reached the terminal in November 2022, financed partly by tolls MWAA itself collects on Route 267. Remote bag-drop and curb-to-gate check-in at a Reston or Tysons node ride rails MWAA controls end to end. The landside/airside split the building is sized around is negotiable *because MWAA owns the corridor.*

- **2012 non-aviation land authority ↔ dwell relocation ↔ real estate.** Congress authorized MWAA in 2012 to develop federal airport land for non-aviation uses. If dwell monetizes off-airport and processing compresses, the revenue the terminal is sized around can migrate landside — hotel, conference, data-center-adjacent development — instead of being trapped in oversized airside retail the thesis warns will empty out. The revenue architecture has an escape hatch the building drawings usually ignore.

- **ARFF/public safety ↔ distributed screening ↔ the security regime.** Decentralized screening multiplies sterile-boundary geometry and changes the police/response footprint. The durable decision (where hardened walls and secure risers go) is made once; the volatile decision (how many checkpoints, what tech) changes every 5–15 years. These two teams rarely sit together early enough. They should here.

## 2. Three Pattern Matches

**1. Stewart Brand's shearing layers — the science of how buildings age.** In *How Buildings Learn*, Brand separates a building into layers that change at different speeds: Site (eternal), Structure (30–300 years), Skin (~20), Services (7–15), Space plan (3–30), Stuff (daily). Buildings fail when a fast layer is welded to a slow one — when you pour the wiring into the concrete. The mapping is exact and it is the whole thesis: Dulles's Site, Structure, and the Aerotrain box are the slow layers to lock; the security regime, sensing, and fit-out are fast layers to leave soft. The failure taxonomy the run asks for (BER, PKX, LGA, IST) is, in every case, a fast layer cast into a slow one — a checkpoint hall or baggage geometry sized for one decade's processing logic and structurally welded in. Hand leadership Brand's diagram and the durable-vs-volatile framework designs itself.

**2. The aircraft carrier flight deck — engineering for aircraft not yet invented.** A carrier's flight deck is deliberately generic: catapults, arresting gear, and deck geometry sized so the ship can operate airframes that don't exist when the keel is laid. The USS Enterprise launched aircraft its 1958 designers never drew. The apron and gate question is identical. You cannot forecast the 2050 fleet mix — widebody retirement, single-aisle long-haul, regional-plus-eVTOL — so you don't design gates *for* a fleet; you design a generic "flight deck" of flexible stands (MARS, swing, remote) that hosts a mix not yet certified. ICN, DOH, Changi T5, and the ORD Global Terminal are all paying a modest premium today for exactly this deck-level flexibility. That premium is the carrier's lesson priced out.

**3. Mainframe → edge computing — and the metaphor is literal here.** For thirty years, computing centralized: one mainframe, batch jobs, everyone queues for the core. Then it distributed to the edge — processing migrated to where the work actually happens, and the central facility shrank to a switch. The centralized checkpoint *is* the mainframe of airport design: batch-process every passenger through one hall. Distributed screening (TSA's Screening at Speed, CAT-2 self-service, self-pace prototypes already live at Harry Reid) is the edge migration. Here's the part that should stop an executive reader cold: Dulles is being rebuilt three miles from the largest concentration of edge computing on the planet, and the terminal is being drawn around a mainframe processing model. The industry next door already ran this exact transition and can tell you which fixed assets it stranded.

## 3. The Opportunity Register

Ranked by what MWAA can actually move in 24 months.

**1. An energy-adjacency MOU with Dominion and a data-center operator.** Most actionable, because the constraint (Dominion's Loudoun power bottleneck to ~2028) creates the counterparty's motive. Shared substation, thermal recovery, or behind-the-meter generation makes the terminal an energy node, not just a load. *First step:* commission a joint load-and-thermal study with Dominion, timed to the Golden-to-Mars 500kV in-service date, and reserve a switchyard footprint in the site plan before geometry hardens.

**2. A federal "entitlements-for-grants" trade.** The administration wants a showcase; the appropriations don't exist. Convert that mismatch into a *programmatic* Section 106 and NEPA agreement and P3 latitude — a federal contribution measured in schedule, not dollars. *First step:* request a programmatic agreement with the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation and SHPO now, while federal enthusiasm is peaking.

**3. Preservationists as design co-authors.** Turn Docomomo and the National Trust from Section 106 adversaries into narrators of the durable-layer story. *First step:* invite them into a head-house charrette to define what is sacrosanct (Saarinen's roof, the piers, the section) versus deliberately soft (everything behind the glass).

**4. A United swing-gate / MARS-stand deal inside the use agreement.** Flexibility is optionality United will pay for *if* the premium is shared and phased. *First step:* stand up a joint gate-flex working group before the concourse column grids are frozen.

**5. An off-airport processing pilot on the Silver Line.** Prove curb-to-gate decentralization on rails MWAA already owns. *First step:* a remote bag-drop pilot MOU with WMATA and TSA at a Reston or Tysons station — small, reversible, and a data-generating hedge against airside over-sizing.

## 4. The Collaboration Paths

**Opposition A — United Airlines (against rising CPE; against paying for flexibility it can't yet use).** United's objection is rational: it is already the most expensive large hub in the country, and "future-proofing" reads as billing it for optionality with no near-term revenue. The path from *against* to *with* runs through reframing flexibility as CPE insurance — the stranded-asset losses at LGA and BER are the alternative bill — and through a shared "flexibility reserve" that phases provisioning so United pays for soft space only as it's activated. *Convener:* MWAA CFO with United network planning. *First meeting:* a single-question session — "who pays for optionality, and when" — that ends with a term sheet for phased flexibility inside the 2025–2039 agreement, not a design review.

**Opposition B — Preservationists and the Section 106 process (against demolition or alteration of the Saarinen terminal).** This is the easiest hard opposition to convert, because the thesis already agrees with them. Their case — that the 1962 head house is a multi-decade asset worth protecting — is the durable-layer framework verbatim. The cautionary tale that unlocks the room is Penn Station: New York demolished an irreplaceable head house in 1963, spent sixty years regretting it, and only then learned (via Grand Central) that the preserved structure becomes the value engine. Demolition is the one move in this program that is truly irreversible. *Convener:* SHPO plus the National Trust with MWAA design leadership. *First meeting:* a charrette that produces a shared "sacrosanct vs. soft" map — giving preservationists a co-authored durable layer and giving MWAA a Section 106 partner instead of a plaintiff.

**Opposition C — The federal money gap (Congress on the frozen PFC; the FY2026 AIG cliff).** No one is exactly "against" Dulles here — they're against the traveler-fee increase and the grant dollars that would fund it, and the AIG supplement is expiring on schedule. The path is to stop asking for cash and start asking for what Washington can actually give: a programmatic environmental and historic-preservation agreement, P3 authority, and cover for MWAA's own non-aviation land revenue under the 2012 authorization. *Convener:* DOT with MWAA and the Virginia delegation. *First meeting:* a "federal contribution that isn't a check" session — trading the administration's showcase ambition for the regulatory certainty that saves years off an eight-year schedule.

## 5. Pull-Ready Observations

1. **"The Aerotrain is the most durable thing MWAA will pour and the most centralizing. Provision every station as a distributed processing node now, or the tunnel becomes a monument to a passenger flow that's already dissolving."**

2. **"Dulles is being financed on United's back at the moment its cost-per-enplanement is already the highest of any large hub in the country. The scarcest resource in this program isn't concrete — it's optionality the airline will actually pay for."**

3. **"The preservationists aren't the obstacle to future-proofing. They're its first coalition. Their argument that the Saarinen head house is a fifty-year asset is the durable-versus-volatile framework in a bow tie."**

4. **"The centralized checkpoint is the mainframe of airport design; distributed screening is the edge. Dulles is being rebuilt three miles from the largest concentration of edge computing on earth, and no one has noticed the metaphor is literal."**

5. **"The federal government wants a world-class hub but froze the fee that pays for one in 2000. The realistic federal contribution to Dulles isn't a check — it's a signature on a programmatic environmental agreement that buys back years of the eight-year schedule."**
