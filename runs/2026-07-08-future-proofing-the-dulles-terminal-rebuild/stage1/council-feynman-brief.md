# Feynman Brief: Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*An independent lens for the Council. Method: start from what the thing physically does, build up from there, refuse any word that hides a confusion, and check every claim against something that actually happened.*

---

## 1. Strip it down: what is a terminal, really?

Forget "gateway," "journey," "experience." At the lowest level a passenger terminal is a **machine for moving human bodies and their bags from parked cars to parked airplanes, and back.** Everything else is decoration on that function.

That machine has exactly three things that set its shape:

1. **The size of the airplane.** Wingspan and door height fix apron geometry, bridge reach, and the column-free bay you need at the frontage. A body can't shrink a 777.
2. **How far a human will walk (and how much vertical change they'll take with a bag).** This fixes concourse length, the number of level changes, and where you must put moving walkways or a train. Bodies haven't changed since 1962.
3. **Where you are forced to funnel everyone through a single controlled point.** Today that's the security checkpoint. It is the only mandatory choke point between the curb and the plane, and it is the single most powerful shaper of built form in the entire building.

Notice what's *not* on that list: IT, sensing, biometrics, retail fit-out, the security *technology*, the airline lounge program. Those are real, but they ride on top of the machine. They change on a 5-to-15-year clock. The three things above change on a 30-to-50-year clock or never.

**That rate-of-change gap is the whole durable-vs-volatile question.** It is not a philosophy. It's arithmetic: lock what tracks the slow clock, leave soft what tracks the fast clock, and — the part everyone skips — *actually compute what it costs to be wrong in each direction.*

---

## 2. The one fact that should reorganize this entire program: Dulles already ran the experiment

Here is the thing I cannot get past, and I think the design team has to stare at it directly.

The thesis says the future is **de-sequencing** — the gate stops being 1:1 with the aircraft, processing decentralizes, the passenger is brought to the plane rather than the plane to a fixed jetway.

**Dulles was built in 1962 as exactly that bet.** Saarinen's original concept had *no concourses and no fixed gates.* Aircraft parked out on an open apron wherever there was room. Passengers were driven to the airplane in "mobile lounges" — rolling rooms on truck chassis. The gate-to-aircraft relationship was deliberately **not** 1:1; a plane could park anywhere and the building would come to it. This is, almost line for line, the decentralized, de-sequenced, non-fixed-gate future the thesis imagines for 2050.

It failed. Not because anyone mis-forecast technology. It failed on **physics and unit economics**: the mobile lounges were slow, they were labor-heavy, they didn't scale with traffic, they created a serial bottleneck exactly when volume rose, and connecting passengers — the lifeblood of a hub — hated and lost time in them. So the airport spent decades bolting on the very thing Saarinen designed *against*: fixed midfield concourses (A/B), a "temporary" C/D that has outlived several presidents, and finally the Aerotrain in 2010. The $22B program's headline act is **killing the mobile lounges for good.**

Two conclusions fall straight out of this, and they point in opposite directions — which is why it's the right anchor:

- **For the futurists:** the "bring the building to the plane, decouple gate from aircraft" idea is not new and not obviously progress. It was tried here, at this airport, and the market and the physics rejected it. Anyone proposing to design 2050 Dulles around de-sequenced processing owes an answer to: *what is physically different this time that makes the thing that failed here now work?* "Better software" is not an answer until you can name the pounds, minutes, and dollars it removes.
- **For the traditionalists:** the reason it failed — serial processing that doesn't scale — is precisely the failure mode that *centralized checkpoints* are now walking into. So you don't get to use the mobile-lounge corpse to defend the status quo either.

The mobile lounge is the perfect teaching example because it's the thesis's own idea, run to completion, with a known outcome. Use it as the honesty check on every "de-sequencing" claim in the program.

## 3. The checkpoint: is the choke point durable, or is it the *machine* that's transitional?

This is where jargon does the most damage, so let's go slow and literal.

Why does screening exist at all? Because you cannot allow a weapon into a pressurized aluminum tube at 35,000 feet. That constraint is permanent. It is not going away by 2050.

Why is screening *centralized*? Two real reasons, and they are different:

- **The machine is big, expensive, and staffed.** CT scanners and their operators are capital you want to amortize across the most passengers, so you pile them into one hall.
- **You need a topological boundary** — a clean membrane between "not yet screened" (landside) and "screened" (sterile/airside). Below the membrane, anyone. Above it, only cleared people and cleared goods. That boundary has to be *continuous and un-jumpable*, or the whole thing is theater.

Now the Feynman question: **which of those two is the durable thing?**

The *machine* is the volatile part. Boston Logan is, as of June 2026, running live off-airport screening out of a facility in **Framingham, ~20 miles from the terminal** — passengers on JetBlue and Delta clear security in a suburb and arrive at the airport already sterile. Las Vegas (Harry Reid) is running **self-service** checkpoint lanes where PreCheck passengers screen themselves at their own pace. Both are real, both are deployed, both are small. They tell you the *machine* is going to get smaller, cheaper, self-operated, and physically movable within 10–25 years. That is a genuine trend, not a brochure.

But watch what does **not** dissolve. Even if you distribute the machines to every gate, or push them to a garage, or to Framingham — **you still need the sterile/non-sterile membrane.** Someone screened in Framingham has to stay sterile all the way to the seat, which means a protected, boundary-continuous path from that facility onto the aircraft. Distributing the *machine* doesn't delete the *boundary*; it just changes where the boundary runs.

**So here is the durable-vs-volatile split for screening, done from first principles:**

| Element | Clock | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| The need to screen | Permanent | Always design for it |
| The *centralized hall* (big fixed checkpoint) | Volatile (5–15 yr) | **Do not** pour it as immovable structure. Size it generously, but make the floor plate a large clear-span room that can be re-tenanted. |
| The *sterile/non-sterile boundary topology* | Durable (30–50 yr) | **This** is the thing to lock. Vertical circulation cores, the landside/airside stacking, and the connector geometry must let the membrane be *redrawn* — pulled forward to the gate, or pushed off-site — without moving a column or a floor slab. |

The mistake mega-terminals make is treating the checkpoint *hall* as permanent structure. The durable commitment is not the hall — it's a **clear-span, boundary-agnostic floor plate** and a circulation spine that lets you move the membrane later. Build the room so screening can migrate to the gate *or* off to Framingham *or* stay central, and you've hedged the one genuinely uncertain thing without betting on which way it breaks. That's the opposite of "design for distributed screening now." It's "design so the location of the boundary is cheap to change." Reversible. Cheap. Honest about not knowing.

## 4. Gates and apron: flexibility is not free, and "future-proof" is where value engineering goes to kill it

The apron and column grid are the slowest clock in the building — they track aircraft dimensions, which move on 30–50-year cycles. Get these wrong and you cannot fix them; get them "maximally flexible" and you may have overpaid for insurance you never claim.

The honest tension, stated plainly:

- **MARS stands** (one widebody *or* two narrowbody on the same position, dual bridges) are the real, proven hedge against fleet-mix uncertainty. They're how you avoid betting on the widebody-vs-single-aisle-long-haul question you genuinely can't call. They cost more per position — more bridge, more apron depth, more lead-in complexity, more software and training.
- **International/domestic "swing" gates** hedge the other uncertainty (which passenger needs to be routed through Federal Inspection). They require the sterile-corridor and boundary geometry from Section 3 to be built in from the start — you cannot retrofit a swing gate cheaply, because it's a boundary-topology feature, not a fit-out feature.

Here's the Feynman warning, and it's the one a program meeting most needs to hear: **flexibility is an insurance premium, and nobody on the team is pricing the premium against the probability of the claim.** "Make it flexible" and "future-proof it" are cargo-cult phrases when they arrive without a number. A terminal where *every* stand is a universal MARS swing gate is more expensive per unit of throughput than one tuned to a realistic fleet forecast — you've bought insurance on risks that won't all materialize. And because that premium shows up as capital cost with no day-one revenue, it is *the first thing value-engineered out* when the $22B number gets scary in year three.

So the rule isn't "maximize flexibility." It's: **decide which specific uncertainty each flexible feature insures against, estimate the probability and the cost-of-being-wrong, and buy only the insurance that pays.** Concretely for apron/gate:
- **Overbuild (cheap now, unaffordable later):** apron depth and pavement strength, bridge foundations, the *provisioning* for dual bridges, utility/fuel/preconditioned-air and **ground-power capacity to the stand.** These are dirt and conduit. You cannot add apron depth after the concourse is built.
- **Leave soft (buy later when you know):** the second bridge itself, the actual gate-lounge fit-out, the stand-mix ratio. Install to a realistic forecast; provision for the extreme.

The 1:1 gate–aircraft question is real but slower and softer than the deck-talk implies. Remote/bussed stands and common-use gates already break 1:1 at the operational layer without any structural commitment. That's a soft lever. Don't pour concrete to solve a scheduling problem.

## 5. Failure taxonomy: every one dated the same way

I looked for the single decision that dated fastest in each recent mega-terminal. There's a pattern, and it's the thesis restated as a law:

**A terminal dates the instant a choke point it was sized around moves, appears, or disappears.** The building is a frozen photograph of one throughput assumption. Change the assumption and the geometry is wrong overnight — and geometry is the one thing you can't re-pour.

- **MCI (Kansas City), 1972 horseshoe:** the clearest case in the whole set. Sized around *drive-to-your-gate* — park, walk 30 meters, board. Zero centralized choke point, because in 1972 there wasn't one. Post-2001 security *created* a choke point the geometry had no room for, and the horseshoes became unscreenable. They demolished all three and opened a single centralized terminal in 2023. **A checkpoint appeared and the building died.** This is Section 3's warning with a body count.
- **Dulles mobile lounges, 1962:** sized around a serial people-mover that couldn't scale with volume — *the choke point was the building's own transport system.* Being undone now, at cost, in this very program.
- **The general pattern for IST / PKX / BER / LGA / T5 / Jewel** (to be confirmed with hard sourcing by the historian and engineer briefs): each locked a *fixed geometry* to the throughput and processing logic of its drawing-board decade — vast fixed central checkpoint halls, walking distances tuned to an assumed transfer/O&D split, retail volumes sized to an assumed dwell. When any one of those assumptions moved, the immovable geometry — hall size, walking distance, level changes — was the thing that couldn't follow.

The lesson is not "forecast better." You will forecast wrong. The lesson is: **only the choke points and their geometry are worth agonizing over, because they are the only things that both dominate the experience and cannot be changed later.** Everything downstream of them is fit-out.

## 6. Commercial model: check the arithmetic, don't inherit the assumption

The revenue architecture assumes a specific chain: *long dwell → captive audience → retail spend → non-aeronautical revenue that helps carry the building.* The thesis says processing time compresses, so dwell changes. Which direction?

Don't guess — decompose. Dwell time = (arrival-buffer time) + (processing time) + (schedule/connection slack). Faster processing removes only the middle term. If passengers *bank* that saved time as later arrival, dwell shrinks and airside retail shrinks with it. If they *spend* it airside because the anxious part (security) is now behind them earlier and more predictably, dwell could hold or even shift *airside*. The honest answer: **processing speed changes *where* the dwell sits (landside vs. airside) more reliably than *how much* there is.**

That is a built-form question, not a merchandising one. If off-airport and gate-level screening pull the sterile boundary around, the profitable, screened, captive zone moves with it. **Do not hard-wire the revenue-generating floor to a fixed post-checkpoint location** you're already planning (Section 3) to make movable. The commercial plan and the boundary plan are the same plan. Right now they're drawn by different consultants who don't talk. That's the expensive mistake hiding in plain sight.

## 7. Sustainability: this is physics, and it's the cheap-now/impossible-later item everyone underfunds

Decarbonization is not aspiration here; it's load. Concretely, by 2050 a terminal must physically host:

- **Electrified ground service equipment and electric-aircraft/APU-replacement charging at the stand.** This is a *megawatts-to-the-apron* problem. The item that is trivially cheap now and effectively impossible to retrofit is **electrical distribution capacity and conduit/duct-bank sizing** — the buried spine. You cannot re-trench a live apron. Oversize the ductbank and the substation provisioning now; it's the single highest-leverage "overbuild today" decision in the program, and it's invisible, so it's exactly what gets cut.
- **Thermal load and on-site generation.** Roof structure and plant space sized for future PV and heat-recovery, and a structural roof that can *carry* it. Cheap as intent at the structural-design stage; a demolition job later.

Feynman rule for this section: **anything that lives in the dirt or in the structural frame is the "overbuild now" list. Anything that hangs off it is the "leave soft" list.** Power spine, duct banks, apron depth, roof-load capacity, riser and shaft *sizing* → lock and oversize. The equipment those things eventually carry → buy later.

## 8. Two dated snapshots, kept concrete and bounded

**2035 — the passenger and the operator.** Mostly today's building, done well. Mobile lounges gone; Aerotrain extended; centralized checkpoint still central but faster (CT + on-person screening, less divestment). Off-airport screening exists as a pilot for a few carriers, not the norm. Biometric ID at a handful of touchpoints, not universal. Gate is still ~1:1 with the aircraft; MARS stands in use where fleet mix warrants. Dwell roughly as today. **Operator's reality:** the wins are boring and real — throughput up because the checkpoint got faster and the train reaches more gates. Nothing structural has been vindicated or refuted yet. The bets are still open.

**2050 — the passenger and the operator.** The sterile boundary has *moved* for a meaningful minority of passengers — some cleared off-airport, some at distributed gate-level pods. The central hall from 2035 has been partly *re-tenanted* into other use because screening thinned out — and this only happened cheaply **if** it was built as a clear-span room (Section 3). Stand mix has shifted with the fleet; the MARS provisioning got claimed on some stands, never on others, and the ones never claimed were cheap enough that nobody minds. The power spine is fully loaded with electric GSE and aircraft charging — and the airports that undersized their duct banks in 2027 are the ones tearing up aprons now. **Operator's reality in 2050:** the program is judged not on its roofline but on two things — *did the boundary move cheaply, and was there enough power in the ground.* Both were decided in 2027.

---

## 9. The Dulles decision: sort the forces

**DESIGN FOR NOW (build it, it's real and load-bearing today):**
- Centralized checkpoint, but as a **clear-span, re-tenantable floor plate**, not permanent structure.
- Contact gates at ~1:1, MARS where the actual fleet forecast warrants.
- Aerotrain extension and generous vertical-circulation cores sized for peak bodies.
- Kill the mobile lounges. (Physics already voted.)

**ACCOMMODATE LATER (provision now because retrofit is unaffordable; buy the equipment later):**
- **Electrical/power spine and duct-bank capacity to every stand — oversize aggressively.** #1 regret-avoidance item.
- Apron depth, pavement strength, dual-bridge and utility provisioning at stands.
- Sterile/non-sterile boundary topology that lets the membrane be pulled to the gate *or* pushed off-airport — cores, connectors, swing-gate geometry built in.
- Roof-load capacity for PV/thermal; riser and shaft *sizing*.
- Commercial floor that can *relocate* with the boundary, not welded to a fixed post-checkpoint spot.

**BET AGAINST (do not design around; treat the arrival date as unknown):**
- De-sequenced, non-1:1, "building comes to the plane" processing as the *primary* model. It failed here once on physics, not forecasting. Provision for it as an option (movable boundary); do not organize the building around it.
- Universal off-airport screening, universal biometrics, eVTOL-at-scale, full ground-handling autonomy on a *schedule.* Leave hooks, bet no concrete.

**The one sentence for the program meeting:** *Agonize only over the choke points and what's buried in the dirt and frame — those you cannot change later and they dominate everything; make every choke-point room clear-span and movable, oversize the power spine until the CFO winces, and treat every de-sequencing promise as a bet you provision for but never pour concrete on — because Dulles already poured concrete on that bet once, in 1962, and is spending $22 billion to dig it out.*

---

*Caveats on my own lens: my bottom-up method is strong on physics, geometry, and unit economics, and deliberately weak on the political and institutional forces that actually decide capital programs — bond structure, board accountability, federal funding rules, airline use-and-lease leverage. Those can override every first-principles argument above. Take the Machiavelli, CEO, and regulatory briefs as the corrective. And the failure taxonomy for IST/PKX/BER/LGA/T5/Jewel needs the historian's and engineer's hard sourcing; I've given the pattern-law, not the verified case files.*
