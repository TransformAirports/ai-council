# Virtual-Christian Brief: Dulles Already Ran This Experiment

*Stage 1 — independent lens. Informal by design. The reframe is the product; everything else props it up.*

---

## 1. The reframe

Here's the thing nobody in the room is going to say out loud, so I'll say it: **Dulles is the only airport in America that has already run the exact experiment this thesis is worried about — and lost.** In 1962 Eero Saarinen future-proofed Dulles by *de-sequencing passenger processing.* The mobile lounge was a deliberate bet against fixed concourses and against the 1:1 gate-to-aircraft relationship — park the jets out on the midfield, drive the passengers straight to the door, keep the apron flexible, and never commit the building to a rigid processing geometry. That is *precisely* the move the thesis says a 2035 terminal should make. It failed. The jet bridge and hub banking killed it, the "temporary" midfield concourses of the 1980s are still standing, and the $22 billion rebuild's single most-cheered feature is extending the AeroTrain to four new concourses so we can *finally finish deleting Saarinen's bet.* So the question in front of the design team is not the hypothetical "will a de-sequenced terminal open dated?" The honest question is: **why did Dulles's own de-sequenced terminal date, what part of it actually held for 64 years, and are we about to make the same category of mistake in the opposite direction?**

Because here's the punchline that should stop the room cold: **we are spending real money on this rebuild to undo the last time Dulles future-proofed itself.** The airport's own scar tissue is the case study. Use it.

---

## 2. Connect-the-dots arguments

### A. De-sequencing dies when the network economics don't cooperate — not when the technology fails

The mobile lounge wasn't a dumb idea. Saarinen solved three real problems with it: shorter walks, shorter taxi, flexible apron. It lost anyway — to the jet bridge and to the economics of hub-and-spoke banking. Once United needed to bank 40 connecting flights in a 90-minute window, passengers had to move plane-to-plane fast and predictably, and a fleet of 15-mph buses that you *wait* for could not do that. The building's premise assumed a processing model the airlines then refused to operate.

**That is the whole warning for distributed screening in one sentence: a built-form bet against the centralized checkpoint will die exactly the way the mobile lounge died — not because the tech doesn't exist, but because the people who operate and pay for the process won't run it the way your concrete assumes.** Before Dulles pours a distributed-screening geometry, someone needs to answer who staffs it, who funds it, and whether the airline connection bank tolerates it. If the answer is "TSA, on a budget, during a hub bank," the building will get retrofitted back to centralized — at Dulles-in-2010 prices.

### B. Labor is what dates first. Not technology. (Hospitals already learned this.)

Everyone frames obsolescence as a technology problem — biometrics, sensing, digital twins. Wrong layer. **A terminal doesn't get dated by the technology it can't fit; it gets dated by the labor model it can't afford.** Distributed screening is the cleanest example. TSA staffs roughly 4.25 officers per lane at about 200 passengers/hour design throughput. Centralize and you share those officers across a surge. Distribute screening to the gates and you multiply lanes — and you multiply the *payroll floor,* because a gate checkpoint still needs a minimum crew whether it's screening a full A350 or four early connectors. You cannot cost-decentralize a labor-heavy function. The math punishes you for spreading it out.

Hospitals ran this movie. Decentralized nursing stations — a nurse pod at every few rooms — tested beautifully in design charrettes: shorter walks to the patient, better sightlines. Then they hit nurse-to-patient ratios and staffing budgets and many hospitals quietly re-centralized, because you can't put a warm body at every pod on every shift. **The building could flex. The payroll could not.** Same lesson for Dulles: design the *shell* so screening can distribute if the labor economics ever change, but do not sink capital into a distributed geometry that assumes a staffing model TSA has never once agreed to fund.

### C. "Durable vs. volatile" done right isn't prediction — it's interstitial slack (Salk figured this out in 1965)

The success-criteria asks for a durable-vs-volatile split, and the temptation is to answer it by *forecasting* — guess 2050, build for the guess. That's the trap. The right answer is 50 years old and comes from a lab building, not an airport. Louis Kahn's Salk Institute put full-height *interstitial service floors* between the lab floors — a whole level of nothing but pipes, ducts, and access, so the volatile stuff (every lab's changing services) could be ripped out and re-run without touching the durable stuff (the structure). RIBA president Alex Gordon named the same principle in 1972: **"long life, loose fit, low energy."** The durable frame lives long; the fit-out stays loose so it can change without a demolition.

**The most future-proof thing Dulles can build is not a smart terminal — it's a dumb, generous, over-provisioned shell that makes the next three renovations cheap.** Concretely: raised floors deep enough to re-route screening and sensing, service mezzanines above the checkpoint hall, vertical shafts and electrical service sized at 2x today's load, structural bays generous enough to move a checkpoint without moving a column. That's cheap now and unaffordable later. Don't spend the money predicting 2050. Spend it making 2050 a fit-out, not a rebuild.

### D. The security membrane is the single most expensive line on the drawing — treat it as relocatable

Of everything in a terminal, the landside/airside boundary is the thing you least want to hard-wire, because it's the thing that moves when the security regime changes and the thing that costs the most to move. If screening distributes, the membrane fragments. If screening migrates off-airport (curb-to-gate, credential-on-device), the membrane *dissolves* — and suddenly your beautiful post-security retail hall is on the wrong side of a boundary that no longer exists there. LGA's rebuild already leaned the other way, pushing concessions airside on the bet that the membrane stays put and dwell lives behind it. **Nobody actually knows which direction the membrane moves over 25 years — so the one thing you must not do is pour 50-year concrete that assumes its location.** Make the security boundary a designed-to-move line: soft partitions, provisioned-but-not-committed screening zones, floor and utility grids that don't care which side of the room the checkpoint sits on. Lock the structure; leave the membrane soft.

### E. Dwell doesn't shrink — it relocates and grows, if you put the floor area where it lands (ask a casino)

The reflexive read is that faster processing means less time in the terminal means less retail. Backwards. **Every minute you take out of the security line is a minute the passenger now spends on the sterile side with a credit card — processing compression *grows* airside dwell, it doesn't kill it.** Airports are the last great captive-audience retail environment left in America, and the people who engineer captive-audience spend for a living — casinos — never shrink the floor when they speed up the transaction; they move the customer deeper into the monetizable zone faster. The risk for Dulles isn't that dwell dies. It's that we build the *landside* retail generously and starve the airside, then watch the dwell migrate behind the checkpoint to a hall we didn't size for it. Overbuild airside gross leasable area and vertical concessions; keep landside lean. The revenue architecture should be sized around where the passenger stands *after* you've compressed their processing, not before.

## 3. The operator's gut check

What I'd actually say at the bar, not in the board meeting — word for word:

> **"We're about to spend twenty-two billion dollars, and the one feature everybody's clapping for — killing the mobile lounges — is us finishing the demolition of the last time this airport tried to future-proof itself. Before we bet the building against the centralized checkpoint, somebody in that room should notice we already made that exact bet in 1962, it took fifty years and a billion and a half in AeroTrain money to walk it back, and the only part of Saarinen's design that survived was the part he *didn't* try to be clever with — the roof and the bones. Lock the bones. Stop being clever about the process. Leave the process soft."**

That's the whole brief in a bar napkin. The Saarinen main terminal is a national landmark still in daily use, expanded twice exactly the way he drew it to expand — because the *structure* was right. The mobile lounge is in a museum. The lesson isn't "don't innovate." It's "innovate in the fit-out, never in the concrete." An operator who's stood on that terminal floor knows in his gut that the durable layer is the only place genius survives contact with 25 years of TSA directives, airline mergers, and budget cycles.

## 4. Productive tangents

- **eVTOL — the cheap hedge is dirt and copper, not a vertiport.** Do not design a vertiport into the 2034 building; the timeline is a bet, not a given. Do protect the surface land rights and pull oversized electrical feeders to a plausible landing zone. If eVTOL shows up, you want it to be a tenant fit-out on reserved land with power waiting — not a structural surgery. *(Matters. This is the exact "cheap now, unaffordable later" move applied to the most over-hyped item on the list.)*

- **BER didn't fail on concrete — it failed on the volatile layer.** Berlin Brandenburg's nine-year delay was smoke-control and cabling — the systems layer, not the structure. It's the cleanest proof that terminals die in the volatile stratum, which is the whole argument for keeping that stratum accessible and loose. *(Matters. Reinforces C directly.)*

- **The Saarinen roof as an argument about what "iconic" actually buys.** The run prompt bans starchitecture brochure language, rightly. But the Dulles roof is worth one honest sentence: its value wasn't beauty, it was *designed expandability* — Saarinen drew it to be lengthened symmetrically, and it was, twice. Iconic that's also expandable is durable. Iconic that locks geometry is the trap. *(Matters — it separates good landmark from vanity landmark.)*

- **Two I chased that didn't pan out, left in on purpose.** (1) I went down the "were the mobile lounges just autonomous apron shuttles born 60 years early?" rabbit hole. Not load-bearing — but I left it in because it's a useful humility check: the mobile lounge *concept* wasn't stupid, it was un-fundable and un-ownable by the operators of its day, which is exactly the failure mode distributed screening faces. Early isn't the same as wrong, and neither one saves you. (2) I checked whether the Silver Line Metro connection changes the landside sizing math. Interesting, genuinely off-thesis for a 25-year built-form question — left in only as a flag that landside access mode is a variable someone else on the council should own.
