# Council Brief — Dieter Rams

## Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*One lens among many. I evaluate this rebuild through the eyes of the two people who have to live inside it: the passenger moving through it under time pressure, and the operator running it at 2 a.m. when something breaks. Not the architect, not the board, not the rendering.*

---

### The question is not "what should the terminal look like." It is "what are we forcing the user to do."

A terminal is not a building. It is a sequence of instructions given to a stranger who is anxious, often late, sometimes carrying a child and a bag and a boarding pass on a cracked phone. Every wall, every level change, every sign, every queue is an instruction. The thesis is correct that the danger is fixing built form around a passenger-processing *sequence*. I would put it more plainly: the danger is pouring concrete around a set of instructions, and instructions have a much shorter life than concrete.

So my contribution to this council is one discipline: **separate the building from the instructions, and make sure you have only committed structure to the things that will still be true when the instructions change.** That is not an aesthetic preference. It is respect for a user you have not met yet — the 2050 passenger — whose task you cannot fully predict.

Three of my ten principles do the work here. I will not run all ten; that would be a lecture. These three are the load-bearing ones for a $22B rebuild:

- **Good design is honest.** It does not promise the user a capability the building does not have, and it does not hide complexity that will ambush them later.
- **Good design is long-lasting.** It is not fashionable, and therefore it does not become dated.
- **Good design is as little design as possible.** Less, but better — every committed element has earned its place by serving the user's actual task.

---

### The Dulles irony: the original terminal already solved this, and the rebuild is about to un-solve it.

This is the single most important thing I can put in front of the design team, and it is specific to Dulles.

Saarinen's 1962 terminal was, in my terms, one of the most honest and least over-designed airport buildings ever built. He studied real users and found they walked an average of ~900 feet from terminal to plane. His response was radical restraint: keep the terminal a **simple, open, single volume** and decouple the gate from the building entirely. The mobile lounge carried the passenger to a **remotely parked aircraft** on a **soft, reconfigurable apron**. ([PBS American Masters](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/eero-saarinens-revolutionary-design-dulles-airport/7853/), [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/the-lonely-ballad-of-the-mobile-lounge))

Read what that actually was, structurally: Saarinen refused to fix the gate-to-aircraft relationship in built form. No fingers, no piers, no 1:1 gate geometry locked into a slab. The building was durable; the aircraft-parking logic was left deliberately soft. That is *exactly* the durable-vs-volatile split this thesis is asking for. He did it in 1958.

The rebuild proposes to retain the Saarinen headhouse as a monument, expand it 300 feet each way, and replace the mobile-lounge/remote-stand model with **conventional linear concourses and an extended AeroTrain** — precisely the fixed, sequential, 1:1 gate geometry the thesis warns will date. ([View from the Wing](https://viewfromthewing.com/dulles-airport-could-finally-kill-its-mobile-lounges-but-the-rebuild-plan-now-costs-22-billion/), [FFXnow](https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/))

I am not sentimental about mobile lounges. As a *product* they failed the user: slow, cramped, an extra transfer, 800 trips a day of accumulated friction. Killing them is correct. But notice what is being thrown out with them: the *principle* of a soft apron and a decoupled gate. The mobile lounge was a bad implementation of a good idea. The rebuild risks discarding the good idea to fix the bad implementation. **Keep the decoupling; replace the vehicle.** That distinction — bad execution vs. bad principle — is the one the program most needs to hold onto, and it is the one a "kill the mobile lounge" headline erases.

### The durable-vs-volatile framework, stated as a user-respect rule

The success criteria ask for a layered decision framework. Here is mine, and it is not a materials list. The organizing question for every element is: **when the instructions to the passenger change, does this element have to change with them?**

- **Lock now (50-year life): the box, not the flow.** Structural grid and column spacing, floor-to-floor heights, level changes, vertical-circulation and utility *spines*, apron geometry and taxilane clearances, and — critically — **conduit, power, and comms capacity provisioned at 2–3x today's load.** These are the things that are cheap to overbuild now and financially impossible to retrofit later. A column grid you regret is a 50-year regret. Overbuilt conduit is a rounding error you thank yourself for in 2040.

- **Leave soft (5–15-year life): everything that is an instruction.** Checkpoint location and count, check-in and bag-drop positions, retail fit-out, gate-hold seating, sensing and IT, the security regime itself. None of this should be load-bearing. If moving your checkpoint requires moving a shear wall, you have committed structure to an instruction. That is the cardinal error.

The honest test for the design team, applied element by element: *"If TSA changes the rules in 2032, does this wall move?"* If yes, and it is structural, you have designed dishonestly — you have promised the user a permanent solution to a temporary problem.

The single most valuable durable commitment is boring and I will say it plainly: **generous, continuous, accessible service voids — raised floors, oversized risers, walkable ceiling plenums — running the full length of the building.** Not a signature roofline. The roofline is the volatile part dressed up as the durable part. Provisioning is the durable part disguised as nothing. Spend on the invisible thing.

### Screening, gates, dwell — three user walkthroughs, not three trend forecasts

I will not inventory technology. I will describe the moment a specific user hits each transition, because that is where the design decision actually lives.

**Screening.** The centralized checkpoint exists for real reasons — regulatory chokepoint control, a defined sterile boundary, a place to concentrate scarce expert labor. It is not mere inertia. But the *user's* experience of it is the worst moment in the journey: the queue, the divestment ritual, the anxiety of being judged. TSA is already prototyping self-service, CT-based lanes and off-premises remote image review. ([TSA/DHS](https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/news/2024/03/06/tsa-and-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system), [Passenger Terminal Today](https://www.passengerterminaltoday.com/features/exclusive-feature-is-self-service-screening-the-solution-to-capacity-issues.html)) The direction of travel is distribution: smaller, self-paced screening modules closer to the gate. The design consequence is not "build distributed checkpoints now" — the regime isn't ready and betting on its date is exactly the techno-optimism this run bans. The consequence is: **do not pour a single monumental checkpoint hall that only works as one big room.** Design the screening zone as a **column-free, service-rich floor plate that can hold one large checkpoint today and be subdivided into distributed pods later without structural surgery.** Honest design here means admitting you don't know the 2040 regime and refusing to bet the structure on today's.

**Gates and apron.** The 1:1 gate-to-aircraft relationship is the most confidently-drawn and least defensible assumption in any terminal. Fleet mix is diverging — widebody retirements, single-aisle long-haul, regional, and whatever eVTOL becomes. ICN, Doha, Changi T5, and the ORD Global Terminal are all paying today for *swing* and *flexible* stands — gates that flex between narrow- and widebody, contact and remote. That flexibility has a cost: wider apron geometry, more taxilane clearance, dual boarding-bridge provisioning. **Apron geometry is a lock-now element** — you cannot widen a taxilane after you've built the concourse next to it. This is where Saarinen's instinct was right and where the rebuild must not regress: keep the apron soft, keep stands swing-capable, and accept that a rigid linear concourse sized to a 2026 fleet is a bet against a future you can already see arriving.

**Dwell and retail.** The commercial model assumes processing time compresses, so the passenger has more idle time to monetize. But watch the *actual* user: if check-in is on her phone, bag-drop is 30 seconds, and screening is distributed and fast, she arrives at the gate later and calmer — but she has *less* captive dwell in the central marketplace, not more, and she spends it where she chooses, not where the wayfinding funnels her. The honest planning question is not "how much retail" but "**is the giant central retail hall a durable revenue engine or a 2010-era assumption cast in concrete?**" Size the volume so it can lose 30% of its central retail to gate-distributed and off-airport spend without becoming a cavernous, half-empty embarrassment. Over-designing for dwell is designing for the airport's revenue model, not the user's task — a classic case of building for yourself instead of your user.

### Sustainability is not aspiration — it is a lock-now user requirement

Decarbonization is a physical demand on the building, not a values statement. Electrified GSE needs apron-edge power at a scale today's aprons don't carry. SAF logistics, on-site generation, and thermal loads all demand distribution capacity and plant space. Every one of these is cheap to provision as *empty capacity and routed conduit* during a ground-up rebuild and brutally expensive to retrofit into an occupied terminal. This belongs firmly in the **lock-now** column, for the same reason as comms conduit: the retrofit is unaffordable, so the honest move is to overbuild the invisible capacity now. The operator in 2050 either has the risers and the apron power or spends a decade and a fortune cutting them in.

### Two dated snapshots — the user's actual moment

**2035, passenger.** She checks in and drops her bag at a kiosk cluster that used to be a check-in hall; two-thirds of that hall is now something else, because it was fit-out, not structure. Screening is still mostly central but faster and self-paced. The building feels emptier and calmer than the renderings promised — which is a *success*, though the retail P&L may not agree. The parts that annoy her are the parts that were locked too hard: a checkpoint hall sized for a queue that no longer forms, a walk to the gate set by a concourse geometry drawn in 2026.

**2035, operator.** He is grateful for every service void and spare riser and cursing every place the design committed structure to a 2026 instruction. His recurring problem is not capacity; it is that a wall he needs to move is holding up the roof.

**2050, passenger.** Processing has migrated onto her device and off the airport. She experiences the terminal as a *place to be*, briefly, before boarding — not a machine to be processed by. The building's value is now almost entirely in its durable bones: daylight, clear sightlines, short honest paths, and the apron flexibility that let the airport keep matching gates to a fleet nobody in 2026 predicted. Nothing about the fixed sequence survives; everything about the good box does.

**2050, operator.** The technology has been swapped three times. The screening regime has changed twice. The one thing he never had to touch was the structure — *if* the 2026 team drew the line in the right place. Whether he blesses or curses them was decided in a 2027 program meeting about column grids and conduit, not about the roof.

---

### The Dulles decision — sorted

**Design FOR now:** a single large central checkpoint (the regime demands it); conventional bag systems; today's fleet's contact stands. Serve the real 2026 user; don't martyr her to a 2050 guess.

**Accommodate LATER (build soft, provision generously):** distributed/gate-level screening (column-free, subdividable screening floor); off-airport and on-device processing (shrinkable check-in/bag halls as fit-out, never structure); dwell/retail that can contract 30% without looking abandoned; comms/power/thermal capacity at 2–3x, routed in continuous accessible service spines.

**Explicitly bet AGAINST:** the permanence of the 1:1 gate-to-aircraft relationship; the central retail hall as a growing revenue engine; the centralized checkpoint as a 50-year fixture; eVTOL-at-scale and universal biometrics on any known date. Do not pour structure around any of them.

**The one move I would fight for in the program meeting:** preserve Saarinen's actual innovation — a **soft, swing-capable apron and a decoupled gate** — even while killing the mobile lounge that implemented it badly. Retaining the headhouse as a monument while discarding the principle that made it great would be the deepest, most expensive irony in the whole $22B program.

---

### What my lens misses — in honesty

User experience is necessary but not sufficient. I optimize for the passenger's and operator's clarity; I under-weight three things this council must supply. **Financing and airline use-agreements** (the airport-ceo's ground) can force a rigid concourse for reasons that have nothing to do with the user and everything to do with bond covenants and gate leases. **Regulatory reality** (the security and regulatory agents) may make distributed screening undeliverable for longer than my optimism assumes — I should not wave away the sterile-boundary problem. And **competitive position** may make a signature roofline worth building for reasons of catchment and carrier attraction that my "as little design as possible" instinct discounts. A beautifully honest terminal in a losing hub is still a losing terminal. Weigh my brief accordingly: I am the argument for restraint and provisioning, not the whole decision.

*Less, but better. In a terminal, "better" means the user — passenger and operator, 2035 and 2050 — never has to fight the building to do the simple thing they came to do.*
