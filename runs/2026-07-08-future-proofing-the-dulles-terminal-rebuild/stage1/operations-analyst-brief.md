# Operations-Analyst Brief: Where the Terminal Actually Binds

**Run:** Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild
**Lens:** Airport operational performance — throughput, screening, gate/apron utilization, turnaround, and the durable-vs-volatile split seen from the ramp and the checkpoint, not the drafting table.
**Prepared independently.** No other Stage 1 brief was read.

---

## The operational question underneath the design question

The thesis asks what built form still holds when screening distributes, the gate-to-aircraft relationship stops being 1:1, and processing migrates off-airport. My job is narrower and more testable: **which of the terminal's performance constraints are physics, and which are coordination?** Physics you must build for now, because retrofit is unaffordable. Coordination you should leave soft, because the industry keeps discovering it can move the same number of people through the same concrete by changing software, sequencing, and rules — not steel.

The evidence is unusually one-sided on this point. Over the last decade the largest measured capacity gains in US and European aviation came from re-rules and re-sequencing, not new pavement. That is the single most useful fact a Dulles capital planner can carry into a program meeting: **the building is rarely the binding constraint until you have exhausted the coordination levers — and most airports have not.** But the counter-case is real and specific, and I treat it seriously below: apron geometry, walking distance, and baggage topology are physics, and when they are wrong no amount of coordination saves you.

---

## 1. Key findings (each numerically grounded)

1. **Re-sequencing beat construction, repeatedly.** FAA wake-turbulence recategorization (RECAT) raised Memphis airport capacity roughly **20%, allowing up to 22 additional arrivals per hour**, by reclassifying aircraft separation — zero new runway [Source: https://www.mobilityengineeringtech.com/component/content/article/25559-recat-wake-turbulence-recategorization]. This is throughput bought with a rule change.

2. **Airport CDM cut taxi-out time 10–20%** across European airports on average, per EUROCONTROL — a coordination protocol, not a taxiway [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making]. For 17 CDM airports EUROCONTROL counts ~34,000 tonnes of fuel and ~250,000 minutes of flow-management delay saved per year.

3. **Gates are chronically under-worked.** "Full" gate utilization is commonly cited at **6–8 turns per gate per day**, yet Delta's hubs ran **2.9 (MEM) to 6.3 (ATL)** daily turns per gate while Southwest's focus cities ran **7.2 (PHX) to 10.3 (BWI)** [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. The spread is operational strategy, not building capability. Latent gate capacity at a legacy hub is often 30–50%.

4. **The checkpoint is getting faster on the same footprint.** TSA's CT scanners let passengers keep laptops and liquids in bags and have cut wait time by **as much as 30%** at some airports; TSA holds PreCheck to **under 10 minutes** and standard lanes **under 30** [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/]. Screening throughput is migrating from a spatial problem to a technology-and-staffing problem.

5. **Turnaround variance, not turnaround time, is the hidden tax.** A full A320 turn runs **35–45 minutes** (Airbus), but even top-quartile short-haul carriers average **8 minutes of ground delay per turn**, and observed schedule variance runs to **~19% (JetBlue, ~12 min)** [Source: https://simpleflying.com/narrowbody-turnaround-times-time-differences-guide/]. Variance is what forces you to overbuild gates — buffer, not demand.

6. **The 1:1 gate–aircraft relationship is already dissolving where airports chose to dissolve it.** MARS stands let one position serve either one widebody or two narrowbodies (Code C or smaller), and Frankfurt deliberately used remote stands to add capacity without lengthening the terminal [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. Flexibility is a design choice with a known cost, not a technology waiting to be invented.

7. **Coordination has limits, and they are physical.** Heathrow T5's opening lost **~42,000 bags** in days — a baggage-topology and staffing failure inside a brand-new building [Source: https://www.mightytravels.com/2024/06/heathrow-terminal-5-revisited-lessons-learned-from-an-operational-nightmare/]. Istanbul's geometry imposes **20–30 minute walks** to the farthest gates that no scheduling can shorten [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/]. Where the physics is wrong, operations cannot rescue it — and that is precisely what Dulles must get right now.

## 2. Evidence

### A. The bottleneck is usually coordination, and the proof is that re-rules keep unlocking capacity

The cleanest natural experiments in aviation capacity are the ones where nothing physical changed. RECAT is the marquee case: by reclassifying wake-turbulence separation by wing design and approach speed rather than weight alone, the FAA let aircraft fly closer. At Memphis this produced a **~20% capacity boost and up to 22 more arrivals per hour**; FedEx aircraft separations dropped from 4 nautical miles to 2.5–3.5, worth **14 extra aircraft per hour**, and the program trimmed roughly **3 minutes of average taxi time** [Source: https://www.mobilityengineeringtech.com/component/content/article/25559-recat-wake-turbulence-recategorization]. No runway, no taxiway, no terminal. A rule.

Airport CDM is the same story on the surface. EUROCONTROL's assessment credits A-CDM with a **10–20% reduction in taxi-out time** and measurable falls in late stand/gate changes and flow-management delay, with higher peak departure rates [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making]. The mechanism is information-sharing — a shared, accurate off-block time so the tower, ramp, and airlines stop guessing. It is close to free relative to a concrete program, and it directly reduces the gate-occupancy time that drives how many gates you need.

The lesson for Dulles is not "buy A-CDM." It is that **a large fraction of what looks like a capacity shortfall is coordination debt**, and coordination debt is cheap to service relative to a $22B build. The building should be sized for the demand that survives after the coordination levers are pulled — not for today's uncoordinated peak.

### B. Gate and apron: the hidden capacity is enormous, and the 1:1 relationship is a choice

Dulles today has **139 gates, 123 with jetways and 16 hardstands**, serving ~29 million passengers in 2025, with a master-plan ambition toward **90 million** [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport; https://www.ffxnow.com/2025/07/17/new-master-plan-for-dulles-airport-envisions-growth-to-90-million-annual-passengers/]. The instinct is to triple gates. The utilization data says look harder first.

"Full" utilization is **6–8 turns per gate per day**, and low-cost operators routinely clear that: Southwest hit **10.3 turns at BWI** [Source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/assessing-us-gate-utilization/14707011]. A United regional operation running **2.9–6.3** turns is leaving a large multiple of throughput on the table — much of it recoverable through common-use gating, tighter turn discipline, and the coordination levers above. FLL codifies "full" as 6 departures / 600 seats per gate per day; PHL's 2024 competition plan sets a 5-turn / 625-seat minimum-use bar [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-gate-management]. These thresholds exist because regulators know gates get hoarded and under-worked.

On the apron, the 1:1 gate–aircraft relationship is already optional. MARS stands serve **one widebody or two narrowbodies** from a single position, and remote/hardstand operations let Frankfurt add capacity without extending the pier [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/; https://gadallon.substack.com/p/remote-stands-bus-boarding-and-last]. The trade is explicit and known: remote stands are cheaper for the airport (lower parking fees, no jet bridge) but cost the passenger bus time and cost the operation a longer, more variable turn [Source: https://simpleflying.com/jet-bridges-vs-remote-stands/]. This is a durable design lever — **the apron geometry and taxilane clearances that make MARS and remote-stand swings possible are 50-year physics; the decision to use them on any given day is soft.** Provision the geometry now; leave the operating mode adjustable.

### C. Screening: is the centralized checkpoint durable, or transitional?

This is the load-bearing durability question, and the honest answer is **"structurally durable for ~15 years, spatially shrinking, and regime-volatile."**

Two forces run in opposite directions. First, throughput per square foot is rising: CT scanners cut wait times by up to 30% and remove the divest-and-repack ritual [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/]. Second, the divestiture is starting to move to the passenger: TSA's self-service "Innovation Checkpoint" prototype at Harry Reid (LAS) lets PreCheck passengers self-screen at their own pace with minimal officer involvement, explicitly to let travelers self-resolve alarms [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]. Self-service screening decouples throughput from officer headcount — the classic precondition for distributing a function that used to require a central, staffed hall.

But note what has *not* happened: CT machines are **larger and heavier** than the legacy X-ray they replace, and TSA reports a new bottleneck of passengers waiting for bags because bodies now clear faster than bags [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/news/tsa-ct-security-scanners/]. A distributed, gate-level checkpoint has to carry that machine footprint, the divest/recompose queue, and a secure sterile boundary to every satellite — multiplying the hardest, heaviest equipment across the building rather than concentrating it.

The operational read for Dulles: **do not hard-wire the checkpoint hall's walls, but do provision for both futures.** Reserve structural bays, floor loading, power, and secure-side/non-secure-side boundary flexibility so the checkpoint can shrink (as CT + self-service raise per-lane rates) or partially distribute (if regulation ever permits gate-level screening) without moving columns. The checkpoint's *location and boundary logic* is durable; its *lane count and machine mix* is a 5–10-year fit-out. Lock the first, soften the second.

### D. Turnaround: variance is the thing you actually build for

Planners size gates for peak simultaneous occupancy, and occupancy is turn time plus its variance. Turn time is well-behaved: **35–45 minutes for a full A320** (Airbus); LCCs push 25 [Source: https://simpleflying.com/narrowbody-turnaround-times-time-differences-guide/]. Variance is the problem. Even the top 75th-percentile short-haul carriers average **8 minutes of ground delay per turn**, and observed variance reaches **~19%** [Source: https://simpleflying.com/narrowbody-turnaround-times-time-differences-guide/]. Late passengers and slow bag loading dominate the causes — again, coordination, not concrete.

Every minute of variance you fail to squeeze out is a fraction of a gate you have to build as buffer. This is where A-CDM pays a second dividend: fewer late stand/gate changes and better ground-handling resource use directly compress the buffer [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/airport-collaborative-decision-making]. For Dulles, the framework move is to **treat gate count as a function of achievable turn variance, and to invest in the operational systems that shrink variance before committing to gate quantity.** Otherwise the building permanently pays for the ramp's bad days.

### E. Off-airport and de-sequenced processing: real, but bounded by bags and law

The de-sequencing thesis is strongest on the landside. Biometric bag drop can check a bag in under 10 seconds; home/RFID bag tags and curb-to-gate biometric verification are deployed or piloted, and IATA-cited surveys have most airlines and airports targeting touchless, unassisted bag drop [Source: https://www.beumergroup.com/knowledge/airport/biometric-bag-ids-a-future-without-tags/; https://www.hidglobal.com/solutions/air-travel-passenger-processing]. Check-in as a fixed counter row is genuinely dying; that hall can shrink and the queue can move to the passenger's phone.

The bounding constraint is the bag, not the boarding pass. Even fully de-sequenced identity processing still has to inject a physical bag into a secure, screened baggage stream somewhere, and that injection point plus the inline EDS baggage system is heavy, spatial infrastructure with a multi-decade life. So the durable commitment is **the baggage handling topology and its screening injection points; the volatile layer is the check-in fit-out and the identity software.** Overbuild bag-system capacity and routing flexibility now; build check-in halls that can be repurposed to retail, lounge, or dwell as counters vanish.

## 3. Counterexamples: where operations could NOT solve it and infrastructure was the right answer

Intellectual honesty requires naming the cases where the coordination-first instinct fails. There are four clean ones, and each maps to a Dulles decision.

1. **Walking distance is physics.** Istanbul's terminal imposes **20–30 minute walks** to distant gates; Beijing Daxing's radial "starfish" caps the farthest gate at **under 8 minutes / 600 meters** [Source: https://upgradedpoints.com/travel/airports/istanbul-airport-ist-terminal-guide/; https://www.designboom.com/architecture/zaha-hadid-daxing-international-airport-beijing-09-26-2019/]. No scheduling app closes that gap. Concourse length, pier geometry, and the placement of vertical circulation are locked on day one and cannot be coordinated away. Dulles's AeroTrain extension and concourse layout are exactly this kind of irreversible physics — get the geometry right, because operations cannot fix a long walk.

2. **Baggage topology is physics.** Heathrow T5 was a new building with a modern system and still lost **~42,000 bags** at opening because the baggage system, staffing, and testing were not coordinated — but the deeper lesson is that once the *conveyor topology and sortation geometry* are poured, you live with them for decades [Source: https://www.mightytravels.com/2024/06/heathrow-terminal-5-revisited-lessons-learned-from-an-operational-nightmare/]. Under-provisioning bag-system capacity or flexibility is an infrastructure error operations cannot undo.

3. **The curb and roadway are physics.** Kansas City's new single terminal (2023) traded gate proximity for a better building — and its **pickup roadway backed up to the highway** because curb and cell-phone-lot capacity were mismatched to demand [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-03-12/pickups-at-kansas-citys-new-airport-terminal-are-a-nightmare-heres-how-officials-say-to-fix-it]. Landside roadway and curb geometry are among the least reversible and most under-forecast commitments in any terminal program.

4. **Some capacity really does require pavement.** RECAT and A-CDM buy 10–20% at the margin; they do not manufacture a runway or a taxiway where geometry constrains simultaneous movements. When an airport is genuinely airfield-constrained at the peak after coordination is exhausted, infrastructure is the correct answer. The discipline is *sequence*: pull the coordination levers first so you build the right, smaller amount of the expensive thing.

The through-line: **the durable, un-coordinatable commitments are geometric — walking distance, apron/taxilane clearance, baggage topology, curb/roadway.** These are where Dulles must overbuild now, because retrofit is either impossible or ruinous. The volatile commitments are the ones software and rules keep improving — checkpoint lane count, gate assignment, turn discipline, check-in fit-out. Those should be left deliberately soft.

## 5. The durable-vs-volatile split, from the operations chair

| Layer | Life | Verdict for Dulles |
|---|---|---|
| Apron/taxilane geometry, MARS clearances, stand adjacency | 50 yr | **Lock & overbuild.** Provision widebody/narrowbody swing and remote-stand access; retrofit is unaffordable. |
| Concourse length, vertical-circulation placement, walking distance | 50 yr | **Lock.** Radial/compact geometry (Daxing model) beats sprawl; operations cannot shorten a walk. |
| Baggage topology + inline screening injection points, floor loading | 30–50 yr | **Overbuild capacity & routing flexibility.** The bag is the binding constraint on de-sequencing. |
| Curb/roadway/cell-lot capacity | 30–50 yr | **Overbuild.** Most under-forecast, least reversible; KCI is the warning. |
| Checkpoint hall location, secure/non-secure boundary, structural bays, power/conduit | 50 yr structure / soft interior | **Lock location & boundary logic; leave lane count and machine mix soft.** |
| Checkpoint lane count & machine mix (CT, self-service) | 5–15 yr | **Soft.** Regime and technology will change; do not pour walls around today's lane math. |
| Gate count | Function of turn variance | **Size to achievable variance, not today's uncoordinated peak.** |
| Check-in hall / counters | 5–15 yr | **Soft.** Design for repurposing to dwell/retail as counters vanish. |
| IT, sensing, identity software, gate-assignment logic | 3–10 yr | **Soft by default.** Provision conduit/power/comms generously; change the electronics cheaply. |

## 6. Two dated operational snapshots

**2035 — the operator's day.** Check-in counters are half gone; most passengers cleared identity on their phone and dropped bags at a biometric elevator in under a minute. The central checkpoint still exists but runs fewer, faster CT + self-service lanes at higher throughput per square foot, so the hall is smaller than it was drawn in 2026. A-CDM-class coordination has squeezed turn variance, so gates run closer to 7–8 productive turns. MARS-capable stands flex daily between one widebody and two regional jets as United's fleet mix shifts. The bag system, not the checkpoint, is the peak-hour constraint. **The building feels efficient because the volatile layers were left soft and got upgraded twice already.**

**2050 — the operator's day.** Screening may be partially distributed to secure sub-zones, and the central hall the 2026 team refused to hard-wire has been re-partitioned twice. Some gates now serve mixed fleets — single-aisle long-haul, regional, and whatever the small-aircraft/eVTOL question resolves into — off the same apron geometry, because the clearances were poured wide in 2028. Check-in halls became dwell, lounge, and revenue space as processing left the building. What still constrains the airport in 2050 is exactly what constrained it in 2026: **apron geometry, walking distance, baggage topology, and the curb.** The airports that dated badly are the ones that locked the volatile layer and skimped on the durable geometry. Dulles's job is to invert that.

## 7. The Dulles decision — operational moves for the program meeting

**Design FOR now:** central checkpoint (regulatory and level-of-service reality; distributed gate-level screening is not sanctioned and multiplies heavy equipment); contact-gate majority for the hub's connecting complex; inline hold-baggage screening.

**Accommodate LATER (build the geometry, leave the mode soft):** MARS/swing-stand clearances and remote-stand access on the apron; checkpoint structural bays, floor loading, and boundary flexibility for a shrinking or partly distributing checkpoint; check-in halls convertible to dwell/retail; generous conduit/power/comms spines so the electronic layer changes cheaply for 50 years.

**Explicitly bet AGAINST:** sizing the building for today's uncoordinated peak (pull coordination levers first); a fixed 1:1 gate–aircraft apron; permanent, hard-walled check-in counter rows; and — the recurring failure mode — under-provisioning the un-coordinatable geometry (walking distance, baggage topology, curb) to fund a signature interior.

The single sentence for the room: **spend on the geometry operations can never fix, and stay cheap and soft on everything a rule change or a software release keeps improving — because for a decade the rule changes and software releases have been winning.**
