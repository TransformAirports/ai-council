# Munger Brief — Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*An independent lens for the Transform Airports AI Council. My job is not to forecast 2050. Nobody in the room can, and pretending otherwise is the first error. My job is to invert the problem, name the mental models that actually govern the outcome, and price the cost of being wrong in each direction. The durable-versus-volatile split the run asks for is not, at bottom, an engineering question. It is an optionality-pricing question dressed in concrete.*

---

## 1. Invert first: the recipe to guarantee Dulles opens dated

Instead of asking "how do we future-proof a $22B terminal," ask the Munger question: **what would we have to do to guarantee it opens functionally obsolete?** If the current program resembles the recipe, that is the alarm.

The recipe for a dated mega-terminal, drawn from the actual record:

1. **Encode a specific passenger-processing topology into fixed geometry.** Size the checkpoint hall, the column grid over it, and the gate-hold geometry around today's curb→check-in→central-screening→gate sequence. (Kansas City did this. See §3.)
2. **Bet the building on one airside-movement technology.** Pour irreversible infrastructure — tunnels, fixed guideways — around a single conveyance whose lifespan is shorter than the concrete's. (Dulles already did this once. The mobile lounge.)
3. **Fix the gate–aircraft relationship at 1:1 with a single fleet in mind.** Size stands to the anchor carrier's current fleet plan, so a shift in aircraft mix strands apron geometry you can never re-pour.
4. **Size the building around a commercial model — dwell and retail volume — that assumes processing time stays long.** Then watch processing compress and leave you heating and cooling volume no revenue supports.
5. **Solve an aesthetic problem with an irreversible structural commitment.** (Berlin: a clean glass ceiling required a smoke-extraction system with no precedent on earth. It cost them a decade.)
6. **Spend the flexibility budget on the parts that are cheap to change later, and starve the parts that are impossible to change later.** Under-provision conduit, riser, apron depth, and structural reserve — the 50-year layer — to fund fit-out and IT, the 5-year layer.
7. **Let the schedule and the bond math pick the throughput number,** then let the throughput number pick the geometry, so the building is optimized for a forecast rather than for a range.

Now score the Dulles program against this list. From the public record: a preserved Saarinen head-house, a 300-ft east/west extension, four new linear concourses, deep subterranean AeroTrain circulation replacing the mobile lounges, ~$22B, targeting 2034. Two items on the recipe are live risks worth naming out loud:

- **Item 2 is the one to watch.** Killing the mobile lounge and committing to deep-bored AeroTrain tunnels trades one irreversible movement bet for another. Tunnels are the single most un-retrofittable object in the whole program. That is not automatically wrong — but it means the margin of safety must be spent *there*, on tunnel and station envelope, more than anywhere else in the building. (Detail in §4.)
- **Item 1 is the thesis's own trap.** The seductive move is to design Dulles *around* distributed screening arriving. If it doesn't — and TSA certification inertia is a real force, not mere habit — you have built the Kansas City mistake in mirror image: a building optimized for a screening topology that never came. The honest answer is not "bet on decentralization." It is "buy optionality on the direction and bet on neither." (§5.)

## 2. Incentives — why optionality is an orphan in the capital-planning room

Munger's first law: **show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome.** Never ask what the parties around the program table believe about 2050. Ask what they are paid to deliver.

- **The bond underwriters** need a throughput number to size ~$22B of debt. A range does not price a bond; a point estimate does. Their incentive pushes the whole design toward a single forecast the geometry then hardens around.
- **The design architect's** reputation attaches to a finished, photographable form. Optionality — deliberately soft, unfinished, over-provisioned volume — is invisible in a rendering and wins no awards. The incentive runs against leaving things loose.
- **The anchor carrier (United)** wants its gates guaranteed to its *current* fleet and hub plan now, in the use-and-lease agreement. Its incentive is to lock the gate count and stand mix to the fleet it flies today — precisely the 1:1, single-fleet commitment (recipe item 3) that dates fastest.
- **The political sponsors** are rewarded for a ribbon-cutting inside an electoral horizon, not for a building that ages gracefully across 2050. "First-of-a-kind airport" is a ribbon-cutting phrase.

Notice what's missing: **no one in that room is paid to preserve flexibility.** Every strong incentive pushes toward locking geometry early and hard. Optionality has no natural constituency. That is the structural reason mega-terminals open dated — not failure of foresight, but an incentive field with no defender of "leave it soft."

**The move this implies for MWAA:** somebody at the table must be explicitly chartered and rewarded to own the flexibility budget — a named owner of the durable-vs-volatile split with veto power over decisions that harden geometry to fund fit-out. If optionality is nobody's job, the recipe wins by default.

## 3. Base rates — the failure taxonomy is the most useful evidence you have

The run asks for a failure taxonomy. To a Munger, this *is* the analysis: a mega-terminal that opens dated is not a freak event, it is the base rate. Here is the single design decision that dated fastest in each, and the model it teaches:

| Terminal | The decision that dated fastest | The lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **MCI (Kansas City, 1972→demolished 2023)** | "Drive to your gate" horseshoe geometry with tiny gate-level security pens, designed around the pre-hijacking-era screening regime. | **The killer case.** They encoded a *screening topology* into the building's geometry. One regulatory event — post-9/11 centralized checkpoints — made the whole form unworkable. A building whose geometry hard-codes a screening model is fragile to a regime change nobody can forecast. |
| **Dulles itself (mobile lounge, 1962)** | Betting airside transport on a single bespoke vehicle technology. | Saarinen's own future-proofing bet dated. MWAA is now spending billions to undo it. Read this before pouring the next irreversible movement commitment. |
| **BER (Berlin, opened 2020, 14 yrs late)** | Downward smoke extraction with no world precedent, chosen to keep a clean glass ceiling. | An *aesthetic* goal solved by an *irreversible, unprecedented* structural system. Solving beauty with permanence is how you buy a decade of delay. |
| **IST (Istanbul, 2018)** | World's largest terminal under one roof with no internal train; 20–30 min gate walks. | Sized for headline throughput, not for the human it processes. Scale without an internal movement system is a dated decision on day one. |
| **PKX (Beijing Daxing, 2019)** | A radial "starfish" optimized to minimize walking, sited 46 km from the city, sized for ~72M. | Handled ~49M in 2024 — well under design. Geometry optimized for a demand curve and a catchment that didn't materialize on schedule. |
| **LHR T5 (2008)** | A "tried and tested" integrated baggage system launched at full scale on day one. | 28,000 bags stranded in a week. The *volatile* layer (IT/baggage) failed catastrophically because it was commissioned as if it were the *durable* layer. Never big-bang the soft systems. |
| **LGA (2016–22 rebuild)** | Beautiful new terminals on the same land-locked footprint; apron/taxiway geometry still constrained (no room to fix taxi paths). | You can gold-plate the *volatile* fit-out and still be strangled by the *durable* airside geometry you couldn't move. Proves the split is real and the durable layer is the one that bites. |
| **Jewel Changi (2019)** | A vast retail/dwell mall as the commercial anchor. | Worked — *because* Changi is a destination transfer hub with long dwell. The lesson is the opposite of "copy it": the dwell-retail bet is contingent on a dwell profile Dulles must verify it actually has before sizing volume around it (§4). |

The pattern across all eight: **the decision that dated fastest was almost always in the durable, irreversible layer — geometry, apron, tunnels, structural commitments to a specific topology — not in the fit-out everyone worries about.** The volatile layer (T5's baggage) fails loudly but is fixable. The durable layer (MCI's geometry, LGA's apron) fails quietly and is fatal. This inverts the usual anxiety. Executives fret about picking the right biometrics vendor. The base rate says: fret about the concrete that encodes an assumption.

## 4. Margin of safety, reframed as a regret-asymmetry test

The run wants a decision framework separating "lock now" from "leave soft," and asks where to overbuild. Munger's tool for this is **margin of safety** — but the operational form here is a **regret-asymmetry test**, applied decision by decision:

> For each design element, ask two questions. *If I lock this and I'm wrong, what does it cost to undo?* And *if I leave this soft and I'm wrong, what does it cost to add?* Spend the flexibility budget wherever the asymmetry is worst — where getting it wrong by locking, or by under-provisioning, is catastrophic and irreversible, while the cost of generosity today is merely annoying.

This produces a clean sort:

**Overbuild now — retrofit is unaffordable or impossible (high regret asymmetry):**
- **AeroTrain tunnel and station envelope.** You can never re-bore a tunnel or enlarge an underground station box once the building sits on it. Size the bore, the station platforms, and the vertical shafts for more throughput and more/other vehicle technologies than the current plan assumes. The *vehicle* is volatile and swappable; the *hole in the ground* is forever. This is where the mobile-lounge lesson (§3) must be paid forward.
- **Apron depth and taxiway geometry** behind the linear concourses. LGA proves you cannot buy this back later. Provision for larger stands, dual taxi-lanes, and remote-stand growth even if the current fleet plan doesn't need it.
- **Structural grid and floor-to-floor / level-change generosity** over the processing hall, so the checkpoint volume can be re-partitioned for centralized *or* distributed screening without moving columns or slabs.
- **Conduit, riser, power, and comms provisioning, and electrical service capacity** for electrified GSE, on-site generation, and thermal load. These are cheap as empty space today and brutally expensive to chase through a finished building. Decarbonization is a hard physical constraint on a building meant to run in 2050, not an aspiration — and the cheapest moment to provision for it is before the slab is poured.

**Leave deliberately soft — cheap to change, expensive to guess wrong (low regret asymmetry):**
- Checkpoint equipment, sensing, biometrics, all IT and security-regime fit-out.
- Retail/concession fit-out and its partition walls.
- Gate-hold furniture and boarding-door configuration.
- The AeroTrain *rolling stock and control system* (as distinct from the tunnel).

**The one line to hand the design team:** overbuild the hole, the grid, and the conduit; starve nothing structural to pay for anything you can rip out in fifteen years. The base rate in §3 says the durable layer is the one that dates fatally, so that is where the margin of safety belongs.

## 5. Circle of competence + second-order effects — don't bet the building on a 2050 forecast

**Circle of competence:** be honest that *nobody* can forecast the 2050 screening regime, the gate–aircraft ratio, or whether processing migrates to the phone. Those are outside every planner's circle, including this council's. The disciplined response to "outside my circle" is not to pick a side more confidently — it is to *refuse the bet and buy the optionality instead.* Design the processing hall as a structurally generous, soft-partitioned volume that can be run centralized *or* distributed, and commit to neither. This is intellectually honest about the counter-argument the thesis under-weights: centralized screening exists for real regulatory, security, and level-of-service reasons, and the regulator, not the architect, controls the migration date.

**Second-order effects** are where the thesis's own logic must be followed to its uncomfortable end. *If* processing time compresses and journeys de-sequence — the thesis's premise — then:
1. Dwell time falls, so the retail/dwell revenue the building is sized around shrinks or relocates landside/off-airport.
2. Which means terminal *volume* built for long-dwell commercial revenue becomes stranded space you heat, cool, clean, and depreciate with no offsetting revenue.
3. Which means the commercial pro-forma the bond was sized against is the thing most exposed to the very decentralization the thesis predicts.

That is the trap Munger would flag hardest: **the same decentralization thesis that justifies the rebuild also undermines the retail-volume assumption used to finance it.** You cannot bank the processing-compression story to justify the design and simultaneously bank the long-dwell-retail story to justify the volume. Pick a lane, or — better — size the commercial volume conservatively and treat upside dwell as optional, convertible space, not committed program.

## 6. The Dulles decision — sorted by inversion

Per the run's final ask, here is the FOR-now / LATER / AGAINST sort, derived from the models above:

**Design FOR now (lock it, because the regret of getting it wrong later is catastrophic):**
- Generous, oversized AeroTrain tunnel/station *envelope* — the irreversible hole.
- Apron depth, dual taxi-lanes, and remote-stand growth behind the concourses.
- Structural grid, level changes, and vertical-circulation spines sized for reconfiguration.
- Deep electrical/thermal service capacity and empty conduit/riser for decarbonization and unknown IT.
- A named, empowered owner of the durable-vs-volatile split (the incentive fix from §2).

**Accommodate LATER (build soft, reversible, convertible):**
- The screening hall's internal topology — centralized today, re-partitionable to distributed.
- All security, sensing, biometric, and IT fit-out (and commission it incrementally, never T5-style big-bang).
- Gate-hold and boarding configuration for a de-coupling gate–aircraft ratio.
- Retail/concession volume treated as convertible space, not committed revenue program.
- AeroTrain vehicle technology and control system, swappable within the fixed envelope.

**Explicitly bet AGAINST (do not hard-code the geometry to these):**
- eVTOL-at-scale, universal biometrics, and full ground-handling autonomy on any known date. Provision conduit and structural reserve so they *can* land; encode none of them into fixed form.
- A single anchor-fleet 1:1 gate plan. Do not let the use-and-lease agreement pour the apron.
- A long-dwell retail revenue model as a load-bearing financing assumption.
- The premise that centralized screening is *only* inertia. It is also regulation you don't control.

## 7. Where my lens is weak — read this against the specialists

Honesty per my own method. My contribution is breadth and inversion; it is deliberately shallow on depth. Three cautions:

- My durable/volatile split and regret-asymmetry test are **frameworks, not quantities.** The chief-engineer and infrastructure-economist must put real numbers on "how much oversized tunnel costs today vs. retrofit," because margin of safety is only as good as the price you pay for it. Overbuilding the wrong things is also a way to open dated — and broke.
- I pattern-match to base rates. If Dulles is genuinely a novel case in some dimension (Karpathy's warning that some things resist historical analogy), my failure taxonomy will mislead by false analogy. The mobile-lounge and MCI parallels are strong; test them, don't assume them.
- I've treated incentives as near-deterministic. They usually are. But a determined, well-chartered flexibility owner can beat the incentive field — the regulatory and political analysts should judge whether MWAA's governance can actually sustain that role across a decade-long, multi-administration build.

**The single sentence I'd want to survive this brief:** the mega-terminals that opened dated did not fail at forecasting technology — they failed at pricing the cost of being wrong, and they spent their flexibility budget on the layers that were cheap to change while hard-coding the layers that were impossible to undo. Invert that, and Dulles has its answer.
