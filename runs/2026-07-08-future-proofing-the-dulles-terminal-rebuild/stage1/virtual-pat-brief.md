# Virtual Pat — The MacGyver Brief: Future-Proofing the Dulles Rebuild

*Independent Stage 1 research brief. Lens: what the property already owns, and the cheapest defensible way to hedge a 2035–2050 terminal against a processing sequence that is decentralizing under it.*

The dominant Council instinct on a $22B program is to answer every uncertainty with more program — another concourse, another system, another nine-figure line item. My job is to hold the other door open. The Dulles thesis is really a hedging problem: you must lock structure for 50 years while the passenger-processing sequence has a 10-year half-life. The good news is that the airport already owns most of the assets a smart hedge would use. It just doesn't think of them as hedges. It thinks of them as legacy nuisances or transit lines. Reframing what's on the property is worth more here than any new sensor.

---

## 1. The asset inventory read — what's already on the property that matters

- **A fleet of 49 mobile lounges and plane-mates, 30 of which physically dock to any aircraft anywhere on the field — and MWAA just committed up to $160M to give them another ~20 years of life.** This is the single most misread asset at Dulles. Everyone treats it as an embarrassing relic. It is, functionally, a *gate-decoupling system that already works*: it moves a planeload of people from any point in the terminal to any aircraft parking position, with no fixed 1:1 gate-to-aircraft geometry. The thesis frets that the gate–aircraft relationship "stops being 1:1." Dulles was *born* non-1:1 and is paying to keep the hardware. [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2025/09/22/dulles-mobile-lounges-could-last-another-two-decades-airport-officials-say/]
- **A direct heavy-rail line into the terminal — the Silver Line — with an indoor moving-walkway tunnel that lands you at the ticketing/bag-claim levels, and it's already the busiest of the six new stations (~1M+ riders in year one).** That is a fully built, publicly-funded off-airport processing node that today does nothing but deposit bodies. [Source: https://www.washingtonpost.com/transportation/2023/10/04/dulles-airport-metro-station/]
- **One of the largest land banks of any US commercial airport (well over 10,000 acres), most of it undeveloped midfield and buffer land.** Remote stand fields, off-airport staging, modular fabrication yards, and reversible pilots need cheap flat land near the apron. Dulles has more of it than almost anyone.
- **The AeroTrain — an underground automated people mover currently serving only two concourses, now being extended under the $22B program.** Its tunnels and station provisioning are the durable, expensive-to-retrofit spine; whatever gets locked there is locked for 50 years.
- **A single dominant carrier (United) and its regional operation.** A dominant hub carrier is the cheapest possible pilot partner: one commercial agreement unlocks a testbed, versus negotiating with 20 airlines. Every unconventional move below is easier at Dulles than at LGA precisely because of this.
- **Saarinen's original design intent.** The 1962 terminal's whole premise was to process passengers in a compact headhouse and carry them to the aircraft — screening and boarding deliberately *not* co-located at a fixed pier. The building's DNA is decentralized. The rebuild is at risk of engineering that DNA *out* just as the industry rediscovers it.

## 2. Five solutions, most to least achievable

### Solution 1 — Reframe the plane-mate fleet as the airport's de-sequencing hedge, not its embarrassment
**Problem attacked:** The 1:1 gate–aircraft lock-in, and the risk of pouring 50-year concrete around a fixed pier geometry sized for today's boarding logic.
**Mechanism:** Keep a deliberate remote-stand field served by the just-refurbished plane-mates as *permanent flex capacity* alongside the new contact gates. When fleet mix shifts (widebody retirement, single-aisle long-haul, regional churn, whatever eVTOL becomes), you re-point aircraft to apron positions instead of rebuilding piers. The plane-mate is the reversible gate.
**Assets recombined:** The $160M-refurbished fleet + midfield apron land + United's regional operation as anchor user.
**Cost order-of-magnitude:** The fleet money is *already committed*. Marginal cost is apron pavement and power — low tens of millions against a $22B program, i.e. rounding error, for optionality you otherwise cannot buy.
**Implementation path:** Preserve one plane-mate-served remote apron zone in the master plan rather than treating "kill the lounges" as the goal. Instrument it as flex/surge/irregular-ops capacity.
**Who resists and why:** Airlines want jet bridges (passenger experience, turn speed). Operators remember the lounges as slow. The board wants the visible modernity of demolishing them. The resistance is emotional as much as technical.
**Failure mode:** Weather exposure, slower turns, and passenger dislike at scale. This is a *hedge held in reserve*, not the primary boarding model — sized for flexibility, not for peak throughput.

### Solution 2 — Turn the Silver Line station into an off-airport check-in and bag-drop node
**Problem attacked:** Processing migrating off-airport and onto the passenger's device; the headhouse being over-sized for curbside check-in that is already thinning.
**Mechanism:** Put self bag-drop and biometric check-in at the Metro station (and eventually in-town), so a share of passengers arrive already processed. This directly shrinks the check-in hall the building must be sized around — the thesis's "does dwell/retail grow, shrink, or relocate" question, answered with an existing rail asset.
**Assets recombined:** Silver Line station + the moving-walkway tunnel + WMATA relationship + a bag path into the new baggage system.
**Cost order-of-magnitude:** Kiosks and desks are cheap (hundreds of thousands). The real cost is baggage chain-of-custody and secure transport from station to CBIS — low single-digit millions to pilot, and it must be designed into the new bag system now, when it's cheap, not bolted on later.
**Implementation path:** Start with a staffed United bag-drop desk at the station for a few routes; measure uptake and per-bag cost before committing built infrastructure.
**Who resists:** TSA (chain-of-custody, screening location), and the finance office (who pays to move the bag). 
**Failure mode:** Low uptake and a per-bag transport cost that never pencils. Hong Kong makes this work because the rail link is fast and integrated; Dulles's ~50-minute ride to downtown is the honest constraint. Pilot before you pour.

### Solution 3 — Buy optionality with provisioning overbuild: the durable/volatile split made physical
**Problem attacked:** Retrofit being unaffordable later — the core "overbuild now because you can't retrofit" mandate in the run.
**Mechanism:** Overbuild the *cheap, permanent* stuff and leave the *expensive, volatile* stuff soft. Concretely: spare structural bays and floor loading for screening equipment that doesn't exist yet; oversized electrical rooms, risers, and conduit/raceway (empty is fine — empty conduit is the cheapest future-proofing there is); generous floor-to-floor heights; power and data stubbed to *every* potential gate, apron, and floor zone, not just today's. Then deliberately leave partition walls, screening halls, and fit-out demountable.
**Assets recombined:** Standard construction practice + column-grid discipline. No new technology at all — this is pure engineering judgment applied at design time.
**Cost order-of-magnitude:** A low single-digit percentage premium at construction versus 10–50x that cost to retrofit occupied space later. Industry guidance is blunt: it's far easier to overbuild raceway up front than to rip out and re-cable an operating terminal. [Source: https://www.electriciantalk.com/f2/spare-raceway-future-use-55645/]
**Implementation path:** A "durable vs. volatile" provisioning overlay on the concourse design sets, with a locked minimum for conduit fill, spare electrical capacity, and floor loading.
**Who resists:** Value engineering. This is always the first thing cut because it buys nothing you can photograph on opening day. Naming it as insurance — with the retrofit multiple attached — is the only defense.
**Failure mode:** Guessing the wrong loads. Mitigate by provisioning *generic capacity* (power, space, conduit, structure) rather than technology-specific rooms.

### Solution 4 — Build a durable shell with a demountable interior (DfMA + reconfigurable fit-out)
**Problem attacked:** The whole durable-vs-volatile problem — 50-year structure married to 5–15-year screening, IT, and retail.
**Mechanism:** Lock the shell, column grid, level changes, and apron geometry as the 50-year layer. Make everything above the floor slab a kit of parts: screening halls, gate holds, and retail as demountable modules and furniture, not poured walls. When screening distributes or dwell relocates, you reconfigure the floor plate in a weekend instead of a capital project.
**Assets recombined:** Proven airport DfMA/modular practice — DFW's record modular Terminal F moves, Atlanta's Concourse D modular widening, PDX's mass-timber prefab. Changi T5 is explicitly designing as reconfigurable "sub-terminals." This is copying operators who already run it.
**Cost order-of-magnitude:** Comparable or cheaper capital, with documented 22–45% schedule compression and, at ATL, ~$500M of avoided airline revenue loss from staying operational during construction. [Source: https://www.internationalairportreview.com/news/292266/dallas-fort-worth-airport-completes-record-breaking-modular-move-as-new-terminal-f-construction-reaches-major-milestone/] [Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2673-7590/5/3/109]
**Implementation path:** Mandate a demountable-partition standard for all non-structural interior in Concourse B; specify raised access floor or generous plenum so MEP moves with the plan.
**Who resists:** Architects wanting monumental fixed interiors; MEP coordinators wary of modular joints.
**Failure mode:** Modular interface details and MEP at the joints — the recurring modular failure point. Solvable with disciplined tolerances, but it's real.

### Solution 5 — Make Dulles TSA's live distributed-screening testbed, and reserve the floor plate for it
**Problem attacked:** Whether the centralized checkpoint is a durable feature or a transitional one — the single biggest bet in the whole program, because centralized screening drives the landside/airside split and the building's entire geometry.
**Mechanism:** Don't bet the building on either answer. Instead, (a) reserve floor plate, power, and structural provisioning for *distributed/gate-level* screening as a soft option, and (b) host a live TSA/DHS self-service screening lane now to generate real throughput data on your own floor. Learn before you lock.
**Assets recombined:** The LAS Innovation Checkpoint precedent + United's dominance (one carrier's PreCheck population as the pilot cohort) + reserved concourse floor area.
**Cost order-of-magnitude:** The pilot itself is cheap — TSA and DHS S&T fund the screening technology; the airport supplies space and coordination (tens to low hundreds of thousands). The *design reservation* is nearly free if done now.
**Implementation path:** An MOU to bring a self-service lane to a Dulles concourse; a design directive to keep one distributed-screening zone soft.
**Who resists:** TSA's regulatory conservatism and staffing model; the security establishment's legitimate reasons for centralized screening (the run rightly warns against dismissing these).
**Failure mode:** Distributed screening never clears the regulatory/throughput bar and the reserved plate sits idle — but idle reserved plate is a cheap wrong bet compared to a hard-poured centralized hall you can't unbuild.

## 3. Precedents — operations already running a version of this

- **Hong Kong International — in-town/at-station check-in and bag drop on the Airport Express, plus the world's only fully smartphone-and-face departure flow.** The proof that off-airport processing on a rail link is operational, not theoretical. [Source: https://www.hongkongairport.com/en/baggage/airport-express-check-in.page] [Source: https://www.scmp.com/news/hong-kong/transport/article/3272670/hong-kong-airport-rolls-out-45-second-smartphone-express-bag-drop-service-cathay-passengers-first]
- **Harry Reid (Las Vegas) — TSA/DHS S&T self-service screening prototype (Vanderlande PAX MX2), live for PreCheck since March 2024, with self-screening pods in build for 2025.** The distributed-screening testbed already exists; Dulles can join it rather than invent it. [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/releases/2024/03/06/tsa-and-dhs-st-prototype-self-service-screening-system-harry-reid]
- **DFW, Atlanta Hartsfield-Jackson, and PDX — airport DfMA/modular delivery at scale**, with documented schedule compression and ~$500M of avoided airline revenue loss at ATL by staying operational through construction. [Source: https://www.internationalairportreview.com/news/292266/dallas-fort-worth-airport-completes-record-breaking-modular-move-as-new-terminal-f-construction-reaches-major-milestone/]
- **Frankfurt — deliberate use of remote stands to add capacity without unmanageable walking distances**, i.e. remote stands as a designed strategy, not a fallback. The economic case: remote handling is materially cheaper than jet-bridge stand time. [Source: https://gadallon.substack.com/p/remote-stands-bus-boarding-and-last]
- **Changi Terminal 5 — a two-year pandemic pause used to redesign the terminal as modular, reconfigurable "sub-terminals" that can open, close, or isolate.** The current best example of designing built form for de-sequencing and contingency rather than a single throughput assumption. [Source: https://www.flightglobal.com/air-transport/singapore-changis-terminal-5-design-to-draw-on-lessons-from-pandemic/149922.article]
- **Dulles itself, 1962–present — the mobile lounge as an already-running gate-decoupling system.** The most persuasive precedent for a non-1:1 hedge is the one the airport already owns and just re-funded. [Source: https://www.masstransitmag.com/rail/vehicles/press-release/55020243/brookville-equipment-corporation-brookville-equipment-corporation-to-rehab-dulles-internationals-plane-mate-and-mobile-lounge-vehicles]

## 4. The 90-day pilot tier — under $100K, real evidence, no study required

1. **Instrument one plane-mate as a flexible-boarding data probe.** Measure real turn time, throughput, and passenger sentiment when a boarding is fully decoupled from a fixed gate. Cost: near zero — you own the vehicle and the field. Output: hard numbers on whether the fleet is a viable de-sequencing hedge or only a legacy people-mover. This is the single highest-value 90-day move because it tests the thesis's central claim with an asset already on the ramp.
2. **Stand up a staffed United bag-drop desk at the Silver Line station** for two or three routes. Cost: a desk, two agents, and a bag cart process — well under $100K for a season. Output: real uptake and per-bag transport cost, before anyone designs station bag infrastructure into the new CBIS.
3. **Sign a TSA/DHS S&T MOU to host a self-service screening lane** and issue a one-page design directive reserving a distributed-screening zone in the Concourse B set. Cost: essentially staff time. Output: a live throughput dataset on your own floor and a preserved option worth far more than it costs.
4. **Run a "fixed vs. demountable" red-line charrette over the current concourse design set** — a two-week exercise tagging every interior wall as 50-year or 15-year. Cost: a few weeks of design fees. Output: the durable/volatile map the program needs before it pours.

Pilots 1 and 3 generate evidence about the two load-bearing bets in the entire program — gate flexibility and screening decentralization — for less than the cost of a single design study.

## 5. The honest kill list

- **An eVTOL vertiport pad built now.** Betting 50-year concrete on an unproven arrival date is exactly the techno-optimism-on-a-schedule the run forbids. *Provision* flat land and power; don't *build* the pad.
- **A fully automated underground inline baggage mega-system as the "future-proof" bag solution.** Complex, brittle, and the source of the most famous terminal failures (Denver, BER). Buy modular, expandable bag capacity, not a monument.
- **Hard-wiring a single biometric token everywhere.** The biometric/privacy regime is the most volatile layer in the building. Provision the conduit and camera positions; do not fix the system into the structure.
- **A digital twin as the headline future-proofing move.** It's software, not built form. Useful, but it answers none of the structural questions the thesis actually poses — and it's a classic way to look modern while dodging the concrete decisions.
- **A signature roofline / grand fixed atrium.** The run explicitly rejects this, and rightly: a monumental fixed volume is space you cannot repurpose when processing decentralizes. Elegant beats iconic here.
- **A permanent, purpose-built in-town city terminal in downtown DC (à la Seoul).** Tempting, but the ~50-minute rail ride makes the economics far worse than Hong Kong's. Test bag-drop at the *airport* Metro station first; earn the downtown version with data, or don't build it.

**The line to hand the Strategist:** the most expensive thing Dulles can do is pour certainty around a processing sequence that is already dissolving. The cheapest — and the most defensible — is to lock only the 50-year layer hard, keep the 15-year layer deliberately soft, and prove the two biggest bets (gate flexibility, screening location) with assets already on the property, by Thursday, for under $100K. The airport is paying $160M to keep the most literal de-sequencing machine in the industry running for another 20 years. It should stop apologizing for it and start designing around it.
