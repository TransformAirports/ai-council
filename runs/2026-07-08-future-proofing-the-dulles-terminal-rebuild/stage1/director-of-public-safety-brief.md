# Director of Public Safety — Stage 1 Brief
## Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*Independent research brief. Lens: the executive who owns airport police, ARFF/fire, and 911/dispatch under one command at a top-tier hub. Written for the Strategist synthesizing the Dulles rebuild thesis.*

---

## Framing: where public safety actually intersects this thesis

The thesis is about durable form versus volatile fit-out — what in a terminal has a 50-year life and what has a 5-to-15-year life. That framework is exactly how I think about public safety, and it is exactly where most terminal programs get it wrong. Screening regimes, camera analytics, access-control readers, and dispatch software are volatile — they will turn over three or four times before the concrete does. But the things public safety depends on to *work* — apron geometry that lets an ARFF truck hit the runway midpoint in three minutes, the conduit and riser capacity that a distributed screening future will demand, the hardened dispatch/EOC core, the standpipe and egress spine — are durable, expensive to retrofit, and get value-engineered out first because they are invisible on opening day.

Here is the uncomfortable truth I bring to a program meeting: **compliance and competence are not the same thing.** A terminal can be fully Part 139 and Part 1542 compliant on the day it opens and still be a building where my teams cannot deliver the response the traveling public assumes they are buying. Most airport executives treat public safety as a compliance checkbox and discover the gap only during an incident. The Dulles rebuild is a rare chance to design the gap out — or to lock it in for 50 years.

---

## 1. Key findings

- **The 3-minute ARFF response standard is a built-form constraint, not a staffing footnote.** 14 CFR §139.319(h) requires at least one required vehicle to reach the midpoint of the farthest air-carrier runway and begin agent application within **3 minutes of alarm**, with all other required vehicles arriving within **4 minutes** [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/139.319]. A near-total rebuild with a new runway, new midfield concourses, and reconfigured apron geometry moves that "farthest runway midpoint" — meaning the *location and number of ARFF stations* is a durable, 50-year decision that has to be fixed at the geometry stage, not the fit-out stage. Get the station siting wrong and no amount of staffing buys the three minutes back.

- **Dulles is an Index E airport and the rebuild raises, not lowers, that obligation.** Index E (aircraft ≥200 ft — the widebody international fleet Dulles is built around) requires three vehicles carrying **at least 6,000 gallons** of water plus commensurate AFFF [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/139.317]. Index is sized to *current* fleet with five-plus daily departures — but a terminal designed for the 2035–2050 widebody-and-single-aisle-long-haul mix has to provision fire-station bays, apron access, and agent-resupply for the top of the index for its whole life, even as the fleet mix shifts underneath it.

- **Screening decentralization is the single largest public-safety variable in this program, and it cuts against the durability of the centralized checkpoint.** The centralized checkpoint exists for a security *and* a policing reason: it concentrates the sterile-area boundary where TSA screening, law-enforcement response, and access control can be economically massed. Distribute screening to the gate or off-airport and you multiply the sterile/non-sterile boundary — which multiplies the LEO-response coverage (§1542.215/.217) and the camera/access-control provisioning the building must carry. The physical commitment is reversible in software; it is *not* reversible in the riser, conduit, and power capacity you either did or didn't pour into the slab.

- **Where the crowd is unscreened is where people die.** Fort Lauderdale 2017 (five killed, six wounded in **under 80 seconds** in baggage claim) is the definitive American case that the landside, non-sterile terminal — curb, check-in, bag claim — is the soft target, not the checkpoint [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. A thesis that decentralizes and de-sequences processing must confront that it may *increase* landside dwell and unscreened concentration unless the built form is designed for police visibility, sightlines, and rapid lockdown from day one.

- **The PSAP question is a design decision, not just an org-chart one.** Whoever owns the primary 911 answering point determines who picks up when a passenger dials 911 from a jet bridge — and NG911 geographic routing depends on GIS data that a brand-new terminal's addressing must feed correctly or calls misroute to the county [Source: https://www.fcc.gov/policy-and-licensing-division/911-services/NG911]. A rebuilt terminal needs a hardened, redundant dispatch/EOC core provisioned in the durable structure; that is cheap to pour now and unaffordable to retrofit into an occupied terminal later.

- **Resilience is a public-safety asset, and peer hubs have proven the failure mode.** Atlanta's December 2017 **11-hour** power outage — an underground fire that took out both the primary feed *and* the "redundant" system, stranding 30,000 — is the canonical warning that redundancy on paper is not redundancy in fact [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2017/12/17/us/atlanta-airport-power-outage/index.html]. When the lights go out, public safety *is* the continuity plan. Dispatch, access control, egress lighting, and radio infrastructure are the loads that must survive, and they are durable provisioning decisions.

- **You cannot staff your way out of a design or a labor problem on the schedule executives assume.** Standing up sworn officers is a 12-to-18-month pipeline — background investigation (2–4 months), academy (13–21 weeks, cohorts only 2–3x/year), field training (8–16 weeks), then an 18-month probation [Source: https://joinphillypd.com/application-process/hiring-process-overview/]. Layer TSA-driven clearances and a tight first-responder market where surrounding jurisdictions poach with better pensions, and "hire 12 more officers" is an 18-month program, not a 90-day fix. The building will open before the org chart is real.

- **Public-safety capital is chronically underfunded in the CIP because it is unglamorous and lumpy.** An Index E ARFF apparatus is a multi-million-dollar, ~10–15-year-life asset; patrol fleets cycle on 5–8 years; portable/mobile radios on ~10–12 years with P25 and encryption mandates forcing whole-fleet swaps; security technology (cameras, access control, screening) on 5–10 years. None of these carry a ribbon-cutting, so they lose the CIP fight to terminal aesthetics — until an incident reprices them.

## 2. Evidence

**ARFF response-time mandate (durable geometry driver).** 14 CFR §139.319(h)(2): within 3 minutes of alarm one required vehicle must reach the midpoint of the farthest air-carrier runway and begin agent application; all other required vehicles within 4 minutes. The rule also mandates at least one EMS-trained responder during air-carrier ops and recurrent training every 12 months [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/139.319]. This is the clock that fire-station siting must beat, and a rebuilt airfield with a new/reconstructed runway relocates the point that clock is measured to.

**Index thresholds and equipment (lifecycle driver).** §139.317 sets agent-carriage by index: Index C ~3,000 gal, Index D ≥4,000 gal, Index E ≥6,000 gal water plus commensurate AFFF, across three required vehicles at the top indices [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/139.317]. Index is determined by the longest air-carrier aircraft with ≥5 average daily departures (§139.315). The 2024 consolidation folded NFPA 403/405/412 into **NFPA 460**, and the industry-wide move off legacy AFFF to fluorine-free foam (FFSF) is itself a lifecycle cost the program must absorb [Source: https://www.fire-police-ems.com/NFPA460-2024.shtml]. Note the fleet-timing trap: index is sized to *today's* fleet, but bays, aprons, and agent resupply must be provisioned for the index the 2050 fleet implies.

**Airport police authority and its hiring constraints (Part 1542).** 49 CFR §1542.217 requires LEOs meeting the airport security program to have arrest authority (with or without warrant) for crimes committed in their presence and for felonies on reasonable belief, and to be trained to the state/local standard for comparable officers [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/1542.217]. §1542.215 requires law-enforcement personnel "in the number and manner adequate" to support the screening system. Critically, **MWAA already runs its own full-service, Virginia-accredited police department** patrolling airport grounds plus the Access Highway, Toll Road, and a 300-yard perimeter — an *independent-department* model, not an extension of a host municipal force [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Washington_Airports_Authority]. That is an asset (unity of command, airport-specific training) and a liability (MWAA owns the entire recruitment, clearance, and retention burden with no host department to backstop a shortfall).

**Recruitment/retention reality.** IACP's 2024 survey (1,158 agencies): 70%+ say recruitment is harder than five years ago; agencies average **91% of authorized strength**; 65% have cut services or units for lack of staff; ~18% of sworn personnel retirement-eligible, rising toward 24% [Source: https://www.theiacp.org/resources/the-state-of-recruitment-retention-a-continuing-crisis-for-police]. Standard hiring pipeline runs 6–18 months before an officer is solo-capable [Source: https://joinphillypd.com/application-process/hiring-process-overview/]. Collective bargaining, FLSA §7(k) treatment of irregular first-responder shift cycles, and pension competition from surrounding jurisdictions are the structural reasons the pipeline cannot be compressed by will alone.

**PSAP / NG911.** NG911 routes calls via GIS-based geospatial location to the correct PSAP, with editable boundary layers for failover [Source: https://www.fcc.gov/policy-and-licensing-division/911-services/NG911]. The FCC's 2025 NG911 order pushes implementation and reliability obligations forward [Source: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/06/04/2025-09279/facilitating-implementation-of-next-generation-911-services-ng911-improving-911-reliability]. The practical failure mode: a wireless 911 call from a jet bridge can route to the county PSAP rather than airport dispatch if addressing/GIS is wrong, injecting a transfer delay into exactly the calls where seconds matter.

**Active-shooter case (soft-target lesson).** FLL 2017 AAR findings: no visible armed presence in baggage areas; tabletop exercises had not modeled the actual event; ~90 minutes post-incident, false "shots fired" reports triggered a chaotic self-evacuation across the airport — the *secondary* panic did much of the operational damage [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. Built-form implications: sightlines, police posting economics, controllable egress, and public-address/wayfinding that survives a mass-panic event.

**Resilience case.** ATL December 2017: underground electrical fire disabled primary and redundant feeds; ~11 hours dark; 30,000 stranded; 1,000+ flights cancelled. A year later, full emergency backup was still 30–36 months and $130M+ away [Source: https://www.11alive.com/article/travel/airport-blackout-a-year-later/85-86415a7d-e626-4d15-9be9-894c1773c7f0]. The lesson for a new build: physically separate the primary and backup electrical paths and put public-safety loads on the survivable bus — a durable decision made at the utility-spine stage.

**Mutual aid.** ACRP's model-agreements work and the fire-service literature agree: the signed MOU is necessary but not sufficient. What actually shows up at 3 AM depends on shared SOPs, a common Incident Command System, radio interoperability, and a dedicated fireground/dispatch channel — the absence of interoperable comms is the recurring failure point [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/22542/chapter/8; https://www.firerescue1.com/communications-interoperability/articles/rapid-response-every-department-needs-mutual-aid-agreements-and-communications-interoperability-JZHstO5dxS6mPMCY/]. A rebuilt terminal that doesn't provision interoperable radio infrastructure (DAS, bi-directional amplifiers, mutual-aid channels) into the structure will discover the gap during the first multi-agency event.

## 3. The public-safety case for and against the thesis

**For the thesis (decentralization/de-sequencing is coming and the building should hedge):**
The centralized checkpoint is a *transitional* durable feature, not a permanent one. Distributed and off-airport screening are technically plausible within 10–25 years, and a program that hard-locks a single monumental central checkpoint hall is betting against that. From my chair, the honest hedge is to provision the *durable* layer — riser capacity, conduit, power, structural bays, and clear-span flexibility — so screening can migrate without demolition, while keeping the *volatile* layer (the actual screening lanes and technology) soft and swappable. That is a public-safety argument *for* the thesis: build the bones for a distributed future, don't pour the specific 2026 checkpoint into the foundation.

**Against the thesis (or where it underweights real response capability):**
De-sequencing has a body count risk the thesis glosses. Every place you distribute screening, you extend the sterile/non-sterile boundary and multiply the LEO-response, camera, and access-control burden — and you likely *grow* the unscreened landside concentration that FLL proved is the killing ground. Decentralization also degrades the massed-response economics that let a single well-posted police contingent cover a central checkpoint; spread the boundary and you either spread thin or you hire — and hiring is an 18-month, tight-market, pension-competition problem MWAA owns alone as an independent department. The thesis treats the checkpoint as inertia; part of it is genuine risk architecture. And none of the decentralization futures relaxes the *physical* constants: ARFF still has to hit the runway midpoint in three minutes, dispatch still has to survive a power event, and mutual aid still has to be interoperable at 3 AM. Those are the durable commitments; they should be locked now regardless of which screening future arrives.

**My synthesis for the Strategist:** the thesis is right that *processing sequence* is volatile and shouldn't be poured into concrete. It underweights that *public-safety response capability* is durable, expensive, and the first thing value-engineered out. The move is not to bet for or against decentralization — it's to overbuild the invisible durable layer (apron/station geometry, redundant power to safety loads, hardened dispatch core, radio/DAS infrastructure, conduit and riser capacity) precisely *because* the screening future is uncertain, and to keep the security regime deliberately soft.

## 4. Specifics a strategist can deploy

1. **The 80-second number.** FLL 2017: five dead, six wounded in under 80 seconds, entirely landside/unscreened, ended only when the shooter ran out of ammunition — and the 90-minute secondary panic caused a separate self-evacuation disaster [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. Use this whenever the thesis implies more unscreened landside dwell.

2. **The three-minute constant.** §139.319: 3 minutes to the farthest-runway midpoint, 4 minutes for all other required vehicles [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/139.319]. A new runway relocates that point; ARFF station siting is therefore a 50-year, geometry-stage decision, not a fit-out line item.

3. **Dulles is Index E and independent.** ≥6,000 gallons across three vehicles [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/139.317], and MWAA runs its own Virginia-accredited police force covering the airports plus the Access Highway/Toll Road and a 300-yard perimeter [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Washington_Airports_Authority] — unity of command, but MWAA alone owns the recruitment, clearance, and retention risk.

4. **The 11-hour blackout.** ATL 2017 took out primary *and* redundant power; full backup was still 30–36 months and $130M+ away a year later [Source: https://www.11alive.com/article/travel/airport-blackout-a-year-later/85-86415a7d-e626-4d15-9be9-894c1773c7f0]. Redundancy on the drawing is not redundancy in the ground; separate the paths physically and put safety loads on the survivable bus.

5. **The 18-month org chart.** Sworn-officer standup is a 6–18-month pipeline before solo capability, against an industry running at ~91% of authorized strength with 18–24% retirement-eligible [Sources: https://joinphillypd.com/application-process/hiring-process-overview/; https://www.theiacp.org/resources/the-state-of-recruitment-retention-a-continuing-crisis-for-police]. The building opens before the staffing does — so if the design *adds* posts (distributed screening), the labor plan has to lead the ribbon-cutting by two years.

6. **The jet-bridge 911 call.** Under NG911, a wireless 911 call from a terminal jet bridge routes on GIS data; wrong addressing sends it to the county PSAP and injects a transfer delay [Source: https://www.fcc.gov/policy-and-licensing-division/911-services/NG911]. Decide who answers, and provision the hardened dispatch core in the durable structure now.

---

*Bottom line: design the volatile security regime to move; lock the durable response capability that no future screening model relaxes. The airport that confuses the two will be compliant on opening day and exposed on the first bad night.*
