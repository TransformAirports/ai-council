# Council Brief — Watts Lens

**Run:** Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild
**Agent:** council-watts (the frame, not the picture)
**One line:** You cannot future-proof a building against a future you can't name. You can only make being wrong cheap — and Dulles is about to spend $22B making one particular wrongness expensive.

---

## 1. The word "future-proofing" is the first mistake

The run asks how to future-proof a terminal for 2035–2050. That framing quietly smuggles in a promise no building can keep: that if we forecast hard enough, we can lock geometry that the future will ratify. That is not a design problem. It is a prediction problem wearing a design problem's clothes, and prediction is exactly the thing every dated mega-terminal was confident it had solved.

Reframe the whole exercise with one substitution. Stop sorting terminal elements by **how long they last** and sort them by **how much it costs to be wrong about them.** These are not the same axis, and the run's own durable/volatile split blurs them. A structural column grid lasts 50 years *and* is ruinous to move — high durability, high regret cost. A security fit-out lasts 8 years *and* is cheap to rip out — low durability, low regret cost. The dangerous quadrant is the one nobody names: **elements that are physically permanent but were sized to a convention that expires.** A checkpoint hall poured as a fixed room. An AeroTrain alignment that assumes centralized processing forever. A gate count that assumes 1:1. These are 50-year commitments to 15-year ideas. That quadrant — not "technology" — is where terminals die.

So the deliverable is not a durable-vs-volatile list. It is a single governing question applied to every drawing: *if the operating logic this element assumes turns out to be wrong in 2040, what does it cost to undo?* If the answer is "cheap," decide now and move on. If the answer is "unaffordable," you are not allowed to bake in an assumption — you must buy the empty version.

## 2. Dulles already ran this experiment. It lost. That is the most useful fact in the room.

Here is the thing the McKinsey deck won't tell you, and it is sitting in the airport's own basement.

The thesis champions a decentralized, de-sequenced, gate-decoupled terminal where processing distributes and the gate-to-aircraft relationship stops being 1:1. **That is a precise description of Eero Saarinen's original 1962 Dulles.** The mobile lounge was not a quirk; it was a radical bet that you could keep the terminal small and flexible by severing the fixed relationship between building and aircraft. Planes parked 4,000 feet out. No concourses. No gates. Apron flexibility by design. Saarinen explicitly listed his three problems — walking distance, taxi distance, apron rigidity — and the mobile lounge was his answer to all three. It is the same three problems this run is asking about in 2026.

It lost. Not because the idea was stupid, but because the *surrounding system* refused to cooperate: hub connecting traffic (New York Air stood up hub ops in the 1980s and immediately demanded fixed gates), passenger tolerance for waiting on a bus for a connection, and the operational reality that a decoupled building still needs the airline, the regulator, and the human body to agree to decouple. By 1985 Dulles was bolting on midfield concourses with jet bridges. The building didn't fail. **The assumption that geometry could force a de-sequenced operation onto a system that wanted a sequenced one — that failed.**

Now watch what the $22B program does. It kills the mobile lounges. It extends the AeroTrain from two concourses to a full A-through-D spine. It builds four fixed linear concourses with fixed contact gates for United's hub. It re-centralizes, hard, at the exact historical moment the thesis argues the world is de-centralizing.

I want to be very clear that this is probably **correct for 2034.** United runs a connecting hub; connecting hubs want contact gates and a fast people-mover; the bond market and the airline use agreement are being signed against a concrete program *now*, not against a 2050 hypothesis. The fire is real: you cannot sell $21.8B in bonds against "we'll leave it soft." Machiavelli and the CEO agent will tell you the same thing and they'll be right.

But name the bet honestly. Dulles is spending $22B to un-do the exact experiment the thesis says is coming back. The lesson from 1962 is not "Saarinen was right, distribute everything." The lesson is deeper and it cuts against the thesis too: **the building was never the binding constraint. The operating logic was.** You cannot design your way into de-sequencing while the airline, the TSA, and the passenger still want the sequence — and you could not design your way out of it in 1962 either. Built form follows operating agreements, not the other way around. Which means the highest-leverage future-proofing move is not architectural at all: it is to keep the *operating agreements* short and the *physical provisioning* generous, so that when the logic finally shifts, the building can follow without a second $22B.

## 3. The verbs are durable. The nouns are conventions.

Strip the passenger-processing sequence — curb, check-in, bag drop, checkpoint, gate hold, boarding — down to what is actually physical and what is merely a category we invented.

Physical, 50-year, non-negotiable: a human body with luggage must get from a wheeled vehicle (car, bus, train) up to a seat inside an aluminum tube parked on an apron. That's it. That is the whole irreducible problem. The body moves, changes level, waits, and boards.

Everything else is a **convention**, not a constraint:
- Check-in desks exist because airlines once needed to sell and tag at the airport. That's a business process, not physics — it's already 80% gone to the phone.
- The *centralized* checkpoint exists because that's how we chose to concentrate TSOs and expensive machines. TSA's own Screening at Speed program is explicitly building distributed screening and self-service pods that "reduce the need for a checkpoint." The centralization is a deployment decision, not a security law.
- The gate *hold room* exists because boarding is a batch process run to a schedule. Change the boarding model and the room's whole purpose changes.

So the durable design instruction is: **design the verbs, not the nouns.** Build for *move, change level, wait, board* — which need structural bays, floor-to-floor height, vertical circulation cores, and apron access — and refuse to pour concrete around *checkpoint, check-in hall, duty-free march,* which are this decade's spelling of a temporary agreement. The terminals that dated fastest all committed concrete to a noun. IST poured a single 1.4M m² building sized to one throughput logic and now eats 20–30 minute walks with no train to fix it. PKX poured a starfish and holds 8-minute walks because it committed to a *movement geometry* (radial, decomposable) rather than a *throughput number*. Same era, same ambition; one bet on a verb, one bet on a noun.

## 4. The hidden third option: refuse to decide

The run's framework — design FOR now / accommodate LATER / explicitly bet AGAINST — is good, but it hides a fourth box, and it's the most valuable one. "Accommodate later" still pre-commits geometry (you're shaping space *for* an anticipated thing). The missing option is **deliberately undecided space**: column-free, over-structured, over-serviced high-bay volume with *nothing assigned to it.* Not flex space designed for a known flex use — genuinely empty cubic meters whose value is that no one has spent their meaning yet.

This is the cheapest optionality in the building and the first thing value-engineering kills, because an empty double-height hall with no program looks like waste on a spreadsheet. It is the opposite. It is the only line item that can become a distributed screening floor in 2040, or a consolidated processing mezzanine, or a gate-lounge district, or a thing we don't have a word for yet — *without moving structure.* Overbuild the shell; underbuild the program.

## 5. Kill the metric that builds the wrong building

Grounding, concrete: the single artifact most likely to date Dulles is **peak-hour throughput capacity used as the master geometry driver.** Every dated mega-terminal sized its geometry to a passenger-throughput number, and throughput is exactly the variable that processing technology is collapsing — CT scanners already double lane throughput; distributed and on-device pre-clear will collapse it further. Size the checkpoint hall to 2027 queue physics and by 2035 you own a cathedral-sized room built for a bottleneck that no longer exists.

Replace the master metric with two invariants that *don't* expire:
1. **Maximum walking distance** (be PKX, not IST). Distance is what the body experiences; throughput is what the spreadsheet experiences. Distance ages well.
2. **Reconfiguration cost per square meter.** Make it a scored design criterion, not an afterthought. Every major room gets asked: what does it cost to change your function without touching a column?

## 6. Where the energy actually is (commercial)

As processing time compresses, the revenue model built on a slow, captive, sequenced passenger marched past duty-free evaporates — that passenger is the mobile lounge of retail: a 1970s convention we keep building rooms for. Don't size landside retail volume to walk-through monetization. The genuine dwell — the moments where a passenger *chooses* to spend money and attention — is migrating airside, toward the gate, toward food and lounges they actually want, and it *shrinks in duration but concentrates.* Design flexible commercial frontage near where people wait by choice, not a fixed forced-march concourse of leased boxes sized to a dwell time that is falling.

## 7. Two dated snapshots, through this lens

**2035:** Mobile lounges gone, AeroTrain runs A–D, screening still centralized but CT-based and fast. The passenger clears security in minutes — and the checkpoint hall, sized to 2027 queues, now feels half-empty. The bottleneck moved from screening to nothing; the room shaped like a bottleneck has no bottleneck to hold. The operator's problem is no longer flow, it's *stranded volume* and retail sized to a landside dwell that migrated airside.

**2050:** Screening has distributed — some at curb, some on-device, pods near gates. The 1:1 gate relationship has softened into swing stands and remote positions served by — quietly — buses again, the mobile lounge returning as a ghost. The terminals that won are the ones whose structure let them move walls into the empty volumes they were mocked for building. The ones that lost are the ones whose permanent geometry *was* the checkpoint. Dulles is on the winning side of this only if, in 2027, it overbuilt the shell and refused to pour the nouns.

## 8. Concrete moves for the program meeting

Sorted by the axis that matters — regret cost, not lifespan.

**Overbuild now (cheap now, unaffordable to retrofit — lock these):**
- Structural bay depth, floor-to-floor height, and column-free spans well beyond current program. Height and clear span are the mother of all optionality.
- Vertical circulation cores and utility/power/comms risers sized for 2× current demand. You will never regret an oversized riser; you will always regret a full one.
- Apron depth, fueling, and ground-power/electrification trenching sized for a decarbonized, mixed fleet. The apron is the true 50-year commitment and the one this thesis underweights — electrified GSE and SAF logistics are *physical* loads the slab either carries or doesn't.
- **MARS/swing-stand provisioning** (one widebody or two narrowbody per position) as the fleet-mix hedge — the direct answer to the dying 1:1 gate relationship, and it lives in apron geometry and gate-lounge structure, both pourable now.
- Common-use everything (CUTE/MUSE at every position) so no airline-specific geometry gets baked into the concrete.

**Leave deliberately empty (refuse to decide):**
- The screening hall as a column-free high-bay flex volume, not a fixed room — able to host a centralized CT hall in 2027 and be subdivided into distributed pods by 2040 without moving structure.
- Gate-hold areas as reconfigurable lounge volume, not fixed seat-count boxes tied to a gate.
- At least one genuinely unprogrammed high-bay bank whose only spec is: over-structured, over-serviced, un-assigned.

**Bet against explicitly (and hedge with the two lists above):**
- eVTOL-at-scale, universal biometrics, and full ground-handling autonomy on a fixed date. Provision the *access and power* for them (cheap) but design no dedicated geometry around their arrival (expensive if the date slips, which it will).

**The one non-architectural move that beats all of them:**
- Keep the airline use agreements and the fit-out amortization schedules *short.* The 1962 lesson is that operating logic — not concrete — decides whether a terminal can de-sequence. The most powerful future-proofing lever at Dulles isn't in the drawings; it's in the contracts that govern how long any given operating assumption gets to hold the building hostage.

---

**Bottom line for the Council:** The thesis is right that terminals lock in the throughput logic of the decade they're drawn in. But the fix isn't better forecasting — it's refusing to convert temporary agreements into permanent concrete, and overbuilding the shell so the building can follow the operating logic instead of predicting it. Dulles is the perfect and slightly tragic test case: it invented de-sequencing, is spending $22B to reverse it, is probably right to for 2034, and will only survive 2050 if it treats that reversal as a bet it hedged rather than a truth it believed.
