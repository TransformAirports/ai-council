# Architectural-Historian Brief: Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild

*Stage 1 research brief. Independent lens: the design-philosophical record of the airport terminal as a building type, with architectural intent separated from architectural aesthetic.*

---

## Framing note for the strategist

This run is exceptionally well-served by the architectural record, because the building being rebuilt is the one building in the canon where an architect ran the precise experiment the thesis is about — decoupling passenger processing from a fixed gate geometry — and had it undone. Dulles is not a metaphor for the thesis. It is the thesis's own prior trial, on the same apron, at 1:1 scale, in 1962. The strategist should treat the original Saarinen terminal as *evidence*, not as brand heritage.

The single most useful fact in this brief: the year Dulles opened (1962), the critic Reyner Banham published "The Obsolescent Airport," arguing that the airport is the most transient building type of the century, permanently unfinished, because aircraft evolve faster than buildings can. The thesis is 64 years old. What has changed is not the diagnosis but the cost of getting it wrong — a $22B program instead of a $100M one. [Source: https://www.architectural-review.com/author/reyner-banham]

## 1. Key findings (architectural-historical arc the thesis sits inside)

- **The thesis has a primary-source ancestor, and it names this exact building.** Reyner Banham, *Architectural Review*, October 1962: airports are "the most transitional and transient building types of our time, always unfinished." That was published the same year Dulles and Saarinen's TWA both opened. The intellectual move the thesis makes — separate the durable shell from the volatile processing regime — is the founding critical insight of airport architecture, not a new one. [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reyner_Banham]

- **Dulles was itself a de-sequencing bet, and the bet failed operationally while the shell succeeded.** Saarinen's mobile lounge was designed to eliminate concourses entirely: process the passenger in one compact head-house, then drive them to an aircraft parked anywhere on the apron. This is *precisely* the "gate-to-aircraft relationship stops being 1:1" and "processing migrates off the fixed gate" future the thesis describes. It was tried, and it was abandoned — midfield concourses arrived in the 1980s, the AeroTrain in 2010. [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport_Main_Terminal]

- **Intent vs. aesthetic, stated cleanly at Dulles.** The catenary roof is the *aesthetic*. The mobile lounge is the *operational diagram*. The design philosophy — a compact, extensible terminal decoupled from fixed piers — is the *bridge*. The public remembers the roof; the building's actual behavior was governed by the lounge; and the lounge is what dated. The strategist must not let the roofline stand in for the idea.

- **The durable/volatile split the run wants was literally engineered into Dulles — and it held.** Saarinen designed the head-house to be doubled in length; the ground-level foundations, ramps, and "empty openings" were provisioned for future entrances. SOM doubled the terminal in 1996 by replicating the cable-suspension bay, and the building read as continuous. The *reversible* decision (extendable shell) survived 60 years. The *irreversible* decision (no concourses, fleet of custom vehicles) had to be bought back for hundreds of millions. That is the entire durable-vs-volatile framework, demonstrated once, on this site. [Source: https://www.som.com/projects/washington_dulles_international_airport__main_terminal_expansion]

- **The counter-model exists and is architecturally explicit: Foster's Stansted (1991).** Foster turned the airport "upside down" — all heavy services (baggage, plant) go to an undercroft, structure collapses into freestanding "trees," and the roof does nothing but keep out rain and let in light. The result is a single clear floor plate on which any processing sequence can be re-laid without touching structure. Stansted is the built argument *for* the thesis: express the enclosure, never express the processing diagram in permanent form. [Source: https://www.fosterandpartners.com/projects/stansted-airport/]

- **TWA is the control case: fix the form to the processing model and the fleet, and you get a museum.** Saarinen's TWA "could not be adapted with the rapid change in aviation and the wider aircraft" of the jumbo era; it closed in 2001 and reopened in 2019 as a hotel lobby. TWA and Dulles are the same architect, the same year, the same aesthetic vocabulary — and opposite outcomes, because one was designed soft and one was designed as a finished object. [Source: https://www.archdaily.com/788012/ad-classics-twa-flight-center-eero-saarinen]

- **The architectural conversation was displaced by the engineering-and-operations conversation after ~1980, and the megaterminals show the cost.** When authorship passed from auteurs to corporate planning firms and program managers, the terminal became functionally adequate and architecturally mute. The recent mega-terminals the run names (IST, PKX, BER, LGA) are not failures of *forecasting*; they are failures of *reversibility* — each locked a throughput geometry into permanent structure. The design-philosophical voice that used to ask "which of these commitments can we take back?" was no longer in the room.

- **Honesty flag against hagiography:** Saarinen is *not* a clean hero for this thesis. His most famous processing innovation (the mobile lounge) is exactly the kind of unproven, form-defining bet the run tells you to treat as a bet, not a given — and it lost. The usable Saarinen lesson is the boring one he rarely gets credit for: *he provisioned the shell for a doubling he would not live to see.* The strategist should cite Saarinen for the extensibility discipline, not for the visionary processing concept.

## 2. Evidence

**The FAA as client, and what it actually asked for.** The program brief for Dulles told Saarinen's team to reduce passenger walking distance, minimize aircraft taxiing cost, and maximize apron flexibility; research showed passengers were averaging 900 feet from terminal to plane at existing airports. The mobile lounge was the *answer to a brief about flexibility* — the FAA asked for apron flexibility and got a solution that decoupled the aircraft from any fixed pier. This matters for the run: the decentralization impulse is not new, and the last time an owner optimized hard for apron flexibility, the answer was a custom vehicle fleet that became a maintenance and capital liability. [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport_Main_Terminal]

**Saarinen's own words (first-hand, use with care).** Saarinen called Dulles "the best thing I have done" and described the roof as "a huge continuous hammock suspended between concrete trees." Note what these quotes are *about*: they are about the shell and the image. Saarinen's writing on the mobile lounge is technical and confident — and the confidence is the cautionary part. The strategist can use the "hammock/trees" quote to make the point that the architect's own language memorializes the durable element (the structure) and is silent on the element that failed (the processing vehicle). [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport_Main_Terminal]

**The extensibility provision (documented, and the strongest pro-thesis fact).** "The original 600-by-150-foot structure incorporated provisions for future extensions… vehicular ramps and foundational support anticipated doubling the building's length. Empty openings at ground level were specifically designed to accommodate future entrances." SOM's 1996 annexes "replicated the original roof's cable-suspension system," extending the terminal to 1,240 feet and more than doubling floor area to ~1.1M sq ft. This is the run's "overbuild the physical provisioning now because retrofit is unaffordable later" principle, executed successfully, on the exact site. [Source: https://www.som.com/projects/washington_dulles_international_airport__main_terminal_expansion]

**The processing bet that had to be bought back.** Mobile lounges "eliminated the need for traditional finger-like concourses and allowed flexible aircraft positioning," but "the concept was never widely adopted." Midfield concourses were added from the 1980s and the AeroTrain people-mover opened in 2010, converting the lounges from primary processing infrastructure to gap-filler shuttles. The de-sequenced model did not fail because it was wrong in principle; it failed because it was expressed in *irreversible capital* (a compact terminal with no concourse capacity, plus a bespoke vehicle fleet) with no cheap path back to the conventional pier when volumes, security, and airline economics demanded it. [Source: https://www.mightytravels.com/2024/10/the-rise-and-fall-of-mobile-lounges-a-look-back-at-dulles-airports-unique-passenger-transport-system/]

**The flexible-shell counter-doctrine, stated by its author.** Foster + Partners on Stansted: it "turns the idea of a traditional airport 'upside-down,' banishing the intensive services such as baggage handling usually found at roof level to an undercroft." Service distribution runs through the "trunks" of the structural "trees," so "a roof canopy… is freed simply to keep out the rain and let in light," and "movement through the building is straightforward and direct — there are none of the level changes and orientation problems that characterise most airports." This is the durable/volatile split as a design manifesto: permanent = shell + undercroft spine; soft = everything on the floor plate. [Source: https://www.fosterandpartners.com/projects/stansted-airport/] [Source: https://www.dezeen.com/2019/11/25/stansted-airport-foster-partners-high-tech-architecture/]

**Honesty flag on Stansted.** The flexibility was real but not self-maintaining. Over three decades, retail and security queuing colonized the clean single-level floor Foster designed to keep open; the diagram degraded because *nobody defended the softness*. Lesson for Dulles: designing a soft floor plate is necessary but insufficient — the reversibility has to be governed as an operating covenant, or commercial and security pressures will pour concrete into it by accretion. [Source: https://www.dezeen.com/2019/11/25/stansted-airport-foster-partners-high-tech-architecture/]

**The building-type critique, first-hand and contemporary.** Banham's "The Obsolescent Airport" (*Architectural Review*, Vol. 132 No. 788, Oct. 1962, pp. 250–253) is the primary-source anchor: the airport is permanently obsolescent because "the aeronautics industry [is] continually creating new aircraft to respond to new needs that the airport infrastructure simply couldn't keep up [with]." Published in the month the Saarinen monuments were being celebrated, this is the skeptic's voice already in the room in 1962 — and it is the thesis's voice. [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reyner_Banham]

**Later scholarship (interpretation, distinguish from the above).** The academic frame the strategist can lean on is the "obsolescence of the monument" literature — the argument that the *iconic* airport terminal is structurally most exposed to functional obsolescence precisely because its image is load-bearing and cannot be edited. This is retrospective scholarship, not primary record, but it generalizes the TWA-vs-Dulles contrast into a rule: the more the architecture *is* the processing concept, the faster it dates. [Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280849328_The_obsolescence_of_the_monument_the_future_of_airport_icons]

## 3. The two or three cases that most directly frame the thesis

**Case A — Dulles itself (Saarinen 1962 / SOM 1996 / AeroTrain 2010): the durable/volatile split, pre-run.**
One building contains both halves of the run's framework and their verdicts. The *shell* was designed soft (extendable roof bay, provisioned foundations) and returned 60 years of adaptability. The *processing regime* was designed hard (compact terminal + mobile-lounge monopoly, no concourse) and was expensively reversed. The strategist's sharpest line is available here: *the rebuild is a chance to be right about the half of the 1962 building that was wrong, without discarding the half that was right.* Lock the structural grid, level changes, apron geometry, and vertical/utility spines the way Saarinen locked his extensible bay. Refuse to express the 2026 processing sequence (curb → check-in → central checkpoint → gate) in permanent structure the way Saarinen expressed the mobile lounge — because the mobile lounge is proof that the era's confident processing diagram is the thing that dates. [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport_Main_Terminal]

**Case B — TWA vs. Stansted: the two doctrines, isolated.**
TWA (Saarinen, 1962) and Stansted (Foster, 1991) are the cleanest A/B in the canon. TWA fused architecture and processing concept into one sculpted, finished object; when the jet and the jumbo changed the processing requirement, the object could only be preserved, not adapted — it is now a hotel lobby. Stansted separated the permanent (shell + undercroft) from the reconfigurable (floor plate) and remains a working terminal that has absorbed 30 years of changed security and retail regimes — imperfectly, because the softness was not defended. For a $22B rebuild, this pair is the argument that *legibility of the split is the design deliverable*, and that a signature roofline is worth having only if it is doing Foster's job (enclosure) rather than Saarinen-at-TWA's job (freezing the diagram). [Source: https://www.archdaily.com/788012/ad-classics-twa-flight-center-eero-saarinen] [Source: https://www.fosterandpartners.com/projects/stansted-airport/]

**Case C — The megaterminal displacement (post-1980 corporate planning; IST/BER/LGA as the run's named specimens).**
The architectural-philosophical point is not that these buildings are ugly; it is that the discipline that asks "which commitments are reversible?" left the terminal when authorship passed from named architects to program-management consortia. The mega-terminals lock throughput geometry — checkpoint hall size, pier length, fixed centralized security halls — into structure sized for the forecast of the drawing year. When screening distributes or dwell relocates, that geometry cannot be edited cheaply. The run's "failure taxonomy" is, architecturally, a taxonomy of *irreversibility*: in each case the fastest-dating decision is the one that fused a transient processing assumption to permanent structure. The remedy the mid-century record offers is not a return to auteurs; it is the return of the reversibility question to the program table. [Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280849328_The_obsolescence_of_the_monument_the_future_of_airport_icons]

## 4. Direct citations a strategist can use to ground the piece

1. **Banham, primary, 1962 — the thesis, stated the year Dulles opened:** airports are "the most transitional and transient building types of our time, always unfinished," obsolescent because aircraft evolve faster than buildings can. *Use to open the piece and to preempt the charge that the de-sequencing worry is a novel futurist claim — it is the founding critique of the type.* (Reyner Banham, "The Obsolescent Airport," *Architectural Review* 132/788, Oct. 1962.) [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reyner_Banham]

2. **Saarinen, primary — the aesthetic he memorialized vs. the diagram he did not:** Dulles as "a huge continuous hammock suspended between concrete trees," and "the best thing I have done." *Use to make the intent-vs-aesthetic point: the architect's own words canonize the durable shell and are silent on the processing bet that failed.* [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport_Main_Terminal]

3. **FAA program brief, primary — the flexibility mandate that produced the mobile lounge:** reduce walking distance, minimize taxiing, "maximize apron flexibility" against an observed 900-foot average terminal-to-plane walk. *Use to show that optimizing hard for apron flexibility is exactly what produced Dulles's most expensive obsolete asset — flexibility pursued through custom hardware, not through soft structure.* [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport_Main_Terminal]

4. **The extensibility provision, documented — the run's overbuild-now principle, proven:** Saarinen's foundations, ramps, and "empty openings at ground level were specifically designed to accommodate future entrances"; SOM doubled the terminal in 1996 by replicating the cable-suspension bay. *Use as the affirmative model: the cheap-now / unaffordable-later provisioning the run asks for was done here and paid off across 34 years.* [Source: https://www.som.com/projects/washington_dulles_international_airport__main_terminal_expansion]

5. **Foster on Stansted, primary — the durable/volatile doctrine as a manifesto:** services to the undercroft, structure into "trees," roof "freed simply to keep out the rain and let in light," floor plate left open and level. *Use as the design principle the Dulles rebuild should adopt: permanent = shell + service spine; soft = the processing floor — and pair it with the honest coda that Stansted's softness eroded because it was not governed as an operating covenant.* [Source: https://www.fosterandpartners.com/projects/stansted-airport/]

## 5. Where the architectural record cuts against the thesis (read this before quoting me)

- **The centralized checkpoint is not merely inertial.** The mid-century record shows that when processing was *radically* decentralized (mobile lounges), the owner spent decades and hundreds of millions re-centralizing (concourses, AeroTrain). Some of that was regulatory and security-driven; some was simple economics of staffing distributed points. The thesis should treat centralized screening as a *possibly transitional* feature, but the historical case for its durability is stronger than a pure "inertia" reading allows. Distributed processing has been tried at this site and lost once already.

- **"Design soft" is not free and not self-executing.** Stansted proves a flexible floor plate degrades without a custodian. Softness is a maintained discipline with an owner and a budget line, not a one-time architectural gift. A Dulles rebuild that leaves space "deliberately soft" must also name who defends it against retail and security accretion for 25 years, or the softness will be poured over.

- **Iconicity and adaptability are in genuine tension, not merely rhetorical tension.** TWA shows that the more the image *is* the building, the less the building can change. The run's ban on "signature roofline" recommendations is historically well-founded — but the strategist should ground it in the TWA/Dulles divergence rather than in taste. A roof can be permitted if it does Foster's job (enclosure over a free floor) and forbidden if it does TWA's job (freezing the diagram in sculpture).

*First-hand sources used: Banham 1962 (Architectural Review); Saarinen's quoted statements; FAA program parameters; Foster + Partners' own project description. Later interpretation, flagged as such: the "obsolescence of the monument" scholarship and retrospective press. The strategist should privilege the first group when grounding claims of architectural intent.*
