# Stage 1 Brief — The Operator's Chair

**Run:** Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild
**Agent:** Airport COO
**Frame:** What holds up on a Monday-morning push at 5:45 AM, regardless of which strategy is fashionable

---

I run the place. I don't get to pick the passenger-processing paradigm; I get to make whatever the strategist and the architect drew actually move airplanes and people at 05:45 on a Monday in July when the checkpoint queue is already at the escalator, one of my two international-arrivals people-movers is down for a hydraulic leak, and a line of thunderstorms is sitting on the departure corridor. The thesis in this run — that hub terminals lock in the throughput logic of the decade they were drawn in, not the one they open into — is correct, and it is the single most useful thing a strategist can say to me. But it is correct for a reason most decks get wrong. Terminals don't date because designers mispredict technology. They date because designers optimize the parts the operator doesn't control and under-build the parts the operator can't retrofit. That is the operator's version of the thesis, and it is sharper than the technology version.

---

## 1. Key Findings

1. **The durable/volatile split is really a control split.** The things with a 50-year life — column grid, level changes, apron geometry, vertical-circulation spines, conduit and power and cooling capacity — are exactly the things the airport fully controls and can never retrofit cheaply. The things with a 5–15-year life — screening regime, IT, sensing, fit-out, gate-assignment logic — are largely controlled by *someone else* (TSA, the airlines, CBP, the FAA) and change on their schedule, not mine. Future-proofing means over-building what I own and can't change, and refusing to hard-wire built form around anyone else's process I don't control.

2. **Most "capacity" numbers in the program are planning-model numbers, not held numbers.** A model gives you a peak-hour figure in benign conditions. I hold a different number on the actual peak day, in weather, with a bag system running at 92% and two gates off for a jet-bridge fault. At ATL the arrival rate falls from 166/hour in visual conditions to 109 in instrument conditions — a 34% haircut the moment the ceiling drops [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/planning_capacity/profiles]. Design to the model number and you've designed a terminal that fails on exactly the days it matters.

3. **The first 90 minutes of any IROPS event is run by people whose real authority is narrower than the plan implies.** The IROPS plan on paper is a coordination document; the plan as executed is three people in the ops center working phones and a whiteboard. The airport controls the building, the roads, the gates it hasn't leased away, and public information. It does *not* control the ground stop, the crews, or the airline's decision to cancel — and the terminal's built form either gives those three people room to improvise or it traps them.

4. **Projects get handed back to operations broken in predictable categories, and the operator usually saw it coming in design review and got overruled.** Smoke-control and fire-alarm sequencing, maintenance access to overhead and in-floor equipment, insufficient power and cooling for the IT the building will actually carry, and vertical transportation sized for aesthetics rather than surge. BER is the monument here: a smoke-extraction scheme chosen "for aesthetic reasons," 80% of doors failing acceptance, over 75,000 defects [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport].

5. **Airline relations are transactional on money and genuinely partnered only on disruption.** Gates, fees, and common-use deployment are a negotiation where the hub carrier's cost-per-enplanement is the club they swing. But at 05:45 in a meltdown, the airline duty manager and my ops duty manager are the same team. A terminal that's flexible on gate assignment (common-use-capable, not carved into exclusive-use fiefdoms) is worth more to me in IROPS than any amount of retail frontage.

6. **Deferred maintenance is not a backlog line; it's a set of failures I can already schedule.** Vertical transportation, baggage, jet bridges, and HVAC fail on curves I can see coming. An elevator outage is not an inconvenience — it's an immediate ADA violation and a wheelchair passenger who can't reach the gate [Source: https://oxmaint.com/industries/aviation-management/elevator-escalator-iot-monitoring-airport-terminals]. Airports carrying more than a 15% preventive-maintenance backlog see reactive work orders spike 60–90 days later, every time [Source: https://oxmaint.com/industries/aviation-management/elevator-escalator-iot-monitoring-airport-terminals].

7. **The Dulles-specific operational fact is that IAD already ran the future the thesis is worried about — and it aged badly.** The mobile lounge was a 1962 bet that processing and transport should be decoupled and distributed. It was elegant, it was Saarinen, and operations has spent sixty years and, most recently, $160M refurbishing it while it still can't reach Concourse D efficiently [Source: https://aeroxplorer.com/articles/dulles-airports-mobile-lounges-are-here-to-stay-160-million-refurbishment.php]. The rebuild's instinct to kill the mobile lounge and extend the Aerotrain is the operator finally winning an argument that was lost in 1962. That is the cautionary tale sitting on the property.

## 2. Evidence

**Division structure and where the lines sit.** A large-hub operations division splits into airfield operations (movement area, ARFF interface, Part 139 self-inspection), terminal operations (landside, concessions liaison, passenger flow), airline/properties relations, facilities and maintenance, and engineering/planning. The contested seams are always the same: who owns the baggage system (airline-maintained on airline-leased equipment vs. airport-owned common-use), who owns the checkpoint queue real estate (TSA screens, but the airport built and owns the space and eats the crowding), and who owns the ramp (typically the airline or its handler inside the leasehold, the airport outside it). What centralizes under the COO is anything that crosses tenant lines — the airfield, roadways, utilities, the ops center, emergency management. What pushes out is anything inside a leasehold. The FAA's terminal planning guidance, AC 150/5360-13A, frames the building around processor sequencing but is explicit that level-of-service and flexibility are design obligations, not afterthoughts [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC-150-5360-13A-Airport-Terminal-Planning.pdf].

**What the operator actually controls.** This is the framing the thesis needs up front. The airport controls: the building and its fixed form, the aprons and taxiways it maintains, the roadways and curb, gate assignment on common-use gates, public information, and the ops center. The FAA Air Traffic controls: runway acceptance rates, ground stops, ground delay programs, miles-in-trail. The airlines control: their schedules, their crews, their decision to cancel or hold, and equipment inside their leaseholds including — critically — most bag systems and jet bridges. Host jurisdictions control: off-airport roads, mutual-aid fire and police, and utility feeds. When the December 2017 Atlanta outage started with a fire in a Georgia Power underground vault that took out two substations including the "redundant" one, the airport controlled almost nothing about the cause and everything about the eleven hours of consequence [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2017/12/17/us/atlanta-airport-power-outage/index.html]. Strategy decks that assume the airport can "deliver a seamless journey" are assuming control the operator does not have.

**IROPS execution — plan vs. reality.** ACRP Report 65 is the canonical IROPS contingency guidebook, and it is a coordination-and-communication document by design — checklists, templates, aviation-partner roles [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/14667/guidebook-for-airport-irregular-operations-irops-contingency-planning]. The FAA Modernization and Reform Act of 2012 requires airport operators to file tarmac-delay contingency plans every five years [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/14667/guidebook-for-airport-irregular-operations-irops-contingency-planning]. The gap between plan and execution is authority. The plan says "coordinate with air carriers to deplane"; the reality is my duty manager has no authority to move an airline's aircraft, only to offer a hardstand, stairs, buses, and a place to put people. The Delta August 2016 outage — a six-hour data-center failure that grounded 451-plus flights starting at 02:38 — landed on airports as a terminal-management problem the airport could not fix at the source [Source: https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/tens-of-thousands-of-delta-passengers-grounded-by-outage/]. The building's job in that first 90 minutes is to give the ops center room: circulation that doesn't gridlock when a bank can't push, gate frontage that can absorb held aircraft, hold-room and floor area that can take a passenger surge without becoming a life-safety problem, and power/comms that survive the event.

**Capital handoff from the operator's side.** The recurring categories of pain are boringly consistent. (1) Fire and smoke control that was designed to look invisible and therefore doesn't work — BER's smoke-extraction scheme was routed down through the basement "for aesthetic reasons," failed its acceptance test when flaps didn't actuate and fans imploded ducting, and helped hold the building shut for years amid 75,000-plus defects [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport]. (2) Maintenance access — equipment mounted where you need a lift and a taxiway closure to reach it. (3) Vertical transportation sized to a model average, not a surge, so an escalator or elevator outage instantly becomes an ADA and flow failure [Source: https://oxmaint.com/industries/aviation-management/elevator-escalator-iot-monitoring-airport-terminals]. (4) IT power and cooling under-provisioned because the electrical load of the sensing/screening/common-use build-out was a "later" problem in design. The operator flags all four in design review. They lose because the flags cost capital now and the pain lands on an operating budget later, on someone else's watch. Where the operator could have pushed back and didn't: the design-development phase, before the smoke-control narrative and the maintainability plan were frozen. After that, it's a change order.

**Airline relations, day to day.** Transactional: gate leases, landing fees, cost-per-enplanement, the annual fight over signatory rates. Partnered: de-ice sequencing, fuel coordination, and every IROPS event. Common-use deployment is where the two collide — airports increasingly find common-use gates give a better return on the asset and let them onboard a new carrier in days instead of a lease cycle [Source: https://veovo.com/insights/articles/rate-making-and-gate-management]. Stand economics are unforgiving: an occupancy of 60–120 minutes per turn and 6–8 turns per stand per day is the difference between a gate that pays and a gate that sits [Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Gate_(airport)]. The operator wants common-use flexibility; the hub carrier wants exclusive-use certainty. Whoever wins that argument in the lease sets the terminal's real capacity ceiling far more than the column grid does.

**Maintenance reliability realities.** Baggage is the marquee case. Heathrow T5 claims ~99% reliability and still buried the terminal in ~20,000 delayed bags when the automated system faulted — because 99% of a system that touches every departing bag is a meltdown several times a year [Source: https://airportindustry-news.com/heathrow-baggage-system-failure-raises-questions-over-operational-resilience/]. Denver is the other end: an over-automated baggage system so unreliable it was largely abandoned for manual handling [Source: https://www.cs.vassar.edu/~cs335/Crisis/Denver.pdf]. Jet bridges, escalators, elevators, and HVAC follow known failure curves; the operator already knows which units are near end-of-life. The ACI-NA 2023 needs study put U.S. airport infrastructure needs at $151.1 billion over five years, up more than 30% from $115B two years earlier, with terminals the single largest share at 30–50% of need — much of it renewal and reconstruction, not growth [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/press_release/new-aci-na-report-highlights-heightened-urgency-to-modernize-americas-airports/].

**Throughput as the operator sees it.** Latent capacity almost always lives in three places the model undercounts: gate utilization (moving from exclusive to common-use turns idle frontage into turns), off-peak schedule smoothing, and recovery capacity (the ability to reabsorb a delayed bank). Genuine constraint lives in the runway when the weather turns — the 166-to-109 arrival-rate collapse at ATL is the whole story [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/planning_capacity/profiles] — and in the fixed circulation and checkpoint footprint when a surge hits. The number I hold on a peak day is the constrained one, and no amount of terminal glass changes the runway acceptance rate.

## 3. The Operational Case — For and Against the Thesis

**For (why the operator wants this thesis in the program meeting).** The thesis tells the design team to separate what must be locked from what should be left soft. That is exactly how an operator thinks about a 50-year building. Over-build the durable, controllable spine — structure, level changes, apron geometry, and above all utility, power, cooling, and conduit capacity — because I can never retrofit those without closing the building, and the airport actually controls them. Leave the volatile, other-controlled layers soft — screening zones, gate technology, fit-out — because TSA, the airlines, and CBP will change them on their own clock and I need to swap them without demolition. If Dulles builds generous vertical spines, over-provisioned electrical and cooling rooms, deep raised floors or accessible ceilings for the sensing build-out, and gate modules that can flex between contact and remote and between narrowbody and widebody, then the operator can absorb whatever screening and gate paradigm actually arrives in 2035 and 2050. The thesis, taken seriously, is a maintainability-and-flexibility argument dressed in futurism, and the operator will sign it.

**Against (where the thesis runs into the ramp).** Three cautions. First, "screening decentralizes" is a bet on an authority the airport does not hold. TSA owns the screening regime, and the operator cannot design out the centralized checkpoint on the assumption it goes away — if you build for distributed screening and TSA keeps the central model, you've built dead space and orphaned circulation. Design the *provisioning* for distributed screening (power, comms, floor loading, sightlines at gate level) but do not delete the central checkpoint's footprint. Provisioning is reversible; deleting built form is not. Second, "the gate-to-aircraft relationship stops being 1:1" is operationally attractive but only if the apron and taxiway geometry — the durable, expensive-to-move layer — is drawn for remote-stand and bus/mobile-lounge flexibility now. IAD already learned that a distributed transport model is a permanent operating cost, not a one-time capital saving: sixty years and $160M later, the mobile lounges still can't reach Concourse D cleanly [Source: https://aeroxplorer.com/articles/dulles-airports-mobile-lounges-are-here-to-stay-160-million-refurbishment.php]. Third, "dwell monetizes differently / processing migrates to the device" is a revenue and staffing bet, not a structural one — but if the building is *sized around* compressed dwell and it doesn't compress, or the tech slips, the operator is left holding a terminal too small for the surge on the peak day. Size the building for the surge you must hold in IROPS, and let commercial revenue be a soft, reconfigurable layer inside it. The most dangerous line in any future-proofing deck is the one that shrinks fixed capacity on the strength of a technology arrival date nobody can name.

## 4. Concrete Examples

**A. The BER smoke-control handoff — the operator's nightmare in one system.** Berlin routed smoke extraction down through the basement rather than out the roof "for aesthetic reasons." At acceptance, flaps didn't actuate, fans over-pressurized and imploded ducting, and 80% of doors failed; the building carried 75,000-plus defects and stayed shut for years [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport]. For Dulles: freeze the smoke-control and fire-alarm sequencing narrative in design development with the operator and the fire marshal in the room, and never let an architectural gesture drive a life-safety system. This is the handoff category that keeps a finished building from opening.

**B. The Atlanta 2017 power outage — the redundancy that wasn't.** A Georgia Power vault fire took out two substations, including the redundant feed, and closed the world's busiest airport for roughly eleven hours [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2017/12/17/us/atlanta-airport-power-outage/index.html]. The operator controlled nothing about the cause. For Dulles: physical separation and true independence of primary and backup power feeds is a durable, unaffordable-to-retrofit commitment — build it now, route the feeds apart, and don't let value engineering collapse them into one vault. This is exactly the kind of cheap-now, impossible-later provisioning the thesis should elevate.

**C. Heathrow T5 baggage — 99% is a meltdown schedule.** T5 opened in 2008 into a baggage meltdown and has faulted since despite a claimed 99% reliability, once delaying ~20,000 bags in a single event [Source: https://airportindustry-news.com/heathrow-baggage-system-failure-raises-questions-over-operational-resilience/]. For Dulles: design the bag system for graceful degradation and manual fallback, with physical space for a manual sort, because the automated system *will* fail on a peak day and the question is only whether the building lets ramp recover.

**D. The IAD mobile lounge — a distributed-transport bet that never stopped costing.** The 1962 decoupling of processing from transport was visionary and is now a $160M-refurbished operating liability that still can't serve Concourse D via Aerotrain [Source: https://aeroxplorer.com/articles/dulles-airports-mobile-lounges-are-here-to-stay-160-million-refurbishment.php; https://www.thetransportpolitic.com/2010/01/26/dulles-airport-replaces-distinctive-mobile-lounge-system-with-aerotrain/]. For Dulles: extending the Aerotrain to all concourses in the $22B rebuild is the operator winning a sixty-year argument [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/]. The lesson for *this* program is the same — a distributed or de-sequenced model that looks elegant on paper becomes the operator's permanent labor line unless the durable apron and circulation geometry is drawn to support it from day one.

**E. Southwest December 2022 — the meltdown the building can't fix but must survive.** SkySolver and a point-to-point network turned a winter storm into 16,700-plus cancellations, a $140M DOT fine, and a $1.3B IT overhaul [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Southwest_Airlines_scheduling_crisis]. The airport controlled none of the cause. For Dulles: the terminal's obligation in a carrier meltdown is to hold the surge — circulation, hold-room and floor area, restrooms, power, and information systems that don't fail when a full bank can't push. You cannot design out an airline's crew-scheduling collapse; you can design a building that doesn't become a life-safety incident when one happens.

---

### The operator's bottom line for the strategist

The thesis is right that Dulles risks opening dated. But it will not open dated because the designers mis-forecast biometrics or eVTOL. It will open dated if it hard-wires built form around processes the airport doesn't control (TSA screening, airline gate logic, CBP), under-provisions the utility and power and cooling spine the airport *does* control and can't retrofit, and sizes fixed capacity down on the strength of technology arrival dates nobody can name. Over-build the durable, controllable, unaffordable-to-retrofit layer. Leave the volatile, other-controlled layer soft and swappable. And size the building for the surge you have to hold at 05:45 on the worst Monday in July — because that is the day the terminal is actually for.
