# Infrastructure Economist — Evidence Brief

**Run:** Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild
**Lens:** Capital expenditure economics, project cost behavior, cost overruns, and infrastructure ROI
**Date:** 2026-07-08

---

## Framing: the number that should anchor every design conversation

Washington Dulles (IAD) currently runs one of the lowest airline cost-per-enplanement (CPE) figures of any US large hub: **$9.56 in FY2025**, down from $12.88 in FY2024, on roughly $3.7B of outstanding debt and 14.3M enplanements [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. For comparison, FY2024 actual CPE ranged from $3.94 at Atlanta to $30.16 at LAX, with ORD at $29.56 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe].

The proposed program is **$22 billion — $21.8B in new bonds plus $1.1B in airport fees, with inflation and future interest already baked in** [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority (MWAA) today carries about $4.71B of principal (≈$5.11B with unamortized premium), or roughly $4.9B by mid-2025 convention [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. The program therefore proposes to issue **roughly four to five times the airport's entire existing debt stack**, at a hub whose defining commercial advantage is that it is *cheap for airlines to fly*. That tension — a low-CPE hub proposing a debt program larger than most sovereign airport builds — is the economic spine of this run. Every "future-proofing" decision either protects that CPE advantage or spends it.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **The $22B program is larger than the entire cost of Istanbul or Beijing Daxing.** IST's new airport was built for ~$12B across four phases [Source: https://www.cnbc.com/2019/04/08/photos-inside-12-billion-istanbul-new-airport-biggest-in-the-world.html]; Daxing's terminal-and-airfield core ran ~$11.4–17B [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing_Daxing_International_Airport]. Dulles proposes to spend more than either to rebuild a 29M-passenger airport [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Scale alone does not make the program wrong, but it removes any claim that the price is normal.

2. **Cost estimates for this exact airport diverge by more than $35B.** In RFI responses, Ferrovial priced the rebuild at **$14.4B (AACE range $11.5B–$18.7B)** while Phoenix/Ironbridge priced it at **$35–50B** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. When qualified bidders disagree by 3x on the same scope, the $22B headline is a negotiating anchor, not an engineering estimate. Treat it as ±50%.

3. **The mega-terminal overrun record is the base rate, not the tail risk.** Berlin Brandenburg opened ~9–10 years late at ~€7B against a ~€2–2.83B budget — a **2.3–2.6x overrun** [Source: https://www.euronews.com/2020/10/31/berlin-airport-opens-10-years-late-and-three-times-over-budget]. The LAX Automated People Mover went from a $1.9B construction budget to **$3.34B (+76%)** and slid from a 2023 to a 2026 opening [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report]. DFW Terminal F grew from **$1.6B to ~$4.0B** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation]. The program's own note that $22B does *not* include "any potential delays or cost overruns" should be read against this record [Source: https://byerussell.substack.com/p/a-22-billion-program-for-dulles].

4. **Airport construction cost inflation is outrunning both CPI and airport revenue.** Airport construction costs rose **38.3% from 2014–2024 vs. 31.7% general CPI** — a 6.6-point gap — with the ENR Building Cost Index up 14.8% in 2022 alone [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation]. An 8–9 year build exposes the program to compounding escalation that the fixed $22B figure cannot absorb without scope cuts.

5. **The debt math implies a CPE regime change.** Dulles' current debt-per-enplanement is **$256** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. Financing ~$21.8B of new bonds against ~14.3M enplanements — before demand growth and net of amortization — is an order-of-magnitude increase in debt intensity. MWAA operates a hybrid residual rate model with "no contractual cap on the cost level that can be recovered through the rate formula," so incremental debt service passes mechanically to airlines [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. That is what puts the required **65% airline-approval threshold** at risk.

6. **Dulles enters the program financially strong, which is exactly why the risk is asymmetric.** MWAA's aviation enterprise posted **3.16x debt-service coverage in 2024** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis], against a US airport median DSCR of 1.56x for FY2024 and median days-cash-on-hand of 625 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-bond-ratings]. The airport is not spending from weakness; it is spending down a strong balance sheet. The downside is that a low-CPE, high-coverage hub has the *most* to lose from a program that inverts both metrics.

7. **On-time, on-budget mega-projects exist — and they are disciplined on scope, not ambition.** Kansas City's 40-gate single terminal (~1M sq ft) opened February 2023 for **$1.5B, on time and on budget** [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date]. The distinguishing variable in the failure cases is almost always novel, integrated systems on a critical path (BER's bespoke smoke-extraction; Denver's automated baggage; LAX's P3 dispute structure) — not square footage.

8. **The opportunity-cost frame is national, not local.** $22B is roughly **one-third of the entire FAA NPIAS 2023–2027 estimate of $62.4B in AIP-eligible development for ~3,300 US airports** [Source: https://acconline.org/industry-news-new-faa-npias-identifies-over-62-billion-in-aip-eligible-needs/]. It is ~14 Kansas City terminals. The relevant question is not only "is this the best use of MWAA's capital" but "what does concentrating this much capital in one hub crowd out."

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 The program as proposed

The May 2026 plan expands the main terminal (300-foot extensions east and west), adds four new linear concourses, extends the underground AeroTrain to eliminate mobile lounges for regular operations, and removes the "temporary" Concourse C-D. Targeted completion is 2034 (roughly 8 years) [Source: https://byerussell.substack.com/p/a-22-billion-program-for-dulles]. The first phase — a new concourse plus renovation of Concourse A linked to the main terminal by an above-ground connector — is reported at **$6.2B, with construction potentially starting April 2027** [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/].

Funding is reported as **$21.8B in new bonds plus $1.1B in airport fees, inclusive of inflation and future interest** [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. This supersedes an existing MWAA master plan that contemplated ~$7B in capital improvements phased across a much longer horizon — the new proposal is a dramatic acceleration and enlargement [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/05/12/report-new-plan-to-overhaul-dulles-airport-would-eliminate-mobile-lounges/]. As of late May 2026 the plan "had not been followed by a formal RFQ, RFP, or published financing plan," so debt structure and any federal contribution remain undocumented [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis].

### 2.2 Dulles' current financial position (the baseline being spent)

FY2025 (per DWU's IAD profile, drawing on MWAA filings): CPE **$9.56**; outstanding debt **$3.7B**; debt-per-enplanement **$256**; enplanements **14.3M**; unrestricted cash **$1.1B**; total operating revenue **$516.4M**; operating expenses ex-depreciation **$330.9M**; operating income **$16.7M**; capital expenditures **$494.1M** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. FY2024 CPE was **$12.88**, so Dulles is currently *reducing* airline cost as traffic recovers [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad].

Traffic context: Dulles handled **27.25M total passengers in 2024** (an 8.4% jump and an all-time record surpassing 2005's 27.05M), and **29.01M in 2025** [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2025/03/03/dulles-airports-2024-passenger-total-set-new-all-time-record/] [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. United holds roughly **70% market share**, which concentrates approval risk in a single carrier's cost tolerance [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_United_Airlines].

Note the historical warning: Dulles spent the 1990s–2000s adding capacity (the 1997 expansion nearly tripled handling capacity to ~40M/year) and then spent the early 2010s *losing* domestic passengers to Reagan National after the perimeter rule loosened — ~200,000 passengers lost to DCA between 2011 and 2013 [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport]. Dulles has a lived record of building ahead of demand that then failed to arrive on schedule. That is directly relevant to a program sized, in the Phoenix/Ironbridge variant, for **50M+ annual passengers** — roughly double 2025 volume [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis].

### 2.3 The overrun and schedule-slip record

- **Berlin Brandenburg (BER):** scheduled to open October 2011, opened October 2020 (~9–10 years late); budget ~€2–2.83B, final cost ~€6.5–7.3B (2.3–2.6x). Primary technical cause: a bespoke smoke-extraction system that never passed certification as designed [Source: https://www.euronews.com/2020/10/31/berlin-airport-opens-10-years-late-and-three-times-over-budget].
- **Heathrow T5:** opened March 2008 at ~£4.3B. The building was delivered, but the baggage/IT integration failed on day one — BA cancelled 34 flights immediately, over 500 flights within ten days, and ~42,000 bags failed to travel with their owners [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Terminal_5]. The lesson is operational, not structural: the concrete was fine; the systems weren't.
- **Denver automated baggage:** ~$560M over budget, 16 months late; ultimately used for one concourse, one airline, outbound only, and decommissioned in 2005 [Source: https://www.wrike.com/blog/lessons-learned-from-project-failure-at-denver-international-airport-why-checking-bags-is-still-a-pain/].
- **LAX Automated People Mover:** $1.9B construction budget → **$3.34B total (+76%)**; originally due 2023, now targeting October 2026; failure mode was a DBFOM P3 dispute structure, not physical construction [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report] [Source: https://abc7.com/post/lax-people-movers-budget-increases-400-million-amid/15071618/].
- **IAD's own AeroTrain:** the 3.78-mile people mover opened January 2010 at **~$1.4–1.5B** [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AeroTrain_(Dulles_International_Airport)] [Source: https://airportimprovement.com/article/dulles-debuts-15-billion-train-system/]. Extending it to new concourses is a core cost driver of the new program, and Dulles has direct institutional memory of what that costs.
- **Counter-case — Kansas City (MCI):** 40 gates, ~1M sq ft, **$1.5B, on time and on budget**, opened February 2023 [Source: https://www.kcur.org/news/2023-02-02/kansas-city-airport-new-terminal-expensive-kci-budget-opening-date] [Source: https://flykc.com/newsroom/news-releases/new-40-gate-terminal-at-kansas-city-international-airport].

The pattern across failures: the fastest-dating, most-overrunning decision is almost always a **novel integrated system on the critical path** — automated baggage (Denver), bespoke life-safety (BER), a first-of-kind P3 people mover (LAX). Poured structure and column grids rarely blow up budgets; the "smart," "seamless," future-proofing systems do.

### 2.4 Macro cost environment

- Airport construction costs rose **38.3% (2014–2024) vs. 31.7% CPI**; ENR BCI compounded ~33% over 2019–2024, with +14.8% in 2022 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation].
- 2025–2026 tariff pressure: Section 232 steel tariffs at 50% (effective June 2025), estimated 6–12% cost pressure on structural-steel and equipment-heavy scopes; steel +15–25% YoY in Q1 2026 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation].
- National need is escalating: FAA NPIAS **2023–2027 = $62.4B**, up 43% (~$19B) from the prior cycle [Source: https://acconline.org/industry-news-new-faa-npias-identifies-over-62-billion-in-aip-eligible-needs/]; DWU reports the 2025–2029 figure at **$67.5B, up 92% since FY2019** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation].
- Globally, ACI projects **~$2.4 trillion in airport CapEx needed 2021–2040**; annual global airport CapEx reached ~$40B in 2023, ~1% above 2019 [Source: https://www.airport-technology.com/news/aci-world-capital-expenditure-passenger-demand/] [Source: https://blog.aci.aero/airport-capacity/airports-need-significant-capital-expenditure-to-alleviate-slot-constraints/].
- Federal funding tools are eroding: the PFC cap has been **$4.50 since 2000**, with real purchasing power down to ~$2.72 by 2018 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation]. Airports increasingly self-fund large programs with bonds — which is exactly what the $21.8B Dulles bond figure reflects.

### 2.5 Credit and debt-service context

US airport revenue-bond medians strengthened post-COVID: **DSCR 1.56x for FY2024** (up from 1.42x in 2020) and **median days-cash-on-hand 625** in FY2024 (up from 545 in FY2019) [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-bond-ratings]. Fitch's DSCR rating bands: AAA >2.0x, AA 1.5–2.0x, A 1.25–1.50x, BBB 1.0–1.25x [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-bond-ratings]. MWAA's aviation enterprise coverage of **3.16x in 2024** sits comfortably in AAA territory today [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis] — a cushion the program would draw down substantially.

## 3. The opportunity-cost argument

Three framings, each numerically grounded:

1. **Against national need.** $22B ≈ **one-third of the entire NPIAS 2023–2027 national AIP-eligible need ($62.4B)** across ~3,300 airports [Source: https://acconline.org/industry-news-new-faa-npias-identifies-over-62-billion-in-aip-eligible-needs/]. Concentrating that capital in one hub is a policy choice with a real distributional cost, even if it is MWAA's own bond capacity rather than federal grant money.

2. **Against the durable/volatile split (the run's central design question).** Every dollar spent overbuilding a *volatile* system (screening layout sized for today's centralized checkpoint, IT/sensing fit-out, gate-hold geometry sized for a 1:1 gate-aircraft ratio) is a dollar not available to overbuild a *durable* one (structural bays, level changes, apron geometry, utility/power/comms conduit that is unaffordable to retrofit later). The failure taxonomy says the same thing: the systems that dated fastest at BER, Denver, and LAX were precisely the "smart" integrated ones. From a capital-allocation view, **overbuild the conduit and the column grid; keep the security regime and the fit-out soft and cheap to replace.** That is not a hedge against technology forecasting error — it is the economically rational response to it.

3. **Against Dulles' own income.** FY2025 operating income was **$16.7M** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. A $22B program is more than **1,000x** the airport's current annual operating income and ~43x its annual operating revenue. The program is only serviceable through decades of rate-base recovery from airlines — which is why the CPE trajectory and the 65% airline-approval vote, not the architecture, are the binding constraints.

## 4. Caveats and limitations

- **I did not open the MWAA 2024/2025 ACFR directly.** Dulles' CPE ($9.56), debt-per-enplanement ($256), and DSCR (3.16x) come from DWU Consulting's compilation of MWAA filings and FAA CATS data, not from the primary ACFR PDF [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/iad]. DWU cites primary sources but is a secondary aggregator; a strategist should verify the CPE and debt figures against MWAA's audited statements before quoting them to a board. **Flag: the single most load-bearing number in this brief — current CPE — is secondary-sourced.**
- **Enplanements vs. passengers.** DWU reports 14.3M enplanements (FY2025); MWAA reports 27.25M total passengers (2024) and 29.01M (2025). These reconcile (enplanements ≈ half of total passengers), but any per-enplanement figure must be applied to the enplanement base, not the total-passenger base, or it will double-count.
- **The $22B is a political announcement, not a costed program.** It carries "no contractual cap" language, no published financing plan, and no formal procurement as of late May 2026 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/dulles-rfi-analysis]. Bidder estimates span $14.4B to $50B. I have modeled the *economics of the announced figure*, not a validated program cost.
- **I could not find published CPE-impact modeling for the $22B program specifically.** The claim that it "changes the CPE regime" is my inference from the debt-to-enplanement arithmetic plus the residual rate structure, not a figure MWAA has published. Stated as inference, not fact.
- **International cost comparisons are imperfect.** IST and Daxing were greenfield builds in lower-labor-cost environments with different scope boundaries (Daxing's figure swings from $11.4B to $63B depending on whether ground-access infrastructure is included) [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing_Daxing_International_Airport]. Use them for order-of-magnitude context, not line-item benchmarking.
- **No demand forecast validated here.** Whether 50M+ annual passengers is a realistic 2050 target for Dulles is outside my lens; the operations-analyst and airline-commercial-strategist should own it. My point is narrower: if that demand does not arrive on schedule, Dulles has done this before (the post-2011 DCA leakage), and the debt service does not wait for the traffic.
