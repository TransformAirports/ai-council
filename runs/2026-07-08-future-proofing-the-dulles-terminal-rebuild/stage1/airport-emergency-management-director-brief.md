# Stage 1 Brief — Emergency Management Director's Chair

**Run:** Future-Proofing the Dulles Terminal Rebuild
**Lens:** Director of Emergency Management, top-tier US hub (25 years, prior large hub before that)
**Question I am answering:** Does the thesis — that terminals date because they lock built form around a passenger-processing sequence that is decentralizing and de-sequencing — strengthen or erode the single most undervalued capability at a US hub, which is operational emergency-management readiness? And which built-form decisions in a $22B Dulles rebuild are the ones a program meeting will value-engineer out first and regret for fifty years?

My answer up front: the thesis is **correct and it cuts toward my chair**. Decentralizing screening and de-sequencing flow does not just move the retail and the queues around. It dissolves the two assumptions the current emergency concept of operations is quietly built on — one controllable chokepoint (the central checkpoint) and one predictable crowd location. If the design team optimizes for processing speed and dwell revenue and treats resilience as a fit-out item, Dulles opens in 2034 with a 2050 passenger experience and a 1995 emergency posture. The durable-vs-volatile framework the run is asking for is the *same* framework I use to decide what to harden. EM belongs in the "lock it now, unaffordable to retrofit" column. It almost never gets put there.

---

## 1. Key findings (framed for a peer)

- **The EOC, the power topology, and the communications backbone are 50-year decisions dressed up as IT line items.** ACRP Research Report 189 exists precisely because airports keep siting and specifying EOCs *after* the structure is fixed, then live with a windowless conference room next to a mechanical chase for thirty years [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25280/design-considerations-for-airport-eocs]. Location, structural hardening, and physically separated utility paths are column-grid-and-conduit decisions. You cannot value-engineer them back in.

- **ATL 2017 is the whole argument in one day.** The "redundant" feed and the primary feed ran through the *same* Georgia Power tunnel; one fire took both, and the world's busiest airport went dark for ~11 hours, ~1,183 cancellations, ~30,000 people affected [Source: https://www.ajc.com/news/atlanta-airport-blog/gao-studied-airport-power-outages-in-wake-of-hartsfield-jackson-blackout/LXTBMWTZ2BEGZLD3VDDSATL6Q4/]. Redundancy that shares a physical path is not redundancy. That is a routing/geometry decision made at design, not an operations decision made at 1:06 p.m.

- **FLL 2017 shows the built-form failure mode of decentralization before we even decentralize.** When the sterile boundary was breached, hundreds of passengers self-evacuated *onto active aprons, taxiways, and a runway, then through a breached perimeter fence* [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale_airport_shooting]. That is a preview of what a distributed-screening terminal does under panic: more sterile/non-sterile seams, more places a crowd can spill into live airside. The Broward AAR was written to HSEEP standard and its lessons are about command, communications interoperability, and reunification — none of which the terminal's geometry supported [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf].

- **CrowdStrike (July 19, 2024) is the continuity-of-operations warning shot for a device-and-software processing model.** A single bad update grounded United, Delta, and American; Delta canceled 7,000+ flights over five days and stranded 1.3M passengers because the manual fallback could not carry the load [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption]. The thesis wants processing to migrate onto the passenger's device and off-airport. Every increment of that migration raises the blast radius when the software fails — unless the building still supports a manual, degraded-mode fallback. That fallback needs physical space and power the "seamless" model assumes away.

- **Hurricane response (MCO/RSW, Ian 2022) proves the terminal is an emergency *facility*, not just a processing facility.** GOAA activated its EOC, designated a ride-out crew to shelter inside the terminal through the storm, and had to bus ~100 stranded guests to real shelters because the airport is explicitly *not* a public shelter [Source: https://www.orlandoairports.net/press/2024/10/07/hurricane-milton-update-2-orlando-international-to-cease-commercial-operations-8-am-wednesday-october-9/]. A hub in a hurricane, wildfire-smoke, or grid-failure future needs designated hardened survivability space for the people who stay. That is square footage and structural spec, decided now.

- **The regulatory floor is real but low, and it will not save a bad building.** 14 CFR 139.325 requires an Airport Emergency Plan and a full-scale exercise every 36 months, with annual tabletops in between; FAA AC 150/5200-31C gives the functional-annex structure (command/control, communications, protective actions, health/medical, etc.) [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.325] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/150_5200_31c_chg1.pdf]. Compliance produces a binder. It does not produce evacuation geometry, a hardened EOC, or separated utility paths. The building either has those bones or it does not.

- **EM gets cut first because it generates no revenue and is rarely capital-eligible in the obvious way.** In a $21.8B-bonded, $1.1B-fee program [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/], the EOC and its redundancy are a rounding error that competes against revenue-generating concessions and gate area. When budgets tighten, the hardened alternate EOC, the second physically separated comms path, and the mass-care space are exactly what a value-engineering pass removes — because their return only shows up on the worst day.

- **Institutional memory is the uninsurable asset.** The relationship capital that runs the first 30 minutes — the FBI SAC's cell, the airline duty managers, the county EM, the FAA ATC watch — is not in the binder. A rebuild that spans 8+ years of construction *and* a likely EM-leadership transition inside that window is a double continuity risk the program is not pricing.

## 2. Evidence

**Doctrine and the regulatory floor.** 14 CFR 139.325 obligates every Class I certificate holder to maintain an AEP and exercise it triennially at full scale, coordinated with mutual-aid police, ARFF, medical, hospitals, and principal tenants [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.325]. AC 150/5200-31C is the how-to; note that it is a 2009 document, meaning the federal guidance a 2034 terminal will be certified against was written for a centralized-processing world [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/150_5200_31c_chg1.pdf]. That gap — modern building, legacy emergency doctrine — is the thesis restated in my language.

**EOC design as a capital decision.** ACRP RR 189 frames EOC planning across physical space, technology, function, operations, and governance, with a "Concept and Development Planning Decision Tree" — its very existence is the field admitting that EOCs get bolted on late and designed wrong [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25280/design-considerations-for-airport-eocs]. The companion continuity literature (ACRP Report 93 on prolonged disruptions; Synthesis 78 on continuity of operations) treats sustained operability as a planning discipline, not a generator you buy later [Source: https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/acrp/docs/CompilationReport.pdf].

**ATL 2017 — redundancy that shared a path.** The fire in a Georgia Power underground vault "impacted cables in both directions and burned a wall through which the cables travel, taking out both the main system and the backup system" [Source: https://www.ajc.com/news/atlanta-airport-blog/gao-studied-airport-power-outages-in-wake-of-hartsfield-jackson-blackout/LXTBMWTZ2BEGZLD3VDDSATL6Q4/]. GAO subsequently studied airport power resilience nationally. The design lesson is spatial: dual feeds must not share a chase, a tunnel, or a wall. That is a routing decision locked at the structural/utility-spine stage the run correctly identifies as 50-year-life.

**FLL 2017 — the geometry of panic.** Five killed at Terminal 2 baggage claim, ~36 more injured in the ensuing stampede; the crowd broke through the sterile boundary onto aprons, taxiways, a runway, and out a perimeter fence [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale_airport_shooting]. The AAR, built to DHS HSEEP standard by Broward County Aviation, catalogs command, interoperable-communications, and family-reunification failures [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. Read it as a distributed-screening dress rehearsal: the more sterile/non-sterile seams you create, the more edges a panicked crowd can breach, and the more the building must pre-answer *where do 3,000 people go and how do we account for them*.

**CrowdStrike 2024 — continuity when the processing layer dies.** United/Delta/American ground stops on July 19; Delta alone canceled 7,000+ flights over five days, 1.3M passengers, because crew-tracking and check-in required manual, machine-by-machine recovery [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption] [Source: https://www.ciodive.com/news/delta-air-lines-tech-woes-flight-cancelations-crowdstrike-microsoft/722056/]. The thesis pushes processing onto devices and software; this event is the bill for that when the software is the single point of failure. Manual fallback is a physical requirement — counter space, power, wired comms, staging room — that a "processing migrates off-airport" design tends to delete.

**MCO/RSW hurricanes — the terminal as survivability shelter.** MCO ceased commercial ops, activated its EOC, ran a designated ride-out crew inside the terminal, and moved ~100 guests to real shelters because the airport is not a public shelter [Source: https://www.orlandoairports.net/press/2024/10/07/hurricane-milton-update-2-orlando-international-to-cease-commercial-operations-8-am-wednesday-october-9/]. Continuity of a coastal or grid-stressed hub means designated hardened space and standalone power for the people who cannot leave — a spatial and structural commitment.

**The Dulles program scale.** ~$22B, $21.8B in new bonds plus $1.1B in fees, main-terminal expansion, four linear concourses, Aerotrain extension, mobile lounges retired, ~8-year build [Source: https://northernvirginiamag.com/news/2026/05/12/a-22m-plan-could-transform-dulles-airport-by-2034/] [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/business/dulles-development-projects]. At that scale the marginal cost of doing resilience right is trivial; the cost of retrofitting it after opening is prohibitive. That asymmetry is the entire preparedness case.

## 3. The preparedness case — for and against the thesis

**Where the thesis is right, and it helps my argument.** The run's core claim — that fixed built form around a linear sequence is what dates a terminal — is exactly why EM must be treated as durable infrastructure. The activations above are unanimous: what determined "contained vs. catastrophic" was almost never the plan or the software. It was the *building*. ATL's fate was set by cable routing. FLL's chaos was shaped by where a panicked crowd could run and where it could be accounted for. MCO's survivability was a function of hardened space and standalone power. CrowdStrike's damage tracked how much manual fallback the physical environment could absorb. Decentralized, de-sequenced processing multiplies exactly the variables that turned each of those events ugly: more sterile seams, more distributed crowds, more software dependency, less single-chokepoint control. So the thesis, taken seriously, is an argument to elevate EM from fit-out to structure. That is the outcome I want.

**Where the thesis, if handled naively, erodes readiness.** The current centralized checkpoint is genuinely a resilience asset, not just inertia. It is one boundary to hold, one crowd to protect, one sterile/non-sterile line to defend, and one place to run a lockdown. Distributing screening to gate-level or off-airport trades a controllable chokepoint for a diffuse membrane with many more failure edges — and today's emergency doctrine (2009-vintage AC, centralized-crowd assumptions) has no concept of operations for that. The honest counter-case is that the security and level-of-service reasons for the centralized model are load-bearing, and de-sequencing without rebuilding the emergency ConOps around it is how you get a terminal that is fast on a normal Tuesday and ungovernable on the worst day. The run's own "What this is NOT" guardrail applies to me too: do not design around universal biometrics or off-airport screening as if their arrival dates were known. The correct posture is not "distribute now"; it is *keep the distributed future reversible* — provision for it, but do not delete the ability to re-centralize control during an incident.

**The exercises that most directly bear on it.** The triennial full-scale under 139.325 is the mechanism that would *find* these built-form gaps before opening — if Dulles runs its first full-scale exercise against the *new* geometry during commissioning, not after. An active-shooter/evacuation tabletop and a total-power-loss / IT-outage tabletop, run against the distributed-screening concept while it is still on paper, are the cheapest risk-reduction dollars in the program. The field's after-action record (FLL AAR to HSEEP; the GAO power-resilience review triggered by ATL) is the ready-made scenario library.

## 4. Anchors a strategist can use

1. **ATL, Dec 17, 2017 — "redundant" power that shared one tunnel.** ~11-hour outage, ~1,183 cancellations, ~30,000 affected, because primary and backup cabling ran the same physical path [Source: https://www.ajc.com/news/atlanta-airport-blog/gao-studied-airport-power-outages-in-wake-of-hartsfield-jackson-blackout/LXTBMWTZ2BEGZLD3VDDSATL6Q4/]. Use it to argue: physically separated utility routing is a durable, lock-it-now decision.

2. **FLL, Jan 6, 2017 — sterile-boundary breach and self-evacuation onto the airfield.** 5 killed, ~36 injured in the stampede; crowd onto aprons/taxiways/runway and through the fence; HSEEP-standard AAR on command, interoperable comms, and reunification [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale_airport_shooting]. Use it to argue: distributed screening multiplies sterile seams — design evacuation geometry and reunification space for that future.

3. **CrowdStrike, Jul 19–25, 2024 — the continuity bill for software-mediated processing.** Delta: 7,000+ cancellations, 1.3M passengers, five-day recovery [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption]. Use it to argue: manual, degraded-mode fallback is a *physical* design input, not an IT afterthought.

4. **MCO/RSW hurricanes (Ian 2022; Milton 2024) — the terminal as survivability shelter.** EOC activation, ride-out crew sheltering in the terminal, ~100 guests bused out because the airport is not a public shelter [Source: https://www.orlandoairports.net/press/2024/10/07/hurricane-milton-update-2-orlando-international-to-cease-commercial-operations-8-am-wednesday-october-9/]. Use it to argue: designated hardened space + standalone power is durable square footage.

5. **ACRP RR 189 + 14 CFR 139.325 + AC 150/5200-31C — the doctrine gap.** The EOC design guide exists because EOCs are chronically bolted on late [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25280/design-considerations-for-airport-eocs]; the federal exercise mandate and 2009 planning AC are the floor a 2034 terminal will still be certified against [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.325] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/150_5200_31c_chg1.pdf]. Use it to argue: run the first full-scale exercise against the new geometry *before* opening.

### Bottom line for the Strategist

Sort the EM moves into the run's own three buckets:

- **Design FOR now (lock it):** centrally located, structurally hardened primary EOC with a physically separate alternate EOC; dual power feeds on physically separated routes (ATL lesson); a hardened, physically diverse communications backbone; evacuation geometry and mass-care/reunification space provisioned into both airside and landside; designated survivability shelter space with standalone power.
- **Accommodate LATER (leave soft, keep reversible):** distributed/gate-level screening — provision conduit, power, and floor loading for it, but preserve the ability to re-centralize crowd control during an incident. Sensing, biometrics, and comms electronics as swappable fit-out.
- **Bet AGAINST (do not design around):** fully off-airport, device-only processing with no manual fallback; universal-biometrics or eVTOL-scale timelines as givens. CrowdStrike is the reason the manual fallback stays in the building.

The thesis strengthens the case for EM capability — but only if leadership hears the corollary. Decentralizing processing without rebuilding the emergency concept of operations around it does not modernize resilience; it quietly deletes the one it had. The cheapest insurance in a $22B program is putting emergency management in the column-grid conversation, not the fit-out conversation. It is invisible until the worst day. On the worst day it is the whole ballgame.
