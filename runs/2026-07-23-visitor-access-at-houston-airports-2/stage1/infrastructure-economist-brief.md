# Infrastructure-Economist Brief: The Economics of a Visitor Pass Program at Houston Airports

**Run:** Visitor Access at Houston Airports (IAH / HOU)
**Lens:** Airport capital and infrastructure economics — lifecycle cost, capacity as an asset, financial health, and opportunity cost
**Prepared:** July 2026
**Independence note:** This brief was written without reading any other Stage 1 output.

---

## Framing: this is a rounding error, and that is the interesting part

The reflex of an infrastructure economist is to size a proposal against the capital base it lives inside. Do that here and the headline writes itself: a visitor pass program is financially immaterial to Houston Airports. It will not move the debt service coverage ratio, the credit rating, or the cost per enplanement. Whatever it costs and whatever it earns, both numbers round to zero against a $778.4 million annual budget and a $2.92 billion capital plan [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf].

That does **not** make the decision trivial. It relocates it. The scarce resource a visitor pass program consumes is not capital — it is **checkpoint throughput** (a physical asset with a hard ceiling) and **management/security attention** (a soft one). The economic case must be argued on those terms, not on a lifecycle P&L that will always be small enough to hide. Anyone who tries to justify this program on "it pays for itself" revenue math is fighting on the weakest ground available, and the run prompt already rules that argument out of bounds.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **The program is fiscally trivial to Houston Airports.** HAS runs a $778.4M FY2027 revenue-fund budget with ~$268M in debt service and other uses and ~1,383 FTEs [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. A visitor pass program — even a generous one — is a low-six-figure to low-seven-figure line item. It is <0.2% of the annual budget and cannot be defended or killed on system-level financial grounds.

2. **Checkpoint capacity, not money, is the binding constraint.** At the SEA benchmark cap of 300 passes/day, visitors equal roughly **0.5% of IAH's ~64,000 daily enplaned passengers** (23.3M enplanements in CY2024 ÷ 365) [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/IAH]. That is small — but it is not zero, and it concentrates at specific checkpoints and hours. The economics live in *where and when*, not *how many total*.

3. **Non-aeronautical revenue is the honest upside, and it is modest.** HAS earns roughly **$10.16 in non-airline revenue per passenger** (FY2026 estimate; FY2027 target ≥$10.21) [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. A visitor who behaves like an average passenger is worth ~$10 in concessions/parking. At 300 visitors/day × 365 days, gross non-aero upside is on the order of **$0.5M–$1.1M/year per airport** before program cost — real, but not a business case on its own.

4. **The revenue rationale is the founding rationale elsewhere.** SEA explicitly launched its 2018 program "as a way to bring in more non-aeronautical revenue such as parking, dining and shopping," then found it "took on a life of its own, turning into more of a customer experience initiative" [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/airline/seattle-tacoma-airport-gate-pass-program/]. The revenue thesis is real but has historically underperformed the experience thesis.

5. **HAS financial health is strong and gives it room — but also removes the excuse of scarcity.** Fitch-calculated total DSCR was **1.8x in FY2025** against a self-imposed ≥1.5x floor; net leverage ~4.8x; Fitch upgraded HAS parity bonds to **AA-** and KBRA assigns **AA** to the subordinate lien [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/IAH]. A healthy balance sheet means the program is affordable; it also means "we can't afford it" is not a credible reason to decline.

6. **IAH is already a comparatively expensive airport; the program does not change that, and cannot fix it.** IAH's airline cost per enplanement was **$10.66 in FY2024 and $11.17 in FY2025** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/IAH]. A visitor program neither raises CPE meaningfully (its cost is tiny) nor lowers it (its revenue is tiny). Framing the program as a lever on airline economics is a category error.

7. **The comparable programs are cheap, capped, and reversible — and one died on a capital collision.** SEA caps at 300/day, 5am–10pm, single checkpoint; DTW runs 4am–9pm and earlier capped at 75/day; Pittsburgh's pioneering myPITpass (2017) ended in 2020 and **never restarted because the airport was under terminal construction** [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/page/sea-visitor-pass-program; https://www.metroairport.com/about-us/dtw-destination-pass/faqs; https://thepointsguy.com/guide/seattle-tacoma-airport-gate-pass-program]. That last fact matters directly for Houston: IAH just finished a $1.468B terminal program and is starting a Domestic Terminal Program — capital disruption is the historical killer of these programs, not budget.

8. **The true opportunity cost is not capital — it is checkpoint minutes and security bandwidth.** HAS reinvests ~2% of gross book value into capital renewal annually and carries ~$166M of FY2027 debt service [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. A visitor program competes with none of that. What it competes with is finite lane-hours at peak and the attention of a security organization already managing a major capital build-out.

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 Houston Airports' financial scale — the denominator

The FY2027 HAS Revenue Fund budget totals **$778.4M** in expenditure: ~$510.4M in maintenance and operations and ~$268.0M in "Debt Service & Other Uses." The budget explicitly notes "$166 million" of that is debt service, "also anticipated to be funded from Passenger Facility Charges (PFCs)." Staffing is **1,383.2 FTEs** [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf].

On the capital side, HAS adopted a **$2.92 billion FY2026–2030 capital improvement plan** and has been issuing general airport revenue bonds (a $719.5M series) to fund it [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/houston-to-sell-bonds-for-airport-projects]. The just-completed **IAH Terminal Redevelopment Program cost $1.468B** against a $1.36B baseline — a 6.5% overrun, which is disciplined by industry standards [Source: https://communityimpact.com/houston/lake-houston-humble-kingwood/transportation/2026/01/29/iah-to-wrap-up-146b-terminal-redevelopment-program-in-early-2026/]. Any visitor-pass line item is invisible at this scale.

### 2.2 Financial health — HAS can afford anything reasonable

Fitch calculates HAS total debt service coverage at **1.8x for FY2025**, comfortably above the ≥1.5x covenant HAS holds as a stated goal, with net leverage of roughly **4.8x** and a parity-bond upgrade to **AA-**; KBRA rates the subordinate lien **AA** with a positive outlook revision in July 2025 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/IAH; https://markets.financialcontent.com/pennwell.oilgasjournal/article/bizwire-2025-7-16-kbra-assigns-aa-rating-to-city-of-houston-texas-airport-system-subordinate-lien-revenue-bonds-series-2025a-amt-and-series-2025b-non-amt-affirms-rating-for-parity-bonds-revises-rating-outlook-to-positive]. HAS also targets ">450 days of cash on hand" [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. This is a well-capitalized enterprise. The program's cost is not a financial risk to it.

### 2.3 The revenue upside — real, modest, and easy to overstate

HAS's own performance metric is instructive: **non-airline "revenue per passenger" of $10.16 (FY2026 estimate), targeted at ≥$10.21 for FY2027** [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. Commercial Development (parking, concessions, rental cars) generated **$152.6M of revenue in the FY2027 budget** on ~$63.1M of expense [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf].

Two cautions on applying that $10.16 to visitors:
- **Parking is the largest non-airline line, and many visitors won't pay for it the way passengers do** (short greet-and-go trips, drop-offs). HAS parking receipts run **$43.13/transaction (IAH garage), $32.27 (IAH ecopark), $45.07 (HOU garage), $40.84 (HOU ecopark)** [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf] — but these accrue only if the visitor parks rather than being dropped off.
- **Visitor concession spend is undocumented.** No airport in the benchmark set publishes spend-per-visitor. SEA's own account is that the *revenue* rationale gave way to a *customer-experience* rationale [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/airline/seattle-tacoma-airport-gate-pass-program/]. Treat any revenue projection as directional, not bankable — consistent with the run's prohibition on unsupported revenue claims.

**Order-of-magnitude model (stated assumptions, not a forecast):** 300 visitors/day × 365 = 109,500 visitor-entries/year. At $5–$10 net non-aero spend each (discounting parking and dwell relative to passengers), gross annual upside is **~$0.5M–$1.1M per airport**. This is the ceiling of the honest revenue case.

### 2.4 The cost side — small, but genuinely undocumented

I could find **no airport that publishes the operating cost of its visitor pass program.** Programs are marketed as "free" to the visitor (SEA, DTW, KC, Tulsa) [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/01/travel/visitor-pass-us-airports], but "free to the user" is not "free to the airport." Public statements point to real, if modest, cost:
- One airport noted the relaunch "required staffing and operations" adjustments; another framed the benefit as self-service that "helps alleviate the stress on our staffing and the airline staffing" [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/01/travel/visitor-pass-us-airports].
- The cost drivers are knowable even if the totals aren't published: an application/vetting portal or kiosk (DTW uses "fully automated" departures-level kiosks) [Source: https://www.metroairport.com/about-us/dtw-destination-pass/faqs], identity verification and watch-list coordination with TSA, signage and comms, incremental terminal-management labor, and — the real one — **TSA screening capacity** the airport does not pay for directly but does consume.

**My estimate, flagged as an estimate:** a capped, single-airport program is plausibly a **$0.3M–$1.5M/year** all-in operating cost (a handful of FTEs, a modest technology stack, comms), with a small one-time stand-up cost for the portal/kiosks. This is an infrastructure-economist's order-of-magnitude, not a verified figure. **If a decision hinges on this number, HAS must build a bottom-up cost model — the public record will not supply it.**

### 2.5 Checkpoint capacity — the asset that actually gets consumed

TSA's design throughput target is **300 passengers per hour per lane** [Source: https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/300-people-hour-lane]. Against that:
- A 300/day visitor cap spread across a checkpoint's ~17 operating hours is ~18 visitors/hour — **~6% of a single lane's hourly capacity**, and a smaller share of a multi-lane checkpoint.
- Against **IAH's ~64,000 daily enplaned passengers**, 300 visitors is **~0.5%** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/IAH].

The number is small in aggregate but **not evenly distributed.** SEA manages this by funneling all visitors through one checkpoint (Checkpoint 4) and capping entry hours [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/page/sea-visitor-pass-program]. That is the correct economic design: concentrate a low-value, discretionary flow at a controllable node and shed it instantly when peak demand from paying passengers arrives. TSA screened a record **904 million people in 2024** with peak days above 2.7M/day [Source: https://www.tsa.gov/travel/passenger-volumes/2024]; at IROPS or holiday peaks, a visitor's marginal lane-minute has a real congestion cost borne by ticketed passengers. Every benchmark program reserves the right to suspend without notice for exactly this reason [Source: https://flytulsa.com/travel/amenities/tul-visitor-pass-program/].

### 2.6 The Pittsburgh warning — capital disruption kills these programs

myPITpass was the 2017 pioneer. It ended in 2020 and **did not restart, specifically because Pittsburgh was building a new terminal** [Source: https://thepointsguy.com/guide/seattle-tacoma-airport-gate-pass-program/]. This is the single most relevant benchmark fact for Houston. IAH has just closed out a $1.468B terminal program and its long-term plan commences a **Domestic Terminal Program at IAH** and a seven-gate expansion at HOU [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. A visitor program launched into an active construction environment inherits reduced circulation space, temporary checkpoint configurations, and a security organization with its attention already committed. The opportunity cost here is not dollars — it is the risk of standing up a public-facing program you then have to suspend, which is worse than never launching.

### 2.7 Opportunity cost — reframed correctly

The classic infrastructure opportunity-cost question — "what else could this capital have funded?" — is nearly moot because the capital involved is negligible next to a $2.92B CIP and $166M annual debt service [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/budget/27budprop/V_HAS.pdf]. The honest opportunity-cost ledger is:
- **Checkpoint minutes** at peak, which are genuinely scarce and are transferred from paying passengers to non-paying visitors.
- **Security and terminal-management attention** during a multi-year capital build-out.
- **Reputational capital** — a program that must be repeatedly suspended erodes trust more than its absence would.

None of these appear on a balance sheet, which is precisely why a purely financial analysis will mislead the Director.

## 3. Caveats and limitations

1. **Program operating cost is not in the public record.** My $0.3M–$1.5M/year range is a reasoned order-of-magnitude, not a sourced figure. No benchmark airport publishes its all-in program cost. This is the single largest gap and must be closed with a bottom-up HAS model before any go/no-go.
2. **Visitor spend is undocumented.** The $5–$10/visitor net non-aero assumption is derived from HAS's *passenger* revenue-per-enplanement and discounted judgmentally. Real visitor spend could be materially lower (drop-off greeters who buy nothing) or concentrated (a family dining before a deployment). No airport publishes spend-per-visitor.
3. **Participation volumes are largely unpublished.** Benchmark caps (SEA 300/day, DTW historically 75/day) are known; actual daily *utilization* against those caps is not consistently reported. My throughput percentages assume caps are fully used, which likely overstates the average-day burden.
4. **CPE and DSCR figures are from a third-party finance aggregator (DWU Consulting) citing Fitch/HAS.** They are consistent with HAS budget documents and rating-agency press but should be confirmed against the primary Official Statement before publication.
5. **The FY2027 budget PDF is a City of Houston budget document, not an audited CAFR.** Budgeted figures (especially forward-year) differ from actuals. I used "actual" and "estimate" columns where available and labeled budget figures as such.
6. **I did not model HOU separately in detail.** HOU is smaller (~7.3M enplanements FY2024, rising) and its checkpoint geometry and event profile differ; the per-airport revenue and capacity math should be re-run for HOU specifically [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/houston-to-sell-bonds-for-airport-projects].
7. **This brief deliberately does not assess incremental security risk** — that is another agent's lens. My scope is cost, capacity, and financial materiality.

## Bottom line for the infrastructure-economist lens

Build the program's business case on **customer experience and community value**, budget it honestly as a small operating cost with a bottom-up model (do not import a revenue-per-passenger number and call it a forecast), and design it around the one asset it actually consumes: **checkpoint capacity**. Cap it low, concentrate it at one controllable checkpoint, cap the hours, and write suspension authority into the rules from day one. Above all, **do not launch it into the teeth of the IAH Domestic Terminal Program** — Pittsburgh already ran that experiment, and the program lost.
