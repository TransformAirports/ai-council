# The CEO's Chair: A Visitor Pass Program Seen Through Bond Covenants, the United Agreement, and City Hall

**Brief for the Council — Visitor Access at Houston Airports (IAH / HOU)**
**Lens: Chief Executive Officer, large US hub airport**

A note on my seat before I argue. I do not run the terminals. I allocate three things the operator cannot: the balance sheet, the political capital with City Hall and the carriers, and the strategic direction that decides which fights we pick. A visitor pass program is, on its face, a customer-experience amenity — a rounding error against a $1.43 billion terminal program [Source: https://www.arup.com/en-us/projects/iah-terminal-redevelopment-program/]. My job is to explain why a rounding error can still cost real money and real leverage if it is handled as an operations decision rather than a positioning decision. The operator can win the amenity and still lose the airport. Below is what this thesis costs and what it buys, measured in coverage ratios, the November-2053 and January-2030 lease dates, TSA's calendar, and City Council votes.

---

## 1. Key Findings (for a one-read absorption)

1. **Houston is a city department, not an authority — that is the single most important fact on this page.** The Houston Airport System (HAS) is a department of the City of Houston; the Director of Aviation is appointed by the Mayor and confirmed by City Council [Source: https://communityimpact.com/houston/bay-area/transportation/2024/02/29/jim-szczesniak-named-new-director-of-houston-airport-system/]. There is no independent board insulating aviation policy from municipal politics. A visitor pass program is therefore not an internal management choice — it is a City of Houston public-access policy, and it will be read that way by a councilmember the first time something goes wrong.

2. **We carry the classic large-hub credit vulnerability: single-carrier concentration.** United is roughly 73% of IAH enplanements including its regional flying and operates about 80% of flights [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/junk-rated-united-airlines-backs-1b-houston-airport-bond]. Every rating agency treats >60% single-carrier share as the weakest tier of its carrier-concentration factor [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-key-metrics-credit-vs-mission]. A visitor pass program does not move that number — but nothing we do should give United a fresh grievance to carry into the next rate conversation.

3. **The program's costs and benefits land in different pockets, held by different parties.** Under our hybrid-compensatory agreement, more than half of operating costs are recovered from airlines [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/large-hub-airport-ratemaking-2022-update]. Incremental screening-adjacent staffing, technology, and administration are terminal costs the airlines may be asked to fund; concession revenue the program generates is largely *airport* (non-airline) money. That asymmetry is the whole game: we would be asking signatory carriers to help pay for an amenity whose upside accrues disproportionately to us.

4. **The leverage clock is already ticking toward 2030.** United's terminal leases run on staggered dates, with Terminal E expiring January 31, 2030, even as the ITRP special-facility financing extends the relationship to November 2053 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. The next real ratemaking window is the back half of this decade. Anything that becomes a line item in rates before then is a bargaining chip United will price into that negotiation. Do not hand them one for free.

5. **This thesis quietly makes a federal partner — TSA — a load-bearing dependency we do not control.** Every operating program (SEA, DTW, MCO) routes visitor entry through TSA screening and TSA approval [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/page/sea-visitor-pass-program]. TSA staffs to ticketed-passenger throughput, not to our amenities. We would be building a public promise on a checkpoint we neither staff nor fund.

6. **Peer programs are real but fragile, and the fragility is operational-political, not technical.** SEA caps at 300 passes/day and suspends the program on peak dates — including for 2026 World Cup demand [Source: https://komonews.com/news/local/seattle-tacoma-international-airportspot-saver-and-the-sea-visitor-pass-program-unavailable-on-certain-dates-this-summer-2026-world-cup-in-seattle-games-summer-travel]. Orlando (MCO) paused its program outright to "sort out staffing and operations" and only reinstated it in December 2025 [Source: https://deeparrival.com/news/airport-guest-pass-non-flyers-2026/]. Suspension is a normal state of these programs. A CEO must assume the pass will be *off* during exactly the high-demand moments the public most wants it.

7. **The correct CEO framing is cheap political capital, tightly bounded — not a strategic initiative.** Handled as a small, revocable, self-funding pilot with hard stop criteria, a visitor pass buys goodwill with City Council and the traveling public at low cost. Handled as a headline "reopening the airport to the public," it creates an entitlement that is politically expensive to withdraw and operationally impossible to guarantee.

## 2. Evidence

### The bond-rating lens

HAS is a strong credit and recently got stronger: Fitch upgraded roughly $2.2 billion of subordinate-lien revenue bonds to 'AA-' in early 2026, and S&P has moved the system up on the strength of passenger recovery and what Fitch called "strong regional monopoly characteristics" [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/s-p-lifts-houston-airport-system-ratings]. The three agencies score airport credits on revenue-risk volume and price, debt structure, and a financial profile built from **debt-service coverage, days-cash-on-hand, and cost-per-enplanement**, with single-carrier concentration treated as a distinct risk factor tiered at roughly 30%, 30–60%, and >60% share [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-key-metrics-credit-vs-mission].

Two things follow for this thesis. First, a visitor pass program is immaterial to coverage or liquidity — it will not, by itself, move a rating a notch in either direction. Second, and more important, the agencies reward **cost discipline and a stable airline relationship**. The system already keeps CPE competitive precisely because the airlines cover the majority of operating cost [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/large-hub-airport-ratemaking-2022-update]. The credit risk is not the pass; it is the *precedent* of loading non-aeronautical amenity cost into aeronautical rates and inviting an airline challenge that the agencies read as relationship friction.

The concentration problem is not abstract here. When our dominant carrier's own credit is sub-investment-grade — United backs Houston special-facility bonds rated Ba3 / BB+, i.e., junk, tracking United's issuer rating [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/junk-rated-united-airlines-backs-1b-houston-airport-bond] — the airport's investment-grade rating is doing a lot of work to hold a spread that a single-carrier shock could widen. That is the ballast I protect. I do not spend it on a gate-greeting program.

### Airline use-and-lease structure

IAH runs a **hybrid-compensatory** agreement — compensatory treatment on some cost centers, residual features on others, with the airport retaining non-airline revenue risk [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-rate-methodologies]. That structure is why the concession-revenue upside of a visitor pass is genuinely *ours* rather than flowing back to reduce airline rates. It is also why the cost side is contestable: any capital or staffing we try to recover through rates is a negotiation, and in a hub this concentrated, "negotiation with the airlines" means "negotiation with United."

The instrument to watch is the Majority-in-Interest (MII) clause. MII gives signatory carriers a formal say over capital projects funded through rates [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-use-agreements]. A visitor pass pilot funded from non-aeronautical revenue sits *outside* MII and outside airline consent. The moment it is funded through the rate base, it moves *inside* MII — and hands United a lever it does not currently have. **Keep this program on the non-airline side of the ledger and it stays my decision. Move it into rates and it becomes United's decision.** The staggered lease dates — Terminal E out in January 2030 against a special-facility relationship running to November 2053 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe] — tell me the real rate conversation is late-decade. I will not arrive at that table having volunteered a new recoverable amenity.

### The federal stack

Three federal calendars intersect this thesis, none of them ours to set:

- **TSA** is the binding one. SEA, DTW, and MCO all condition visitor entry on TSA screening and approval [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/SEAvisitorpassFAQs]. TSA staffs to ticketed throughput. Our program's availability is therefore a residual of TSA's checkpoint capacity — which is exactly why SEA suspends on peak days [Source: https://komonews.com/news/local/seattle-tacoma-international-airportspot-saver-and-the-sea-visitor-pass-program-unavailable-on-certain-dates-this-summer-2026-world-cup-in-seattle-games-summer-travel].
- **PFC / AIP.** The FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (PL 118-63) governs FY2024–FY2028, holds AIP near $4 billion annually, and **left the PFC cap frozen at $4.50 — unchanged since 2000** [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/pfc]. ACI-NA is still pressing to modernize it [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/press_release/airports-call-for-modernization-of-passenger-facility-charge-in-faa-reauthorization-proposal/]. Neither PFC nor AIP can fund a visitor amenity — both are restricted to eligible capital. So there is no federal money for this. It is 100% local dollars, which sharpens every cost question.
- **IIJA/BIL airport funds** expire September 30, 2026 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/119th-congress-airport-legislation-tracker]. The supplemental infrastructure window is closing while we would be diverting management attention to a non-eligible program. Opportunity cost is real.

### Board governance

There is no aviation board. HAS is a City of Houston department; Mayor John Whitmire appointed Director Szczesniak in February 2024, and City Council confirmed him unanimously [Source: https://www.fox26houston.com/news/jim-szczesniak-named-new-director-of-aviation-for-houston-airport-system]. The distinction from an operating authority or an airport district matters: an authority board can adopt a public-access policy on its own governance timeline; a city department's policy lives inside the municipal cycle, subject to council committees, the mayoral term, and open-records politics. Szczesniak has already made governance transparency a public theme — he called for more transparency around the HAS Development Corporation [Source: https://www.fly2houston.com/newsroom/releases/director-szczesniak-calls-transparency-has-development-corporation]. In that environment, any visitor pass incident becomes a council hearing, not an internal after-action. Survivable strategy here means: revocable by design, defensible on the record, and not dependent on a single mayor's enthusiasm.

### The host region and the CEO calendar

Peer evidence on where a large-hub CEO's time actually goes points to capital, finance, and government affairs — "both financial fluency and political acumen," with the on-time-versus-delayed capital program as the trajectory-defining variable [Source: https://www.odgers.com/en-us/insights/what-it-takes-to-lead-airports-boldly-through-disruption/]. The cohort is carrying roughly $130–140 billion in announced capital and priced about $9.5 billion in fresh revenue bonds across 2024–2025 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/large-hub-airport-capital-programs-2026]. That is the arena I am judged in. A visitor pass is a constituent-service item — valuable for goodwill with council and the business community, but it competes for the scarcest thing I have, which is not money. It is attention and political capital ahead of a rate negotiation and a federal-funding cliff.

### Long-tenure strategic patterns (2000–2025)

The documented history is unambiguous on one point, and it is the point most relevant to Houston: **the recurring large-hub mistake is building around a single dominant carrier's hub that later collapses.** Pittsburgh's midfield terminal for US Airways, Cincinnati and Memphis for Delta/Northwest, St. Louis for TWA/American, Cleveland for United — each left an airport with stranded cost and a concentration it could not quickly replace. This pattern is widely known in the industry and is exactly why agencies penalize >60% single-carrier share. It is not directly a visitor-pass issue, but it is the frame: with United at ~73%, our strategic discipline is to avoid *anything* that increases dependence on, or friction with, that one relationship. A visitor amenity that turns into a rates dispute does the second. That is the honest through-line from a quarter-century of hub history to a small pilot decision. (Documented in the rating record and industry history; the specific dehubbing cases are widely known but drawn from general industry knowledge, not a single cited filing.)

## 3. The case for and against — as the CEO sees it

**The financial-and-political case FOR.** This is the cheapest goodwill on the menu. Peer programs are free to the visitor, capacity-capped, and screened like any passenger [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/page/sea-visitor-pass-program] — meaning the security argument is largely settled and the marginal risk over a ticketed passenger is small. Funded from non-aeronautical revenue, the program stays outside MII and outside airline consent — it is genuinely *my* decision, and the concession-spend upside is *my* revenue under the hybrid-compensatory structure [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-rate-methodologies]. Politically, it is a rare thing a city-department airport can hand City Council and the business community that photographs well and costs little: reuniting military families, helping elderly travelers, animating the concessions we already built. In a governance environment where the Director is running on transparency and public value, a well-bounded pilot is a low-cost deposit in the political account I will want to draw on in 2030.

**The financial-and-political case AGAINST.** Three risks, in order. **(1) The precedent, not the pilot.** Once visitors expect access, withdrawal is a political event, not an operational one — and SEA and MCO show suspension is routine [Source: https://deeparrival.com/news/airport-guest-pass-non-flyers-2026/]. I would be creating an entitlement I cannot guarantee, backed by a TSA checkpoint I do not staff. **(2) The wrong side of the ledger.** If cost pressure pushes this into rates and charges, it enters MII and becomes a United lever right before the late-decade lease window [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. A CEO who trades a greeting program for even a modest concession in the use-and-lease has made a catastrophic exchange. **(3) Attention.** With IIJA funds expiring in 2026 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/119th-congress-airport-legislation-tracker], a frozen PFC [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/pfc], and $130B+ of cohort capital defining how CEOs are judged [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/large-hub-airport-capital-programs-2026], management attention spent litigating an amenity incident at council is attention stolen from the balance sheet.

**My verdict.** Yes — but only as a small, non-airline-funded, revocable pilot with published stop criteria, sized like SEA's cap rather than an open door, and explicitly walled off from the rate base so it never touches MII. Approve it as a constituent-service pilot, not a strategic initiative. The test is not whether visitors enjoy it. The test is whether, in 2030, it sits in a footnote or across the negotiating table.

## 4. Data points and precedents a strategist can use

1. **Concentration tier.** United ≈ 73% of IAH enplanements (incl. regional), ~80% of flights [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/junk-rated-united-airlines-backs-1b-houston-airport-bond]. Every agency scores >60% single-carrier share as the weakest concentration tier [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-key-metrics-credit-vs-mission]. Any airport-wide policy must be defended in that frame.

2. **The MII line.** Non-aeronautical funding keeps the program outside Majority-in-Interest and outside airline consent; rate-base funding pulls it inside [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-use-agreements]. This is the single most consequential design choice and it is binary.

3. **The 2030 clock.** Staggered United terminal leases (Terminal E expires Jan 31, 2030) against a special-facility relationship to Nov 2053 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe] — the next ratemaking leverage window is late-decade; do not create recoverable line items before it.

4. **Suspension is normal.** SEA caps at 300/day and suspends on peak dates [Source: https://komonews.com/news/local/seattle-tacoma-international-airportspot-saver-and-the-sea-visitor-pass-program-unavailable-on-certain-dates-this-summer-2026-world-cup-in-seattle-games-summer-travel]; MCO paused entirely to fix staffing and reopened Dec 2025 [Source: https://deeparrival.com/news/airport-guest-pass-non-flyers-2026/]. Design for an *off* switch from day one.

5. **No federal money.** PFC frozen at $4.50 since 2000 and AIP restricted to eligible capital [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/pfc]; IIJA airport funds expire Sept 30, 2026 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/119th-congress-airport-legislation-tracker]. This is 100% local dollars — price the pilot accordingly and do not let it compete with the capital program for attention.

*Documented vs. widely known:* Ratings, lease dates, methodology, program rules, and federal timing above are from cited public sources. The single-carrier dehubbing precedents (PIT, CVG, MEM, STL, CLE) are widely known industry history rather than a single citable filing, and are presented as pattern, not as a specific sourced claim.
