# Airline Commercial Strategist — Evidence Brief
## Visitor Access at Houston Airports (IAH / HOU): The Carrier-Response Lens

**Prepared for the Transform Airports AI Council — Stage 1 independent research**
**Framing question I apply to every recommendation in this run: "What will United and Southwest do in response?" That question is my veto.**

---

## Orientation: why the airline lens is load-bearing on a visitor-pass decision

A visitor pass program looks like a customer-experience and community-relations question. It is also, unavoidably, a question about shared airport infrastructure that two carriers dominate and largely pay for. At George Bush Intercontinental (IAH), United controls **58.7% of the market** and runs the airport as its third-largest hub; at William P. Hobby (HOU), Southwest controls **more than 93%** of passengers. Any program that touches checkpoint throughput, holdroom circulation, or the airline rate base runs straight into the two entities with the most leverage over Houston Airports' capital and operating decisions. A visitor-pass strategy that does not model carrier response is not a strategy — it is a wish.

The central, counter-intuitive finding of this brief: **the airports that can run visitor programs cheaply are the ones carriers have already walked away from.** Pittsburgh could open its terminal to the public because US Airways dehubbed it and left half the concourses empty. IAH is the opposite case — a constrained, growing hub where United is spending billions and watching its cost per enplanement climb. Feasibility is inversely correlated with hub health. Houston is choosing to do the harder version of this.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **The visitor-pass "success stories" are mostly dehubbed airports with stranded checkpoint capacity.** Pittsburgh — the first US airport to reopen its post-security terminal (myPITpass, 2017) — could do so precisely because US Airways' 2004 dehubbing left it with 75 gates and under 250 daily departures, and it had literally walled off half of Concourses A and B to save utilities [Source: https://abcnews.go.com/Travel/story?id=3724036]. That spare capacity is the enabling condition. IAH and HOU do not have it in the same way. myPITpass is now suspended [Source: https://stuckattheairport.com/2025/07/23/get-past-security-at-this-airport-without-a-ticket/].

2. **At IAH, United is the veto, and United's cost base is already rising.** IAH terminal rates jumped roughly 20% between FY2023 and FY2026 (Terminal B: $2.281 → $3.277 per sq ft) as the $2B+ Terminal B redevelopment and the ~$1.43B International Terminal Redevelopment Program (ITRP) flow into the rate base [Source: https://assets.ctfassets.net/fwldykjthzos/4SbSgW7X0NBrxMwe7c4Dn5/.../FY26_RC_Rate_Summary_-updated.pdf; https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/united-houston-airport-system-invest-more-than-2b-in-terminal-b-transformation-301989456.html]. A dominant carrier absorbing a rising CPE has little appetite for a program that adds cost or slows its connecting banks without offsetting revenue it shares in.

3. **Houston's hybrid-compensatory use agreement determines whether carriers see a visitor program as upside or pure cost — and the answer is probably "pure cost" to them.** Houston uses a hybrid compensatory bilateral airline use agreement [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-rate-methodologies]. Under a residual structure, incremental concession revenue offsets airline costs dollar-for-dollar (at SFO, signatory airlines receive 85% of concession revenue) [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/non-aeronautical-revenue-benchmarking]. Under compensatory cost centers, the airport keeps the concession upside and the airlines see only the congestion and screening cost. **The Council must confirm how Houston's agreement treats concession revenue before assuming any carrier will support the program on economic grounds.**

4. **Majority-in-interest (MII) rights give United a plausible lever to slow or block any capital spend that hits the rate base.** MII clauses let signatory airlines holding a majority of activity approve or veto capital projects funded through airline rates — typically 50%+1 of airlines and 50%+1 of landed weight/enplanements [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/airport-capital-review]. If a visitor program requires a new dedicated screening lane, holdroom modification, or staffing charged to the terminal cost center, United's ~59% share makes its consent close to decisive. The design that avoids this: fund the program outside the airline rate base and keep it out of shared holdroom capacity.

5. **Southwest's operating model at HOU is congestion-intolerant by design.** Southwest runs ~175 daily departures at HOU on fast, point-to-point turns [Source: https://simpleflying.com/southwest-airlines-doesnt-fly-major-texas-airport-anymore/]. Gate-area dwell and holdroom crowding are the enemy of a 30–40-minute turn. A visitor program that adds bodies to Southwest's holdrooms is a direct operational threat — but because Southwest is the near-monopoly signatory at HOU, it would also capture most of any concession upside, which could flip it into a supporter **if** the program lifts spend and **if** it is walled off from the gates.

6. **The SEA model — a dedicated, capped, off-peak, separately-screened checkpoint — is the design that survives the carrier veto.** Seattle caps its program at 300 passes/day, restricts hours to 5 a.m.–10 p.m., and routes all visitors through a single dedicated lane (Checkpoint 4) [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/page/sea-visitor-pass-program]. This insulates the airlines' peak-bank throughput. Any Houston program that instead pushes visitors through the main checkpoints during United's connecting banks or Southwest's departure pushes will draw carrier objection.

7. **Houston's carrier base makes fast, unilateral network decisions — the program must be sized to survive them.** Southwest exited IAH entirely on August 4, 2024, consolidating all Houston flying at Hobby after a $231M quarterly loss and MAX delivery shortfalls [Source: https://simpleflying.com/southwest-airlines-doesnt-fly-major-texas-airport-anymore/]. Southwest's five-gate international West Concourse at HOU (opened 2015) saw its international ambitions largely retract. A visitor program justified by today's traffic and concession mix can be undercut overnight by a carrier schedule decision the airport does not control.

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 Carrier concentration is the real governance structure

At IAH, United holds **58.7% market share**, operating roughly **28,912 flights and 3.6 million seats** annually as its third-largest hub and its primary Latin America gateway [Source: https://www.flyoveraviationanalytics.com/2025/08/hub-deep-dives-united-airlines-hub-at-houston-iah/; https://simpleflying.com/united-airlines-leading-hubs-passenger-numbers/]. At HOU, Southwest holds **more than 93%**, with Delta at ~3% and American at ~1% [Source: https://simpleflying.com/southwest-airlines-doesnt-fly-major-texas-airport-anymore/].

These concentrations mean neither airport has a diversified tenant base that would dilute a single carrier's objection. Whatever the two dominant carriers decide about a visitor program is, functionally, what happens. This is different from a Chicago or a Dallas where multiple large carriers create cross-pressure. In Houston, the strategist plans against two counterparties, each of which is close to a monopolist in its building.

### 2.2 The use agreement decides who wins from concessions

Houston's hybrid compensatory bilateral agreement applies compensatory calculation to some cost centers and residual calculation to others, with the airport bearing airport-wide non-airline revenue risk [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-rate-methodologies]. This is the pivot point for the entire "the program pays for itself through concessions" argument the run prompt explicitly warns against accepting uncritically.

- If visitor concession spend flows into a **residual** cost center, it reduces the airlines' net requirement and carriers have a financial stake in the program's success. Under a full residual formula, non-aeronautical revenue offsets airline costs dollar-for-dollar [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/non-aeronautical-revenue-benchmarking].
- If it accrues to a **compensatory** center or to the airport's discretionary funds, carriers capture none of it and rationally weigh only the incremental screening cost, holdroom congestion, and irregular-operations risk. That is a losing trade for them.

The Council should treat the concession-benefit case as unproven until Houston's specific revenue-sharing mechanics are documented. Non-aeronautical revenue now exceeds 70% of total revenue at major airports [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/non-aeronautical-revenue-benchmarking], so the stakes of that classification are large — but the benefit only motivates carriers if it reaches them.

### 2.3 MII and the rate base: where the airport's freedom actually ends

An MII clause "provides signatory airlines the opportunity to review and, by a prescribed percentage of airlines, approve or veto capital projects that would be funded through the airline rate base" [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/airport-capital-review]. Most agreements exempt safety, security, and grant-funded projects. A visitor program is not obviously a "security" project in the exempt sense — it is a discretionary customer-experience program that may *increase* security workload. If any of its capital (screening lanes, wayfinding, holdroom reconfiguration, technology) lands in the terminal cost center, United's ~59% activity share makes its consent decisive under a 50%+1 activity threshold.

The clean design avoids this entirely: fund the program from non-signatory sources or a discrete visitor-fee/concession stream, keep the capital out of the shared rate base, and use a dedicated checkpoint so the program consumes no throughput the carriers have paid for. That is not merely good governance — it is the only structure that denies the carriers a procedural veto.

### 2.4 Checkpoint capacity is the binding physical constraint, and hubs don't have it to spare

Seattle's own data show why this matters: "More than one-third of SEA's daily passenger volume arrives at the airport before 9 a.m., causing busy conditions at TSA security checkpoints," with 30–40-minute waits during Alaska's flight banks [Source: https://www.airlineairport.com/sea-airport/wait-times/]. SEA still runs its visitor program — but through a **separate** checkpoint, capped at 300/day, and off the peak where it would collide with the carrier's bank [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/page/sea-visitor-pass-program]. The lesson for a United hub with pronounced connecting banks is direct: visitors cannot share the peak-bank checkpoint capacity United depends on for its connection windows without degrading the product United sells.

## 3. Case studies: carrier responses to airport access decisions

### 3.1 Pittsburgh — the enabling condition was dehubbing, not vision
myPITpass (2017) is celebrated as pioneering. The unglamorous truth is that it was **cheap because the carrier left.** US Airways' 2004 dehubbing collapsed Pittsburgh to under 250 daily departures on a 75-gate midfield terminal, and the airport walled off half of two concourses to save ~$1M/year in utilities [Source: https://abcnews.go.com/Travel/story?id=3724036]. With a dedicated alternate checkpoint sitting idle, opening it to the public cost almost nothing in foregone airline throughput. Pittsburgh is now rebuilding a right-sized $1.57B terminal that explicitly "breaks from its USAir past" [Source: https://www.pittransformed.com/sneak-peek-inside-pittsburghs-brand-new-1-5-billion-airport-terminal/]. **Lesson for Houston: the myPITpass precedent does not transfer to a constrained hub. Do not cite it as proof the model is easy.** And note the program is currently suspended [Source: https://stuckattheairport.com/2025/07/23/get-past-security-at-this-airport-without-a-ticket/].

### 3.2 Detroit — a durable program at a hub, but a controlled one
Delta's Detroit hub kept the DTW Destination Pass running (launched Oct 2019, paused only for the pandemic) [Source: https://stuckattheairport.com/2020/01/04/detroit-metro-airport-gate-pass-program-permanent/]. It is the counter-example that a hub *can* sustain a program — but DTW's McNamara/North terminals have circulation and checkpoint headroom that a peak-banked hub can flex. The relevant variable is not "hub vs. non-hub" but "does the carrier's peak throughput have slack." The Council should benchmark IAH's and HOU's checkpoint peak utilization directly, not assume DTW's slack exists in Houston.

### 3.3 Southwest's IAH exit and the stranded HOU international gates — carriers move first, buildings follow
Two Houston-specific episodes show how fast carrier decisions override airport plans. First, Southwest **exited IAH entirely on August 4, 2024**, moving every Houston flight to Hobby after a $231M quarterly loss and receiving only 20 of 46 planned MAX 8s [Source: https://simpleflying.com/southwest-airlines-doesnt-fly-major-texas-airport-anymore/]. Second, Houston Airports built Southwest a five-gate international West Concourse at HOU in 2015 [Source: https://www.fly2houston.com/hou/redevelopment/west-concourse-expansion/]; Southwest's international network there never scaled to justify the full footprint. **Both are reminders that a visitor program's demand assumptions sit downstream of carrier network decisions the airport cannot control.** Size the program to survive a carrier schedule shock.

### 3.4 United at IAH — the tenant that is also a co-investor
United is not a passive tenant. It co-invested with Houston Airports in the $2B+ Terminal B transformation and is the reason the ITRP's international processor is being built [Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/united-houston-airport-system-invest-more-than-2b-in-terminal-b-transformation-301989456.html]. A co-investing carrier absorbing a ~20% terminal-rate increase [Source: https://assets.ctfassets.net/fwldykjthzos/4SbSgW7X0NBrxMwe7c4Dn5/.../FY26_RC_Rate_Summary_-updated.pdf] will scrutinize any new discretionary program for two things: does it raise my CPE, and does it slow my banks. If the answer to either is yes, expect United to resist — publicly on "security and congestion" grounds, actually on cost-and-throughput grounds. **Name that gap when it appears: the carrier's stated reason and its economic reason will not be identical.**

## 5. The veto, applied

Run the recommendation through the one question that governs this brief — *what will the carriers do in response?* — and three design constraints fall out non-negotiably:

- **Dedicated screening, capped and off-peak.** If visitors touch United's connecting-bank checkpoints or Southwest's departure pushes, the carriers will object and the operation will degrade. Use the SEA architecture: a separate lane, a daily cap, blackout during peak banks and irregular operations.
- **Out of the airline rate base.** If any capital or recurring cost lands in the terminal cost center, MII gives United a procedural lever and Southwest a grievance. Fund the program from non-signatory or discrete concession/visitor-fee sources.
- **Prove the concession benefit reaches the carriers — or stop claiming it as a carrier-buy-in argument.** Under Houston's hybrid agreement the revenue may accrue only to the airport. That is fine as a *public* rationale; it is not a reason the airlines will support the program, and the brief should not pretend otherwise.

If the program is confined to **public / pre-security space** (meeters-and-greeters areas, a landside events venue, observation decks outside the sterile area), most of this carrier risk evaporates — because it never touches the throughput or the rate base the carriers control. That is the version of a "visitor program" a strategist can recommend at a constrained dual-hub system like Houston's with the highest confidence that the carriers will not kill it. The moment the concept crosses the checkpoint into sterile, post-security space at IAH or HOU, United and Southwest become co-deciders, and the program's fate stops being the airport's to determine.

**Bottom line for the Council:** Houston can build the most elegant visitor-pass program on paper, but its survival depends on two carriers who did not ask for it, may not benefit from it under the current use agreement, and can degrade or veto it through checkpoint congestion, MII rights, and network decisions the airport does not control. Model that response first. Everything else is secondary.
