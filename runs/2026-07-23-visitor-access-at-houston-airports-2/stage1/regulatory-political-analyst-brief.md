# Regulatory & Political Brief: Visitor Pass Program at Houston Airports (IAH / HOU)

**Analyst lens:** Federal, state, and local regulatory and political constraints. The governing question is not "is a visitor pass legal?" — it is, hundreds of airports now run one. The question is which parts of this decision Houston Airports controls, which parts TSA controls, and which parts are exposed to appropriations politics that can suspend the program overnight regardless of how well it is designed.

---

## 1. Key Findings

- **A visitor pass program is not a rulemaking fight — it is a permission you apply for.** In 2024–25 TSA amended its requirements to give airports flexibility to admit non-ticketed visitors to sterile areas; the number of participating US airports rose from roughly 20 in mid-2025 to 28 by January 2026 [Source: https://yourmileagemayvary.com/2026/01/02/28-airports-visitor-pass-programs/]. Houston does not need Congress or a NEPA review. It needs a TSA-approved amendment to its Airport Security Program.

- **The one thing that cannot be negotiated: every visitor is screened exactly like a ticketed passenger, and every visitor is run against the Secure Flight watch lists before entry.** This is federal regulation (49 CFR Part 1542 for the sterile area, Part 1560 for Secure Flight), not airport discretion [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-XII/subchapter-C/part-1560]. Any business case that assumes a "lighter" screening path for visitors is dead on arrival.

- **The program is a revocable privilege, not a durable entitlement — and checkpoint stress is what revokes it.** myPITpass, the 2017 pioneer, was suspended in 2020 for COVID and never restarted [Source: https://airportimprovement.com/article/pittsburgh-intl-opens-airside-areas-non-ticketed-visitors/]. Visitor access is the first thing an airport sheds when queues grow, because it is the only checkpoint demand the airport can switch off unilaterally.

- **IAH has a demonstrated checkpoint-capacity problem that is politically exposed, not merely operational.** During the early-2026 federal shutdown IAH posted TSA waits "exceeding four hours" and was named among the worst-hit hubs in the country [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/03/25/us/houston-bush-airport-tsa-wait-times]. A program whose entire value proposition depends on spare checkpoint capacity is being proposed for an airport that periodically has none.

- **Houston's governance is the binding local constraint most peers do not share.** Houston Airports is a *department of the City of Houston*, not an independent authority. The Director of Aviation is a mayoral appointee confirmed by City Council, and the operating budget is adopted by Council annually [Source: https://www.fly2houston.com/airport-business/about/leadership/]. Unlike DTW (Wayne County Airport Authority) or SEA (Port of Seattle), Houston cannot wall this program off inside an independent board — it is a municipal line item visible to 16 council members and a mayor.

- **Every operating program is small, capped, and hours-limited by design — that is the regulatory equilibrium, not a starting point to grow past.** SEA caps at 300/day, PHL Wingmate at 100/day, DTW at 75/day, MSY at 50 weekday / 100 weekend [Sources below]. TSA tolerates the concept precisely because the caps keep incremental checkpoint load trivial. A Houston program pitched as high-volume "activation" invites a harder TSA posture.

- **IAH's international function creates a hard exclusion zone the pass cannot touch.** CBP-controlled Federal Inspection Services space, international arrivals corridors, and CIDA/sterile-transit areas are governed by CBP, not TSA or HAS. A visitor pass grants access to the domestic sterile area only; conflating that with international space is both a regulatory violation and a design trap [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-XII/subchapter-C/part-1542].

## 2. Evidence: What Is Regulation vs. What Is Politics

### This is regulation (TSA requires it; the airport cannot change it)

**The sterile area and its access are federally defined.** A "sterile area" is the post-checkpoint zone whose access TSA controls through screening; a "non-traveling individual" is someone an airport "seeks to issue an authorization to enter the sterile area … for some other purpose permitted by TSA" (49 CFR § 1540.5). The phrase "permitted by TSA" is the whole game: sterile-area visitor access exists only at TSA's sufferance [Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/1540.5].

**Screening and watch-list vetting are non-negotiable.** Under Secure Flight (49 CFR Part 1560), the airport must transmit Secure Flight Passenger Data on each non-traveler to TSA, which vets the individual against the No-Fly and Selectee lists before authorizing entry; the visitor then undergoes the identical physical screening as a passenger, subject to the same prohibited-items rules [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-XII/subchapter-C/part-1560]. SEA's own program documentation confirms it collects personal data specifically "to conduct checks of TSA's Secure Flight watch lists" [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/node/10875].

**The program lives inside the Airport Security Program (ASP).** Any Houston visitor pass requires a TSA-approved ASP amendment under 49 CFR Part 1542 Subpart B. TSA approval is discretionary, airport-specific, and revocable — there is no appeal to a rulemaking record because there is no rulemaking [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-XII/subchapter-C/part-1542/subpart-B]. **Timeline implication:** the launch date is not HAS's to set. It is gated by TSA's Federal Security Director sign-off, which historically moves on TSA's calendar, not the airport's.

### This is politics / discretion (it can change — and has)

**The federal door opened recently and could close.** Reporting is explicit that the current wave of programs "comes after the Transportation Security Administration amended its regulations last year, giving airports more flexibility to allow visitors without tickets into the gate area" [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/01/travel/visitor-pass-us-airports]. This is a posture shift inside TSA, not a statute. A future administration, or a single security incident, can reverse it without Congress — the way TSA ended the crewmember expedited-screening program in 2025 after abuse, unilaterally and quickly [Source: https://www.thestreet.com/travel/a-major-change-is-coming-to-a-decades-old-tsa-rule-at-u-s-airports-guest-pass].

**Appropriations risk is real and recent.** The early-2026 shutdown left TSA officers working unpaid, drove 300+ resignations within a month, and produced multi-hour lines at IAH, ATL, and MSY [Source: https://us.cnn.com/2026/03/31/us/tsa-shutdown-over-airports-wait-times]. A visitor program is discretionary checkpoint demand; under those conditions it is suspended first and quietly. Houston should assume 1–2 multi-week suspensions per decade driven by events entirely outside its control.

**Houston's municipal politics are inescapable.** Because HAS is a City department, the program's staffing and technology costs appear in the aviation budget that City Council adopts each year (the FY2026 budget was presented to Council in a May workshop) [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/council/committees/fy26workshops/airport-may14.pdf]. That is both a vulnerability — any council member can question spending "so non-passengers can shop" — and a lever: a visitor pass is a visible, low-capital, constituent-facing amenity that a mayor can cut a ribbon on. The politics cut both ways; they are not neutral background.

## 3. Case Studies: When a Regulatory or Political Force Set the Outcome

**myPITpass (Pittsburgh, 2017 → suspended 2020, never restored).** The template program. Pittsburgh proved the concept worked and posed no measurable added risk per its CEO — yet the deciding forces were external: COVID killed it, and a terminal-construction program kept it dead [Source: https://airportimprovement.com/article/pittsburgh-intl-opens-airside-areas-non-ticketed-visitors/]. **Lesson for Houston:** a program's survival is uncorrelated with how well it is run. IAH's own multi-year terminal capital work is exactly the kind of condition that has shelved these programs elsewhere. Sequence the pilot around, not into, construction phases.

**SEA Visitor Pass (Seattle, 300/day cap).** The Port of Seattle runs the largest sustained program, and it is deliberately throttled: 300 passes/day, first-come-first-served, one checkpoint, 5 a.m.–10 p.m., explicitly "subject to TSA approval," and barred from PreCheck/CLEAR lanes [Source: https://www.portseattle.org/node/10875]. **Lesson:** the mature-state equilibrium is small and rigid. The cap is not timidity; it is the price of TSA tolerance. Any Houston pro forma assuming thousands of daily visitors is assuming a program TSA has never approved.

**Flight-attendant opposition, PIT.** When PIT launched, the APFA president called TSA's move a "bad idea" that should be "reversed"; the airport's CEO countered that it posed no added threat [Source: https://noticias.foxnews.com/travel/flight-attendants-blast-pittsburgh-airports-new-tsa-rules-its-a-bad-idea.amp]. **Lesson:** organized labor with a national megaphone will frame this as security erosion regardless of the screening facts. Houston should expect the same and pre-position the "identical screening, watch-list vetted" rebuttal — because it is the only rebuttal that is federally true.

**IAH shutdown queues (March 2026).** IAH was singled out nationally for four-hour waits driven purely by federal staffing, not airport design [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/03/25/us/houston-bush-airport-tsa-wait-times]. **Lesson:** IAH's checkpoint headroom is thin and federally hostage. HOU (Hobby), smaller and largely domestic point-to-point, is the lower-risk pilot site. Leading at IAH puts the program's reputation on the most congested, most internationally complex checkpoint in the system.

## 5. What Can and Cannot Move — and On What Timeframe

**Fixed on any project timeline (do not build a plan that assumes otherwise):**
- Identical screening + Secure Flight watch-list vetting for every visitor. Federal, permanent.
- TSA's discretionary, revocable approval via the ASP amendment. Houston can apply; it cannot self-authorize, and it cannot prevent suspension during checkpoint stress or a security event.
- Exclusion of CBP/FIS international space. Never available to a visitor pass.

**Movable, but on TSA's calendar, not Houston's (allow 6–12+ months):**
- The ASP amendment approval itself. This is the true schedule driver — not vendor selection, not app development.

**Movable, and within Houston's control (subject to TSA sign-off):**
- Daily cap, operating hours, blackout dates, checkpoint designation, application lead time, and the choice of HOU vs. IAH as pilot site. These are the design levers HAS actually owns.

**Anchoring to Houston's decision calendar:**
- **City Council budget cycle (annual, spring workshops → summer adoption):** program staffing/technology costs must land as an aviation-fund line item; brief council members *before* they read it as "spending for non-passengers." [Source: https://www.houstontx.gov/council/committees/fy26workshops/airport-may14.pdf]
- **TSA ASP amendment window:** submit early; treat FSD approval as the critical-path gate. Nothing launches until it clears.
- **Terminal capital phasing at IAH:** do not overlay a visitor pilot on active checkpoint construction — that is precisely what stranded myPITpass.
- **Federal appropriations/shutdown exposure:** build automatic suspension triggers tied to published checkpoint wait thresholds so the program pauses itself before it becomes a headline.

**Bottom line for the Strategist:** the regulatory environment permits this program and the security case is federally winnable. The binding constraints are not legal — they are (a) TSA's discretionary, revocable approval, (b) IAH's politically exposed checkpoint capacity, and (c) Houston's municipal-department governance, which keeps the program permanently visible to City Council. Recommend a capped, HOU-first pilot with hard congestion stop-criteria and pre-negotiated TSA suspension triggers. Any plan that assumes durable, at-scale, IAH-led access is assuming away the three forces that actually decide this.
