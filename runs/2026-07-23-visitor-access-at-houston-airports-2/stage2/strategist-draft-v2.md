On the worst mornings of the March 2026 federal shutdown, the security checkpoints at George Bush Intercontinental were the story. Houston's two airports posted the highest TSA officer callout rates in the country — 42.4 percent at Bush and 47.4 percent at Hobby — with roughly half the lanes dark and waits that ran past four hours.[^1] The officers who should have been staffing the empty lanes were working without pay or had stopped coming in.

Now picture the person a visitor pass program is built to serve, standing somewhere in that line. Call him a composite, because he is one: the surveys at the airports that run these programs say the modal visitor is not a romantic at all but someone meeting an arriving traveler, with a minority who come to watch airplanes or have dinner.[^13] Our man is the minority. He has bought a cheap ticket he does not intend to use — the public-safety literature notes drily that a bad actor can already buy a $39 fare — purely to get through the checkpoint, buy a coffee, and stand near a departure gate.

Set that image down and look at it, because it is the whole decision. Houston Airports is being asked whether to install a velvet rope at the door of the most congested checkpoints in the national system on the days that make the news — to invite non-travelers, in a controlled trickle, through the one asset the system is periodically worst at managing. The reflex answer is no, and the reflex is wrong, but only barely, and only if the program is built with a discipline that runs against everything the word "amenity" implies.

Here is the argument in three sentences. A visitor pass program is trivially affordable and, at Houston's specific checkpoints in Houston's specific moment, genuinely capable of doing harm — the cost and the risk live not on the balance sheet but in peak-hour lane-minutes at a system that periodically has none to give. The right decision is yes, but a narrow yes: a hard-capped, off-peak, single-checkpoint, advance-vetted, twelve-month pilot at Hobby, funded outside the airline rate base, sold as community relations and nothing else, with a kill switch wired into the operations center before the first pass is issued. Any larger version — at Bush, at scale, justified by revenue or efficiency, layered onto the terminal construction now underway — is a program that will be switched off in front of angry families on the days they most want it, and it deserves to fail.

## Executive summary

1. **The program is financially invisible and cannot be decided on money.** Houston Airports runs a $778.4 million annual revenue-fund budget against a $2.92 billion five-year capital plan; a visitor pass program is a low-six- to low-seven-figure line item that will not move the debt-service coverage ratio, the credit rating, or the cost per enplanement in either direction.[^2][^3] "We can't afford it" is not an available answer — and neither is "it pays for itself."

2. **Checkpoint throughput, not capital, is the asset the program consumes — and Houston is short of it at exactly the wrong times.** A 300-per-day cap is well under one percent of Bush's daily enplaned passengers, invisible in aggregate but concentrated at specific lanes and hours.[^4] Those are the same lanes that hit four-hour waits during the 2026 shutdown, when national frontline TSA absence rose to roughly 6 percent from a 2 percent baseline; at Houston's two airports, the local callout peaks — 42.4 and 47.4 percent — were the worst in the country.[^1][^5]

3. **Every functioning peer program is small, rationed, and built to be switched off — the cap is the design, not a caveat.** Seattle runs the largest operating cap in the country at 300 a day through a single checkpoint; Philadelphia holds 100, Detroit 75, New Orleans 50 on weekdays.[^10][^14][^13][^12] Realized demand runs far below cap — Philadelphia logged just over 10,300 visitors in roughly two years against a ceiling that would permit 36,500 a year — which shrinks both the congestion risk and the benefit case.[^14]

4. **The pioneer quit and never came back, for Houston's reason — but Houston also holds a card the pioneer never did.** Pittsburgh invented the modern visitor pass in 2017 and ended it in 2020, never restarting because the airport went into terminal construction.[^17] Houston just closed out a $1.468 billion redevelopment at Bush and is starting a Domestic Terminal Program there and a $470 million concourse expansion at Hobby — the same capital disruption that killed Pittsburgh's program.[^3][^20] The countervailing fact, which this report takes seriously and then overrules: that Bush redevelopment just added fresh checkpoint capacity, and one honest reading is that there is no better moment to absorb a small visitor load than the year new lanes come online.[^15]

5. **The security question is federally settled; the accountability question is not.** Every visitor is screened identically to a ticketed passenger and vetted against the Secure Flight watch lists before entry, under 49 CFR Parts 1542 and 1560 — a vetted visitor is arguably a lower individual risk than a walk-up passenger.[^6] But the deadliest airport attacks happen in the public, pre-security zones a pass never touches: at Fort Lauderdale in 2017 a ticketed passenger retrieved a gun from checked baggage and killed five in a baggage claim in under eighty seconds, then a phantom-shooter rumor drove a self-evacuation onto the tarmac.[^34] Visitors are not on any manifest, and density in a concourse is the variable that turns "is everyone out?" into an unanswerable question.

6. **In Houston the airlines are the governance structure, and the design must keep them out of it.** United controls 58.7 percent of Bush and roughly 80 percent of its flights; Southwest controls more than 93 percent of Hobby.[^21][^22] Fund any part of the program through the airline rate base and it falls inside the Majority-in-Interest clause, handing United a procedural lever right before United's Terminal E lease expires on January 31, 2030 — the next real ratemaking window.[^23][^24] Keep it on the non-airline side of the ledger and it stays the airport's decision.

7. **Houston's governance makes this a City of Houston policy, not an internal management choice.** Houston Airports is a department of the City, not an independent authority; the Director of Aviation is a mayoral appointee confirmed by City Council, and the program's costs appear as a municipal line item visible to sixteen council members.[^25] Expect the flight-attendants' union to call it what it called Pittsburgh's — a "bad idea" that will "clog already frustratingly long TSA security lines" — in week one, and pre-position the only rebuttal that is federally true: identical screening, watch-list vetted.[^31]

8. **The honest justification is community value, and it should be sold as exactly that.** At the one airport that launched on a revenue rationale — Seattle — the program was meant to grow non-aeronautical revenue and then, in the Port's own words, "took on a life of its own, turning into more of a customer experience initiative."[^26] Houston should pick the narrow, sympathetic, politically potent use cases — military send-offs, elderly and first-time travelers, sanctioned events — and book the program as goodwill, not ROI.

**The recommendation, in the Director's terms.** Run a twelve-month pilot at Hobby, not Bush — long enough to capture a full seasonal cycle including one peak-blackout period. Cap it at 50 a day; confine it to one designated checkpoint; blackout the morning departure bank and every peak date on a pre-published calendar. Require advance application with TSA adjudicating every applicant days ahead — the Philadelphia firewall, not a curbside kiosk. Fund it from non-airline money so it never enters Majority-in-Interest. Give the operations center and the public-safety chair an unreviewable switch to pause it, and publish the stop criteria at launch: a checkpoint-wait threshold that auto-pauses issuance, an incident threshold, and a utilization floor below which the pilot is not renewed. Set the cap itself from Houston's own hourly checkpoint data, not an industry rule of thumb. Estimated all-in cost: $0.3 million to $1.5 million a year, a figure Houston must confirm with a bottom-up model because no peer airport publishes it.[^40] First ninety days: file the security-program amendment and treat its approval — six to twelve months on TSA's calendar, not the airport's — as the critical path; brief City Council before they read the line item as "spending so non-passengers can shop"; and negotiate a written staffing understanding with HPD and HFD before booking a benefit whose cost lands on departments the airport does not command.

## The argument

### First, say precisely what the thing is

Half the arguments about visitor passes are really arguments about a word. So define it before defending it. A visitor pass is a floor pass: a revocable, TSA-vetted, airport-issued authorization for a named non-traveler to enter the **domestic sterile area** — the post-checkpoint concourse — for a bounded window, after passing exactly the same physical screening and Secure Flight watch-list check as a ticketed passenger.[^6] It is not an airline gate pass, which the carrier issues under its own authority for its own passengers' guests. It is not employee badging, which grants unescorted access to secured and SIDA areas after a fingerprint-based criminal-history check and security-threat assessment. It is not escorted access, where a badged employee vouches for and physically accompanies a visitor. And it is emphatically not access to the Federal Inspection Services space — the CBP-controlled international arrivals halls and sterile-transit corridors — which no visitor pass can ever touch, because that space is governed by Customs and Border Protection, not TSA, and is a hard exclusion zone.[^30cbp] At an international gateway like Bush, that last boundary is a design constraint, not a footnote: a visitor pass buys the domestic concourse and nothing on the international side of the border line.

Hold that definition, because every cost and every risk in what follows is a property of that specific, narrow thing — a screened body in a domestic concourse for a few hours — and not of the looser thing the word "visitor" tends to summon.

### Start with the number that ends the money conversation

The instinct of anyone who reviews airport proposals is to size the thing against the budget it lives in, and here the exercise is almost comically decisive. Houston Airports spends $778.4 million a year out of its revenue fund, carries $166 million of annual debt service, and is midway through a $2.92 billion capital plan.[^2][^3] A visitor pass program — portal, digital passes, a few staff, signage, legal review, insurance — is plausibly $300,000 to $1.5 million a year, all in.[^40] That is less than two-tenths of one percent of the annual budget. It rounds to zero.

This does not make the decision trivial. It relocates it. Because the money is immaterial, every argument that leans on money is fighting on ground too soft to hold. The airport cannot decline the program because it is unaffordable — it sits on a 1.8x debt-service coverage ratio, well above its self-imposed 1.5x floor, more than 450 days of cash on hand, and an AA- credit that Fitch upgraded on roughly $2.2 billion of subordinate-lien bonds in early 2026.[^39] By the same token, no one can defend the program on the promise that it will pay for itself. A visitor who behaves like an average passenger is worth about ten dollars in concessions and parking — Houston's non-airline revenue runs $10.16 per passenger — and at even 300 visitors a day that is a fraction of a million dollars a year of gross upside before any cost, most of it fictional because greeters get dropped off and buy nothing.[^16] Seattle already ran this experiment. It launched its 2018 program explicitly to grow non-aeronautical revenue, and the revenue thesis quietly lost to the experience thesis.[^26]

So the money is a wash, in both directions. What is left is the one thing that is scarce.

### The scarce thing is a lane at 6:10 a.m.

The Department of Homeland Security designs a screening lane to a target of about 300 passengers an hour.[^7] But a design target is not a measured rate; airport planners work from 150 to 250 passengers per hour per lane in the real world, and any Houston cap should be set from Houston's own checkpoint data, not a federal ceiling.[^8] Run the arithmetic honestly across that range. A 300-a-day visitor cap spread over seventeen operating hours is roughly eighteen visitors an hour. Against a 300-per-hour lane that is six percent of one lane; against a real-world 150-per-hour lane it is twelve percent. Against Bush's 48.45 million passengers a year, a 100-a-day cap is 0.075 percent of throughput.[^9] The annual numbers are real, and they are the numbers boosters cite, and they are true, and they are also beside the point.

The load a visitor program creates is not annual and it is not average. It is the handful of extra bodies arriving at one checkpoint during one hour. Five different operators have independently concluded that this load must yield to passenger throughput, and each has engineered its program to keep visitors in the trough between departure banks. Seattle funnels its entire program — all 300 daily passes — through a single lane, Checkpoint 4, and tells applicants that at peak times they should arrive "two or more hours" early.[^10] Philadelphia bars same-day sign-up entirely and admits only on a digital pass adjudicated one to seven days out.[^14] Detroit runs 4 a.m. to 9 p.m. against a 75-a-day ceiling.[^13] Tampa meters guests in six-person blocks per two-hour window, and says out loud that the cap exists "to help reduce traffic in the trams and airsides."[^11] New Orleans opens at 11 a.m., skipping the entire morning bank, and limits each person to one visit a month.[^12] These are not customer-service niceties. They are five operators reaching the same conclusion and building their programs to keep visitors off the top of a departure push.

Now put that design problem inside Houston. Bush runs 30-to-45-minute peak waits on ordinary days, with Terminal E touching 45 minutes even after the new International Central Processor checkpoints came online, and up to an hour early on Sundays.[^15] That is the good state. The bad state is the March 2026 shutdown, when the resource that clears those lines — TSA officers, staffed to a federal budget the airport neither sets nor funds — simply did not show up. A visitor pass program adds discretionary demand to precisely that lane. On a normal Tuesday the addition is a rounding error. On the mornings that make the news, any discretionary load is indefensible — which is exactly why the throttle exists, and why the program has to be capable of switching itself off in a single phone call.

The blunt version of this point belongs to the operator: the airport does not own the lane. TSA owns the lane. The airport owns the permission slip and the floor space on either side of it. That single fact determines which levers Houston actually controls — the daily cap, the hours, the checkpoint, the blackout dates, the marketing throttle — and which it can only ask nicely about, which is whether the lanes are open at all.

### The dead pioneer, and the fresh capacity that argues the other way

In September 2017, Pittsburgh International became the first American airport since the September 11 attacks to let non-travelers back through security. myPITpass was a genuine innovation — weekday nine-to-five, same-day sign-up, standard screening — and it worked. It ended in 2020, and the part that matters for Houston is that it never came back, and the reason was not COVID and not a security incident. It was construction. Pittsburgh went into a new terminal program and left the pass dark.[^17]

There is a deeper truth underneath the Pittsburgh story, uncomfortable for anyone who wants to cite the program as proof the model is easy. Pittsburgh could open its terminal to the public in the first place because US Airways had dehubbed it in 2004, collapsing it to 75 gates and under 250 daily departures, with half of two concourses literally walled off to save on utilities.[^18] The spare checkpoint capacity that made myPITpass cheap was stranded capacity — the byproduct of a carrier walking away. The feasibility of these programs is inversely correlated with the health of the hub. The airports that can run them easily are the ones the airlines abandoned.

Houston is the opposite case — a constrained, growing dual-hub system where United is spending billions and Bush's cost per enplanement climbed from $10.66 to $11.17 between FY2024 and FY2025.[^19] And Houston is doing to itself, on purpose, the thing that killed the Pittsburgh program by accident: it just wrapped a $1.468 billion redevelopment at Bush and is opening a Domestic Terminal Program there, while Hobby finishes a $470 million, seven-gate West Concourse expansion.[^3][^20]

Here is where intellectual honesty requires a detour, because the strongest in-house rebuttal to this whole timing argument comes from the operator's chair, and it deserves to be stated at full strength before it is answered. The Bush redevelopment did not only compress space — it delivered new screening capacity. The International Central Processor added checkpoints and a certified bag system in early 2026. The honest operator's reading is that Houston has fresh checkpoint capacity right now, uniquely, and that there is no better moment in a decade to absorb a modest, capped, off-peak visitor load than the year the new lanes come online. Timing, on this reading, cuts both ways.

It does cut both ways — and it still cuts against launching at Bush. Three reasons. First, the new ICP capacity was built to serve Terminal E's international flows, and Terminal E was still posting 45-minute waits *with* the new checkpoints online; the slack is thinner than the ribbon-cutting suggests.[^15] Second, the Domestic Terminal Program is next, and a multi-year construction sequence will consume the very circulation width and sterile square footage a visitor program needs — new capacity delivered in the middle of an ongoing capital program is not durable slack, it is a window that is already closing. Third, and simplest: the fresh capacity is at Bush, and the recommendation sends the pilot to Hobby. The capacity argument, taken at face value, is an argument for piloting at the airport this report declines to pilot at. So the honest disposition is not to ignore the COO's point but to bank it: if the pilot proves out at Hobby and the Domestic Terminal Program leaves Bush with demonstrated, durable off-peak headroom, that is the evidence — not a projection — on which a later expansion to Bush could ride. Phasing is how the capacity argument gets its due without betting the launch on it.

One more construction fact has to be faced rather than waved past, because this report kills Bush partly on construction and then sends the pilot to an airport that is also under construction. Hobby's West Concourse expansion — $470 million, seven gates — completes in 2027.[^20] The public record does not tell me where that work sits relative to Hobby's existing checkpoint and concourses, and I am not going to assert it doesn't compress circulation when I cannot source that. So it becomes a launch condition, not an assumption: before a pass is issued, confirm the West Concourse construction phasing and site the pilot checkpoint away from active work; if the geometry is compromised, sequence the launch after 2027 completion. The difference between Bush and Hobby here is not that one is under construction and one is not. It is scale, geometry, and the ability to place a single pilot checkpoint clear of the disruption — and that difference is real, but it has to be verified, not adjectivally assumed.

### Demand is modest, which is both the reassurance and the warning

The programs that survive do so at token scale, and the token scale is not an accident of caution — it is what demand actually is. Detroit has issued about 25,000 passes since October 2023, roughly 40 a day against a 75-a-day cap.[^13] Philadelphia logged just over 10,300 Wingmate visitors in about two years against a ceiling that would allow more than 36,500 a year.[^14] Realized daily averages run in the low tens. That is reassuring on congestion: the program will not flood a concourse. It is sobering on benefit: the concession spend, the community goodwill, and the escort-hour savings are all proportional to a small, self-selecting trickle, not a terminal full of new foot traffic.

The escort-labor savings deserve particular skepticism, because they are the number most likely to be smuggled into a business case to make it balance. The categories of non-ticketed access that actually consume labor today — unaccompanied minors, reduced-mobility passengers, badged employees — are airline-run or SIDA-controlled, and none of them converts to a public visitor pass.[^43] A visitor pass serves a want: greet a traveler, watch the planes, have dinner before a deployment. It is real, and it is worth something, but it is not an operational cost driver, and any lifecycle model that books escort-hour savings against program cost is manufacturing an offset. The honest ledger shows a labor swap, not a saving: the program reclaims some discrete escort hours from sanctioned events and meet-and-assist, and it adds a queue-monitor position, a lost-visitor call at 7 p.m., and a new line in the evacuation headcount.

### The airlines are the real board of directors

A visitor pass looks like a customer-experience question. In Houston it is unavoidably a question about shared infrastructure that two carriers dominate and largely pay for. At Bush, United holds 58.7 percent of the market — roughly 73 percent of enplanements counting its regional flying, and about 80 percent of flights — and runs the airport as its third-largest hub.[^21][^25] At Hobby, Southwest holds more than 93 percent.[^22] Neither airport has a diversified tenant base to dilute a single carrier's objection. Whatever the two dominant carriers decide about a visitor program is, functionally, what happens.

This matters because of a clause most travelers have never heard of. Houston runs a hybrid-compensatory use-and-lease agreement, and its capital projects funded through the airline rate base are subject to a Majority-in-Interest provision that lets signatory carriers review and, by a threshold of activity share, approve or block them.[^23] With United at roughly 59 percent activity, its consent on a rate-base project is close to decisive. A visitor program funded from non-airline money sits outside that clause and outside airline consent — it is genuinely the airport's decision. The moment any of its cost lands in the terminal cost center, it moves inside the clause, and it hands United a lever it does not currently have, at the worst possible time. United's Terminal E lease expires January 31, 2030, and the special-facility financing on the international program runs to November 2053; the next real ratemaking negotiation is the back half of this decade.[^24] A leadership team that arrives at that table having volunteered a new recoverable amenity as a bargaining chip has made a bad trade for a rounding error. The design rule writes itself: keep the program on the non-airline side of the ledger, and it stays the airport's to run.

Resist, here, the temptation to lean on concession revenue as a reason the carriers will bless the program — in either direction. Under Houston's hybrid-compensatory structure, whether visitor concession spend flows back to the airport's discretionary funds or offsets airline costs is genuinely unconfirmed in the public record, and the airline strategist's instruction is explicit: confirm how the agreement treats concession revenue before assuming any carrier supports the program on economic grounds.[^23] So this report does not claim the airport keeps the upside at Bush, nor that Southwest captures it at Hobby. It claims only what is safe to claim: fund the program off the rate base regardless of how the concession question resolves, and make resolving that question a ninety-day action item rather than a rhetorical crutch.

There is a subtler carrier point. United's stated objection to a program that crowds its connecting-bank checkpoints will be "security and congestion." Its actual objection will be cost and throughput. Those two reasons are not identical, and it is worth naming the gap when it appears. The way to deny United both the stated grievance and the real one is the Seattle architecture: a designated checkpoint, capped, off-peak, so the program never competes for the lane-minutes United sells to connecting passengers. Southwest's fast point-to-point turns at Hobby are, if anything, even more congestion-intolerant — one more reason Hobby's cleaner single-signatory environment is the better place to prove the model.

### The version the carriers cannot veto — and why it is not quite the product

There is a cheaper, safer program hiding inside this one, and the Director's first question will be why the report does not simply recommend it. Confine the whole concept to public, pre-security space — a landside observation area, a greeting hall, sanctioned events outside the checkpoint — and almost all of the carrier risk evaporates, because the program never touches the throughput or the rate base the airlines control. That is the version a strategist can recommend at a constrained dual-hub with the highest confidence that no one kills it, and it should be said plainly: the landside version is the floor, and Houston can do it tomorrow, with no ASP amendment and no checkpoint exposure at all.

But the landside version is not quite the product, and it is worth being honest about why. The thing a visitor pass sells that no landside amenity can is the gate moment — standing at the window as the aircraft pushes back, walking the returning traveler out of the concourse, the last dinner before a deployment on the secure side of the line. That moment is the entire emotional payload, and it lives past the checkpoint. A landside observation deck is a genuine good and Houston should build toward one; it is not a substitute for sterile-side access, and pretending otherwise concedes the argument to a program that delivers less of what people actually come for. The right way to hold both truths is sequencing: treat landside programming as Phase 0 — the veto-proof, ASP-free goodwill Houston can stand up immediately — and treat the capped sterile-side pilot as Phase 1, the thing the landside experience earns its way toward.

### The federal frame is a permission, not a fight — and permissions get revoked

None of this requires an act of Congress or an environmental review. In 2024 and 2025 TSA amended its requirements to give airports more flexibility to admit non-ticketed visitors, and the count of participating US airports rose from about 20 in mid-2025 to 28 by January 2026.[^27] Houston needs a TSA-approved amendment to its Airport Security Program — a discretionary, airport-specific, revocable permission granted on the Federal Security Director's calendar, not the airport's, which realistically means six to twelve months of lead time that no vendor selection can shorten.[^28]

Two features of that permission are load-bearing and permanent. First, the security floor is federally fixed: every visitor is screened exactly like a passenger and run against the Secure Flight watch lists before entry, under 49 CFR Parts 1542 and 1560.[^6] Any business case that imagines a lighter visitor screening path is dead on arrival, and any critique that imagines visitors as an unvetted crowd is factually wrong. Second, and cutting the other way, a permission this discretionary can be withdrawn without a rulemaking. TSA ended its decades-old crewmember expedited-screening program in 2025, unilaterally and quickly, after it was abused.[^29] A visitor program launched in good times is the first thing suspended in bad ones — which is exactly what Seattle did across a dozen June and July 2026 dates for World Cup and summer peak demand, and what Orlando did when it paused its program outright to "sort out staffing and operations," reopening only in December 2025.[^30] Suspension is not the failure mode of these programs. It is their normal operating state. Houston should assume one or two multi-week suspensions per decade driven entirely by forces outside its control, and it should build the off switch, and the pre-published blackout calendar, before it builds anything else.

Two constituencies will test the program the moment it is announced, and the political section below returns to both: organized labor, which called Pittsburgh's program a "bad idea" that would "clog already frustratingly long TSA security lines," and the data-privacy question that rides along with collecting Secure Flight Passenger Data on members of the public.[^31][^33] Neither is a reason not to proceed. Both are reasons to proceed with the rebuttal and the controls already written down.

## The counter-case, honestly presented

Here is the strongest version of the argument that Houston should not do this at all, and it does not depend on pretending the program is dangerous or that peer airports are reckless. About two dozen airports run these programs and none has produced a reported mass-casualty event or catastrophic breach in the public record.[^37] The honest case against is narrower and harder to dodge: the marginal load falls on a saturated checkpoint, the marginal benefit is a rounding error, and the whole thing depends on a TSA that could not screen Houston's actual paying passengers in 2026.

Begin where the pain is real. Bush and Hobby did not have ordinary checkpoint problems in early 2026 — they had the worst in the nation, with waits of two to four hours and at least half the lanes closed.[^1] You do not add discretionary bodies to the queue that cannot clear the ticketed passengers who paid to be there. And the bodies you add load the slowest lanes specifically: every operating program routes visitors through general screening, with PreCheck and CLEAR explicitly unavailable to pass holders.[^36] So the incremental demand does not spread across the fast lanes. It concentrates on the standard checkpoint that is Houston's binding constraint, on the days that constraint is already broken.

The benefit on the other side of that trade is small by design and vanishes exactly when it is most wanted. Seattle caps at 300 and suspends for the World Cup and summer weekends; Detroit averages about 40 a day.[^30][^13] The program is engineered to disappear on the holidays, the summer Fridays, and the big-event days when families most want to walk someone to a gate — which means the airport builds a customer expectation it plans to revoke on cue, and creating a promise and then yanking it is worse for trust than never offering it. Meanwhile the "avoided escort labor" line that would make a cost model balance is close to fictional, because the escort categories that actually consume labor are airline-run or badge-controlled and do not convert to a public pass.[^43] A free program with a 40-to-300-a-day velvet rope is a marketing and community-relations product. There is nothing wrong with that. But it should be evaluated as marketing spend, competing against other marketing dollars, not smuggled into a security-operations business case dressed as efficiency.

The regulatory and political constraints sharpen the case rather than softening it. TSA "has no official stance or rules on how airports' guest pass programs should operate, as long as they comply with security rules," which means the airport, not TSA, absorbs every residual risk the federal government declines to opine on — reconnaissance by a self-selected long-dwell population, sterile-side hand-offs, insider-assistance surface, and headcount with no passenger name record during an evacuation.[^37] And because Houston Airports is a City department, not an independent authority, all of this is permanently visible to sixteen council members and a mayor, any of whom can ask why the airport is "spending so non-passengers can shop" the first time a line makes the news.[^25] Organized labor will supply the soundbite: when Pittsburgh launched, the flight attendants' union called it a "bad idea" that would "clog already frustratingly long TSA security lines," and a council-visible city department will hear the same framing in week one.[^31] The staffing constraint cannot be argued away on a launch timeline either: 49 CFR 1542.217 requires law-enforcement staffing "in the number and manner adequate" to support the security program, and sworn hiring at HPD runs 12 to 18 months.[^38] The program can launch in 90 days; the staffing to support it cannot.

The deepest version of the objection is that Houston is choosing the hard version of an easy program. Pittsburgh's pass was cheap because a carrier left it with idle checkpoints. Detroit's is durable because its terminals have circulation headroom a peak-banked hub does not. Houston has neither the stranded capacity nor the slack. It has a dominant carrier absorbing a rising cost per enplanement, a multi-year construction program compressing its concourses, checkpoints that periodically post the worst waits in America, and a governance structure that turns every incident into a hearing. The bottom line of the case against is fair: the burden is on the recommendation to explain why Houston should spend scarce checkpoint capacity and administrative attention on 40 to 300 non-travelers a day while ticketed passengers wait two hours — and to be honest that on the peak days, the program will simply be switched off.

## Why the counter-case is insufficient

Concede the hard parts first, by name, because they are true and the recommendation has to survive them.

Concede that Bush's checkpoints were the worst in the country in early 2026, and that adding any discretionary load to that lane on that morning would be indefensible.[^1] Concede that the escort-labor savings are largely fictional and must be struck from any honest model.[^43] Concede that realized demand is small, that the concession upside is therefore small, and that the revenue case does not exist.[^13][^16] Concede that the program will be suspended on peak days, and that the suspensions are not a bug the airport can engineer away.[^30] Concede that the residual security risk lands on the airport, that visitors complicate evacuation accountability, and that the deadliest airport attacks happen in a public zone the pass never touches.[^34][^37] Concede that federal staffing is beyond Houston's control and that "adequate" LEO coverage cannot be conjured in 90 days.[^38] Concede, finally, that Houston is doing the hard version.

Every one of those concessions is an argument against a specific bad program. None of them is an argument against the specific good program, because the good program is defined precisely by conceding them.

Watch what happens when the design absorbs each objection in turn. The saturation objection is an objection to sharing United's peak-bank checkpoint at Bush on a shutdown morning. It is not an objection to 50 vetted visitors passing through one designated checkpoint at Hobby between 11 a.m. and 7 p.m. on an ordinary Tuesday, blacked out on every peak date, paused automatically the instant the wait at that checkpoint crosses a published threshold. The strongest case against conceded this directly: a well-bounded, off-peak, non-SIDA trough program adds little load even at Bush, and the objection weakens considerably if the recommendation is to pilot at Hobby rather than Bush. The saturation argument assumes the airport will not discipline the caps and hours. A serious design does not merely hope to discipline them — it hard-codes them, with an operations-center throttle that pauses visitor entry when the checkpoint wait exceeds a set threshold, no approvals required. That is not aspiration. Pittsburgh's operators watched the lines in real time and cut off myPITpass access when it threatened ticketed throughput; that single feature is the difference between a program that serves passengers and one that fights them.[^17]

There is a fair objection lurking here that the site selection does not answer: Hobby posted the *higher* callout rate of the two airports during the shutdown — 47.4 percent to Bush's 42.4 — so why is the airport with the nation's worst callout rate the "cleaner" site?[^1] Because the shutdown risk is site-independent. Federal staffing collapse hits whatever airport TSA cannot staff, and no choice of terminal cures it; it is answered by the throttle and the blackout calendar, which pause the program on exactly those mornings regardless of which airport it runs at. Site selection cures the variables site selection *can* cure — construction scale, terminal geometry, the number of checkpoints to protect, the cleanliness of the governance boundary — and on every one of those Hobby wins. The shutdown is handled by the kill switch, not the address.

The suspension objection inverts under the same pressure. Yes, the program will go dark on peak days — and the ability to switch it off unilaterally, instantly, without anyone's permission, is not the program's weakness. It is the entire reason it is safe to run. A benefit the operator can withdraw the moment it costs him is a benefit worth having. The failure mode is not suspension; it is unmanaged suspension — improvising a shutdown in front of angry families in June because no one published the blackout calendar in advance. Seattle telegraphed its World Cup suspensions on specific dates. Houston, a 2026 World Cup host city closing out its own checkpoints in the same year, must publish the blackout logic before launch and message the gap between the marketing promise and the operational reality honestly, up front. Do that, and suspension becomes a known term of service instead of a broken promise.

The security objection, taken at its strongest, actually argues for the program's shape rather than against its existence. The public-safety case is right that TSA screening is a compliance control, that it says nothing about density and accountability, and that Fort Lauderdale happened in a baggage claim no credential protects.[^34] But notice where that reasoning leads. It does not lead to "no program." It leads to a program capped hard enough that the marginal density is trivial, confined to one location so the population is knowable, backed by a live registry of who is inside the sterile area on a given pass so the evacuation headcount has an answer, and governed by a public-safety veto written into the rules rather than left to discretion. It also answers the residual-risk trio the counter-case raised and the first draft left hanging — reconnaissance, sterile-side hand-offs, and insider surface. Advance application with a named identity and TSA adjudication makes a pass-holder the *most* attributable person in the terminal, not the least; the cap and single-location design bound the dwell population to a knowable few; and the genuinely unvetted way to run reconnaissance — a refundable ticket bought under any name — remains strictly easier than applying for a pass days ahead and submitting to a watch-list check. A vetted, screened visitor is arguably a lower individual risk than a walk-up passenger, and pulling some meet-and-greet activity out of the entirely unvetted curbside crowd — the Fort Lauderdale zone — into a monitored channel could modestly reduce landside exposure at the points hardest to protect. The honest security verdict is not "reject." It is "cap it, locate it, account for it, and keep the veto" — which is the recommendation.

Even the staffing-lag objection resolves into a design requirement rather than a veto. It is true that "adequate" LEO coverage under 1542.217 cannot be stood up in a quarter and that sworn hiring runs more than a year.[^38] That is precisely why the pilot is 50 a day at one checkpoint at the smaller airport, not 300 across nine checkpoints at the larger one — because a marginal population that small, that concentrated, and that vetted does not move the staffing requirement in the first place, and because phasing is the mechanism that lets demonstrated load, not a projection, drive any later expansion. The staffing constraint does not kill the pilot. It sizes it.

So where does that leave the deepest objection — that Houston is choosing the hard version? It leaves it standing, and it is the reason the yes is narrow rather than broad. Houston genuinely lacks the stranded capacity that made Pittsburgh easy and the circulation slack that makes Detroit durable. The correct response is not to refuse the program but to refuse the hard version of it and build the easy one on purpose: not at Bush, where United's banks and the Domestic Terminal Program make every lane-minute contested, but at Hobby, where a single 24-hour terminal, a near-monopoly signatory, and a cleaner accountability boundary make the downside smallest.

A word about how confident to be in that site choice, because the first draft overclaimed it. Three lenses independently named Hobby: the strongest case against, the operational-throughput analysis, and the regulatory read all pointed to the smaller airport. Two more prescribed the same *shape* — small, capped, revocable, non-airline-funded — without naming a site: the executive-finance view and the public-safety view. And one, the operator's, actively saw a case for Bush, because the new ICP checkpoints are where the fresh capacity physically sits. That is not five-of-five convergence. It is three named, two silent on location, and one honest dissent that this report engages and overrules for stated reasons. An honest three-plus-two-with-a-dissent is persuasive enough. A manufactured consensus would have been a liability the first skeptical staffer could puncture.

The thesis holds, then, under a specific and stated condition: that Houston builds the program the concessions demand rather than the program the marketing wants. Sold as ROI, efficiency, or a revenue line, at Bush, layered onto construction, the program fails, and the counter-case correctly predicts its failure. Sold as bounded community goodwill, at Hobby, capped and sheddable and walled off from the rate base, it clears a low bar honestly — and the counter-case, read closely, agrees.

## Implications for Houston Airports: the pitch to the Director of Aviation

Director, here is the decision in the terms of your chair.

**The strongest case for.** This is the cheapest political capital on the menu. For $300,000 to $1.5 million a year in local dollars — a figure your team must confirm with a bottom-up model, because no peer airport publishes theirs — you can hand City Council and the Houston business community something a city-department airport rarely gets to offer: a constituent-facing amenity that photographs well and costs almost nothing at system scale.[^40] Reuniting a military family before a deployment, walking an 80-year-old to her gate, animating the concessions you just spent $1.468 billion rebuilding. Funded from non-airline revenue, it stays outside Majority-in-Interest and outside airline consent.[^23] It is genuinely your decision, and it is a deposit in the political account you will want to draw on in 2030.

**The strongest case against.** Three risks, in order. The precedent, not the pilot: once visitors expect access, withdrawal becomes a political event, and you would be creating an entitlement backed by a checkpoint you neither staff nor fund. The wrong side of the ledger: if cost pressure ever pushes this into rates, it enters Majority-in-Interest and becomes a United lever right before the Terminal E lease expires in January 2030.[^24] And attention: with IIJA airport funds expiring September 30, 2026, the passenger facility charge frozen at $4.50 since 2000, and a $2.92 billion capital program defining how your tenure is judged, management attention spent litigating an amenity incident at a council hearing is attention stolen from the balance sheet.[^42]

**The recommendation.** Yes — as a pilot, not a program, and at Hobby, not Bush.

- **Site:** Hobby. One terminal, simpler geometry, a near-monopoly signatory, no Bush-scale construction footprint, the cleaner accountability boundary. Prove the model where the downside is smallest — but confirm the West Concourse construction phasing first and site the pilot checkpoint clear of active work; if the geometry is compromised, sequence launch after the 2027 completion.[^20]
- **Duration:** twelve months, long enough to cover a full seasonal cycle including at least one peak-blackout period, with renewal contingent on published criteria rather than automatic.
- **Cap:** 50 a day, one designated checkpoint, with a real-time remaining-count display and an operations-center throttle that pauses issuance automatically when the checkpoint wait crosses a published threshold. Set the cap and the threshold from Hobby's own hourly checkpoint data, not a national design figure.
- **Hours:** midday to evening only. Blackout the morning departure bank entirely; blackout every peak date on a calendar published before launch, tied to demand forecasts and the 2026 World Cup.
- **Vetting:** the Philadelphia firewall. Advance application, one to seven days out, with TSA adjudicating every applicant before arrival. No same-day curbside kiosks — they push identity checks and vetting exceptions onto your terminal staff at the worst moment and place.[^14]
- **Eligibility:** narrow and sympathetic. Military and USO send-offs, credentialed elder- and first-time-traveler assistance, sanctioned events and tours. Capture most of the goodwill at a fraction of the volume and risk.
- **Funding:** non-airline sources only. The moment any cost touches the rate base, you have handed United a grievance for free.
- **Data and privacy:** the program collects Secure Flight Passenger Data on members of the public and runs on a registration platform — and Seattle's program was knocked offline by the Port of Seattle's 2024 cyberattack, the reference precedent for platform fragility.[^32][^33] Specify before launch what data is collected, who holds it, retention limits, and Texas Public Information Act exposure for a city department, and treat the vetting platform as security infrastructure, not a marketing microsite.
- **Governance:** a written throttle protocol living in the operations center, not a policy binder, and an unreviewable pause switch held jointly by the operations center and the public-safety chair. A live registry of who is inside the sterile area on a given pass, integrated into the Airport Emergency Plan before the first pass issues.
- **Do not** launch a program into the active phases of the Domestic Terminal Program at Bush. Pittsburgh already ran that experiment, and the program lost.[^17]

**Estimated cost range:** $0.3 million to $1.5 million a year — portal and digital-pass platform, identity and application processing, incremental terminal labor, communications, insurance, and legal review — plus a modest one-time stand-up. Confirm with a bottom-up model before go/no-go; the public record will not supply the number.[^40]

**First 90 days:** File the Airport Security Program amendment with the Federal Security Director — treat that approval, not vendor selection, as the critical path, and expect six to twelve months.[^28] Brief City Council members before they read a line item as "spending for non-passengers," and hand your communications team the labor rebuttal in advance: identical screening, watch-list vetted, capped, sheddable.[^31] Confirm how your use-and-lease agreement treats visitor concession revenue, so the funding decision rests on fact rather than assumption.[^23] Draft the throttle protocol, the blackout calendar, and the published stop criteria — a checkpoint-wait threshold that auto-pauses issuance, an incident threshold, and a utilization floor below which the pilot is not renewed — with the operations center and the public-safety chair. Negotiate the staffing understanding with HPD and HFD before you book a benefit whose cost you would otherwise externalize onto departments you do not command.[^38]

**The test.** Not whether visitors enjoy it. Whether, in 2030, it sits in a footnote or across the negotiating table.

---

Somewhere in that shutdown line, our composite plane-watcher is still waiting — and the program this report recommends would turn him away. Narrow eligibility keeps the Gold Star family and the first-time flyer's escort and sends the man with the throwaway ticket back to the observation area that Houston has not yet built. That is the honest shape of the thing: the program the evidence supports serves fewer people than the romance of the idea evokes, and it serves them on fewer days than they would choose. The question for Houston is not whether it can afford the velvet rope. It is whether it has the discipline to keep the rope small, to hang it at the right door, and to take it down without apology on the mornings the line already runs to the arrivals hall.
