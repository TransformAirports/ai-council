# The Velvet Rope at the Oversubscribed Door

### A visitor pass program for Houston Airports — what it costs, what it buys, and where it should live

---

At 5:40 on a March morning in 2026, the security queue at Terminal E of George Bush Intercontinental doubled back on itself twice and spilled toward the international arrivals hall. Roughly half the lanes were dark. The officers who should have been staffing them were, that week, working without pay or not working at all — Houston's two airports posted the highest TSA officer callout rates in the country, 42.4 percent at Bush and 47.4 percent at Hobby, and waits ran past four hours.[^1] Somewhere in that line stood a man who had bought a $39 ticket he did not intend to use, purely to get through the checkpoint, buy a coffee, and watch the airplanes. He is the customer a visitor pass program is designed to serve.

Set that image down and look at it, because it is the whole decision. Houston Airports is being asked whether to install a velvet rope at the door of the most oversubscribed checkpoint in Texas — to invite non-travelers, in a controlled trickle, through the one asset the system is currently worst at managing. The reflex answer is no, and the reflex is wrong, but only barely, and only if the program is built with a discipline that runs against everything the word "amenity" implies.

Here is the argument in three sentences. A visitor pass program is trivially affordable and, at Houston's specific checkpoints in Houston's specific moment, genuinely capable of doing harm — the cost and the risk live not on the balance sheet but in peak-hour lane-minutes at a system that periodically has none to give. The right decision is yes, but a narrow yes: a hard-capped, off-peak, single-checkpoint, advance-vetted pilot at Hobby, funded outside the airline rate base, sold as community relations and nothing else, with a kill switch wired into the operations center before the first pass is issued. Any larger version — at Bush, at scale, justified by revenue or efficiency, layered onto the terminal construction now underway — is a program that will be switched off in front of angry families on the days they most want it, and it deserves to fail.

---

## Executive summary

1. **The program is financially invisible and cannot be decided on money.** Houston Airports runs a $778.4 million annual revenue-fund budget against a $2.92 billion five-year capital plan; a visitor pass program is a low-six- to low-seven-figure line item that will not move the debt-service coverage ratio, the credit rating, or the cost per enplanement in either direction.[^2][^3] "We can't afford it" is not an available answer — and neither is "it pays for itself."

2. **Checkpoint throughput, not capital, is the asset the program actually consumes — and Houston is short of it at exactly the wrong times.** At the industry-standard 300-per-day cap, visitors equal roughly half a percent of Bush's ~64,000 daily enplaned passengers, invisible in aggregate but concentrated at specific lanes and hours.[^4] Those are the same lanes that hit four-hour waits during the 2026 federal shutdown, when TSA absence jumped to roughly 6 percent from a 2 percent baseline and more than 300 officers resigned inside a month.[^1][^5]

3. **Every functioning peer program is small, rationed, and built to be switched off — the cap is the design, not a caveat.** Seattle caps at 300 a day through a single checkpoint; Detroit at 75; Philadelphia's Wingmate at 100; New Orleans at 50 on weekdays.[^6][^7][^8][^9] Realized demand runs well below cap — Philadelphia logged just over 10,300 visitors in roughly two years against a ceiling that would permit 36,500 a year — which shrinks both the congestion risk and the benefit case.[^8]

4. **The pioneer quit and never came back, and the reason is Houston's reason.** Pittsburgh invented the modern visitor pass in 2017 and ended it in 2020; it never restarted, specifically because the airport went into terminal construction.[^10] Houston just closed out a $1.468 billion redevelopment at Bush and is starting a Domestic Terminal Program there and a $470 million concourse expansion at Hobby.[^3][^11] Capital disruption, not budget and not security, is the documented cause of death for these programs.

5. **The security question is federally settled; the accountability question is not.** Every visitor is screened identically to a ticketed passenger and vetted against the Secure Flight watch lists before entry, under 49 CFR Parts 1542 and 1560 — a vetted visitor is arguably a lower individual risk than a walk-up passenger.[^12] But the deadliest airport attacks happen in the public, pre-security zones a pass never touches: at Fort Lauderdale in 2017 a ticketed passenger retrieved a gun from checked baggage and killed five in a baggage claim in under eighty seconds, then a phantom-shooter rumor drove a self-evacuation onto the tarmac.[^13] Visitors are not on any manifest, and density in a concourse is the variable that turns "is everyone out?" into an unanswerable question.

6. **In Houston the airlines are the governance structure, and the design must keep them out of it.** United controls 58.7 percent of Bush and roughly 80 percent of its flights; Southwest controls more than 93 percent of Hobby.[^14][^15] Fund any part of the program through the airline rate base and it falls inside the Majority-in-Interest clause, handing United a procedural lever right before United's Terminal E lease expires on January 31, 2030 — the next real ratemaking window.[^16][^17] Keep it on the non-airline side of the ledger and it stays the airport's decision.

7. **Houston's governance makes this a City of Houston policy, not an internal management choice.** Houston Airports is a department of the City, not an independent authority; the Director of Aviation is a mayoral appointee confirmed by City Council, and the program's costs appear as a municipal line item visible to sixteen council members.[^18] That cuts both ways — a ribbon-cutting amenity a mayor can photograph, and a council hearing the first time something goes wrong.

8. **The honest justification is community value, and it should be sold as exactly that.** Seattle launched its program to grow non-aeronautical revenue and then watched it "take on a life of its own, turning into more of a customer experience initiative."[^19] The revenue thesis has historically lost to the experience thesis. Houston should pick the narrow, sympathetic, politically potent use cases — military send-offs, elderly and first-time travelers, sanctioned events — and book the program as goodwill, not ROI.

**The recommendation:** run the pilot at Hobby, not Bush; cap it at 50 a day; confine it to one checkpoint; blackout the morning departure bank and every peak date on a pre-published calendar; require advance application with TSA adjudicating every applicant days ahead; fund it from non-airline money; and give the operations center and the public-safety chair an unreviewable switch to pause it. Estimated all-in cost: $0.3 million to $1.5 million a year, a figure Houston must confirm with a bottom-up model because no peer airport publishes it.[^20] First ninety days: file the security-program amendment, brief City Council before they read it as "spending so non-passengers can shop," and write the throttle protocol.

## The argument

### Start with the number that ends the money conversation

The instinct of anyone who reviews airport proposals is to size the thing against the budget it lives in, and here the exercise is almost comically decisive. Houston Airports spends $778.4 million a year out of its revenue fund, carries $166 million of annual debt service, and is midway through a $2.92 billion capital plan.[^2][^3] A visitor pass program — portal, kiosks or digital passes, a few staff, some signage, legal review — is plausibly $300,000 to $1.5 million a year, all in.[^20] That is less than two-tenths of one percent of the annual budget. It rounds to zero.

This does not make the decision trivial. It relocates it. Because the money is immaterial, every argument that leans on money is fighting on ground too soft to hold. The airport cannot decline the program because it is unaffordable — it sits on a 1.8x debt-service coverage ratio, well above its self-imposed 1.5x floor, more than 450 days of cash on hand, and an AA- credit that Fitch upgraded on roughly $2.2 billion of subordinate-lien bonds in early 2026.[^21] By the same token, no one can defend the program on the promise that it will pay for itself. A visitor who behaves like an average passenger is worth about ten dollars in concessions and parking — Houston's non-airline revenue runs $10.16 per passenger — and at 300 visitors a day that is somewhere between half a million and a million dollars a year of gross upside before any cost, most of it fictional because greeters get dropped off and buy nothing.[^22] Seattle already ran this experiment. It launched its 2018 program explicitly "as a way to bring in more non-aeronautical revenue," and the revenue thesis quietly lost to the experience thesis.[^19] The run that produced this report ruled the "pays for itself" argument out of bounds, and it was right to.

So the money is a wash, in both directions. What is left is the one thing that is scarce.

### The scarce thing is a lane at 6:10 a.m.

TSA designs a screening lane to clear about 300 passengers an hour.[^23] Against that, a 300-a-day visitor cap spread over seventeen operating hours is roughly eighteen visitors an hour — about six percent of a single lane, less across a multi-lane checkpoint. Against Bush's 48.45 million passengers a year, a 100-a-day cap is 0.075 percent of throughput.[^24] These numbers are real, and they are the numbers boosters cite, and they are true, and they are also beside the point.

The load a visitor program creates is not annual and it is not average. It is the handful of extra bodies arriving at one checkpoint during one hour. Seattle understood this and funnels its entire program — all 300 daily passes — through a single lane, Checkpoint 4, and tells applicants plainly that at peak times they should arrive "two or more hours" before they want to be through.[^6] Tampa meters guests in six-person blocks per two-hour window, and says out loud that the cap exists "to help reduce traffic in the trams and airsides."[^25] New Orleans opens at 11 a.m., skipping the entire morning bank, and limits each person to one visit a month.[^9] These are not customer-service niceties. They are five different operators independently concluding that visitor load must yield to passenger throughput, and engineering their programs to keep visitors in the trough between departure banks, never on top of one.

Now put that design problem inside Houston. Bush runs 30-to-45-minute peak waits on ordinary days, with Terminal E touching 45 minutes even after the new International Central Processor checkpoints came online, and up to an hour early on Sundays.[^26] That is the good state. The bad state is the March 2026 shutdown, when the resource that clears those lines — TSA officers, staffed to a federal budget the airport neither sets nor funds — simply did not show up, and the wait went past four hours.[^1] A visitor pass program adds discretionary demand to precisely that lane. On a normal Tuesday the addition is a rounding error. On the mornings that make the news it is the difference between a two-hour line and a two-hour-and-ten-minute line, and every one of those ten minutes is borne by a passenger who paid to be there.

The operator's version of this point is blunt: the airport does not own the lane. TSA owns the lane. The airport owns the permission slip and the floor space on either side of it. That single fact determines which levers Houston actually controls — the daily cap, the hours, the checkpoint, the blackout dates, the marketing throttle — and which it can only ask nicely about, which is whether the lanes are open at all. A program whose value depends on lanes someone else staffs is a program that has to be capable of switching itself off in a single phone call.

### The dead pioneer is the most important benchmark

In September 2017, Pittsburgh International became the first American airport since the September 11 attacks to let non-travelers back through security. myPITpass was a genuine innovation, weekday nine-to-five, same-day sign-up, standard screening, and it worked. It ended in 2020, and here is the part that matters for Houston: it never came back, and the reason was not COVID and not a security incident. It was construction. Pittsburgh went into a new terminal program and left the pass dark.[^10]

There is a deeper truth underneath the Pittsburgh story, and it is uncomfortable for anyone who wants to cite the program as proof the model is easy. Pittsburgh could open its terminal to the public in the first place because US Airways had dehubbed it in 2004, collapsing it to 75 gates and under 250 daily departures, with half of two concourses literally walled off to save on utilities.[^27] The spare checkpoint capacity that made myPITpass cheap was stranded capacity — the byproduct of a carrier walking away. The feasibility of these programs is inversely correlated with the health of the hub. The airports that can run them easily are the ones the airlines abandoned.

Houston is the opposite case. It is a constrained, growing dual-hub system where United is spending billions and Bush's cost per enplanement climbed from $10.66 to $11.17 between FY2024 and FY2025.[^28] And Houston is doing to itself, on purpose, the thing that killed the Pittsburgh program by accident: it just wrapped a $1.468 billion redevelopment at Bush and is opening a Domestic Terminal Program there, while Hobby finishes a $470 million, seven-gate West Concourse expansion.[^3][^11] Active terminal construction compresses the very sterile-area square footage and circulation width a visitor program consumes. Layering non-ticketed bodies onto a construction-narrowed concourse is the exact condition that kept Pittsburgh dark. This is not a budget risk. It is the risk of standing up a public-facing promise you then have to break — which is worse than never making it.

### Demand is modest, which is both the reassurance and the warning

The programs that survive do so at token scale, and the token scale is not an accident of caution — it is what demand actually is. Detroit has issued about 25,000 passes since October 2023, roughly 40 a day against a 75-a-day cap.[^7] Philadelphia logged just over 10,300 Wingmate visitors in about two years against a ceiling that would allow more than 36,500 a year.[^8] Realized daily averages run in the low tens. That is reassuring on congestion: the program will not flood a concourse. It is sobering on benefit: the concession spend, the community goodwill, and the escort-hour savings are all proportional to a small, self-selecting trickle, not a terminal full of new foot traffic.

The escort-labor savings deserve particular skepticism, because they are the number most likely to be smuggled into a business case to make it balance. The categories of non-ticketed access that actually consume labor today — unaccompanied minors, reduced-mobility passengers, badged employees — are airline-run or SIDA-controlled, and none of them converts to a public visitor pass.[^29] A visitor pass serves a want: greet a traveler, watch the planes, have dinner before a deployment. It is real, and it is worth something, but it is not an operational cost driver, and any lifecycle model that books escort-hour savings against program cost is manufacturing an offset. The honest ledger shows a labor swap, not a saving: the program reclaims some discrete escort hours from sanctioned events and meet-and-assist, and it adds a queue-monitor position, a lost-visitor call at 7 p.m., and a new line in the evacuation headcount.

### The airlines are the real board of directors

A visitor pass looks like a customer-experience question. In Houston it is unavoidably a question about shared infrastructure that two carriers dominate and largely pay for. At Bush, United holds 58.7 percent of the market — roughly 73 percent of enplanements counting its regional flying, and about 80 percent of flights — and runs the airport as its third-largest hub.[^14][^18] At Hobby, Southwest holds more than 93 percent.[^15] Neither airport has a diversified tenant base to dilute a single carrier's objection. Whatever the two dominant carriers decide about a visitor program is, functionally, what happens.

This matters because of a clause most travelers have never heard of. Houston runs a hybrid-compensatory use-and-lease agreement, and its capital projects funded through the airline rate base are subject to a Majority-in-Interest provision that lets signatory carriers review and, by a threshold of activity share, approve or block them.[^16][^30] With United at roughly 59 percent activity, its consent on a rate-base project is close to decisive. A visitor program funded from non-airline money sits outside that clause and outside airline consent — it is genuinely the airport's decision. The moment any of its cost lands in the terminal cost center, it moves inside the clause, and it hands United a lever it does not currently have, at the worst possible time. United's Terminal E lease expires January 31, 2030, and the special-facility financing on the international program runs to November 2053; the next real ratemaking negotiation is the back half of this decade.[^17] A CEO who arrives at that table having volunteered a new recoverable amenity — a greeting program — as a bargaining chip has made a catastrophic trade for a rounding error. The design rule writes itself: keep the program on the non-airline side of the ledger, and it stays the airport's to run.

There is a subtler carrier point too. United's stated objection to a program that crowds its connecting-bank checkpoints will be "security and congestion." Its actual objection will be cost and throughput. Those two reasons are not identical, and it is worth naming the gap when it appears. The way to deny United both the stated grievance and the real one is the Seattle architecture: a dedicated or designated checkpoint, capped, off-peak, so the program never competes for the lane-minutes United sells to connecting passengers. Southwest's fast point-to-point turns at Hobby are, if anything, even more congestion-intolerant — but because Southwest is the near-monopoly signatory there, it would also capture most of any concession upside, which is one more reason Hobby is the cleaner pilot site.

### The federal frame is a permission, not a fight — and permissions get revoked

None of this requires an act of Congress or an environmental review. In 2024 and 2025 TSA amended its requirements to give airports more flexibility to admit non-ticketed visitors, and the count of participating US airports rose from about 20 in mid-2025 to 28 by January 2026.[^31] Houston needs a TSA-approved amendment to its Airport Security Program — a discretionary, airport-specific, revocable permission granted on the Federal Security Director's calendar, not the airport's, which realistically means six to twelve months of lead time that no vendor selection can shorten.[^32]

Two features of that permission are load-bearing and permanent. First, the security floor is federally fixed: every visitor is screened exactly like a passenger and run against the Secure Flight watch lists before entry, under 49 CFR Parts 1542 and 1560.[^12] Any business case that imagines a lighter visitor screening path is dead on arrival, and any critique that imagines visitors as an unvetted crowd is factually wrong. Second, and cutting the other way, a permission this discretionary can be withdrawn without a rulemaking. TSA ended its decades-old crewmember expedited-screening program in 2025, unilaterally and quickly, after it was abused.[^33] A visitor program launched in good times is the first thing suspended in bad ones — which is exactly what Seattle did across a dozen June and July 2026 dates for World Cup and summer peak demand, and what Orlando did when it paused its program outright to "sort out staffing and operations," reopening only in December 2025.[^34][^35] Suspension is not the failure mode of these programs. It is their normal operating state. Houston should assume one or two multi-week suspensions per decade driven entirely by forces outside its control, and it should build the off switch, and the pre-published blackout calendar, before it builds anything else.

## The counter-case, honestly presented

Here is the strongest version of the argument that Houston should not do this at all, and it does not depend on pretending the program is dangerous or that peer airports are reckless. About two dozen airports run these programs and none has produced a mass-casualty event or a catastrophic breach. The honest case against is narrower and harder to dodge: the marginal load falls on a saturated checkpoint, the marginal benefit is a rounding error, and the whole thing depends on a TSA that could not screen Houston's actual paying passengers in 2026.

Begin where the pain is real. Bush and Hobby did not have ordinary checkpoint problems in early 2026 — they had the worst in the nation, 42.4 and 47.4 percent officer callout rates, waits of two to four hours, at least half the lanes closed.[^1] You do not add discretionary bodies to the queue that cannot clear the ticketed passengers who paid to be there. And the bodies you add load the slowest lanes specifically: every operating program routes visitors through general screening, with PreCheck and CLEAR explicitly unavailable to pass holders.[^36] So the incremental demand does not spread across the fast lanes. It concentrates on the standard checkpoint that is Houston's binding constraint, on the days that constraint is already broken.

The benefit on the other side of that trade is small by design and vanishes exactly when it is most wanted. Seattle caps at 300 and suspends for the World Cup and summer weekends; Detroit averages about 40 a day.[^34][^7] The program is engineered to disappear on the holidays, the summer Fridays, and the big-event days when families most want to walk someone to a gate — which means the airport is building a customer expectation it plans to revoke on cue, and creating a promise and then yanking it is worse for trust than never offering it. Meanwhile the "avoided escort labor" line that would make a cost model balance is close to fictional, because the escort categories that actually consume labor are airline-run or badge-controlled and do not convert to a public pass.[^29] A free program with a 40-to-300-a-day velvet rope is a marketing and community-relations product. There is nothing wrong with that. But it should be evaluated as marketing spend, competing against other marketing dollars, not smuggled into a security-operations business case dressed as efficiency.

The regulatory and political constraints sharpen the case rather than softening it. TSA "has no official stance or rules on how airports' guest pass programs should operate, as long as they comply with security rules," which means the airport, not TSA, absorbs every residual risk the federal government declines to opine on — reconnaissance by a self-selected long-dwell population, sterile-side hand-offs, insider-assistance surface, and headcount with no passenger name record during an evacuation.[^37] And because Houston Airports is a City department, not an independent authority, all of this is permanently visible to sixteen council members and a mayor, any of whom can ask why the airport is "spending so non-passengers can shop" the first time a line makes the news.[^18] The public-safety chair adds the constraint that cannot be argued away on a launch timeline: 49 CFR 1542.217 requires law-enforcement staffing "in the number and manner adequate" to support the security program, and sworn hiring at HPD runs 12 to 18 months.[^38] The program can launch in 90 days; the staffing to support it cannot.

The deepest version of the objection is that Houston is choosing the hard version of an easy program. Pittsburgh's pass was cheap because a carrier left it with idle checkpoints. Detroit's is durable because its terminals have circulation headroom a peak-banked hub does not. Houston has neither the stranded capacity nor the slack. It has a dominant carrier absorbing a rising cost per enplanement, a multi-year construction program compressing its concourses, a checkpoint that periodically posts the worst waits in America, and a governance structure that turns every incident into a hearing. The contrarian's bottom line is fair: the strategist's burden is to explain why Houston should spend scarce checkpoint capacity and administrative attention on 40 to 300 non-travelers a day while ticketed passengers wait two hours — and to be honest that on the peak days, the program will simply be switched off.

## Why the counter-case is insufficient

Concede the hard parts first, by name, because they are true and the recommendation has to survive them.

Concede that Bush's checkpoints were the worst in the country in early 2026, and that adding any discretionary load to that lane on that morning would be indefensible.[^1] Concede that the escort-labor savings are largely fictional and must be struck from any honest model.[^29] Concede that realized demand is small, that the concession upside is therefore small, and that the revenue case does not exist.[^7][^22] Concede that the program will be suspended on peak days, and that the suspensions are not a bug the airport can engineer away.[^34] Concede that the residual security risk lands on the airport, that visitors complicate evacuation accountability, and that the deadliest airport attacks happen in a public zone the pass never touches.[^13][^37] Concede that federal staffing is beyond Houston's control and that "adequate" LEO coverage cannot be conjured in 90 days.[^38] Concede, finally, that Houston is doing the hard version.

Every one of those concessions is an argument against a specific bad program. None of them is an argument against the specific good program, because the good program is defined precisely by conceding them.

Watch what happens when the design absorbs each objection in turn. The saturation objection is an objection to sharing United's peak-bank checkpoint at Bush on a shutdown morning. It is not an objection to 50 vetted visitors passing through one designated checkpoint at Hobby between 11 a.m. and 7 p.m. on an ordinary Tuesday, blacked out on every peak date, paused automatically the instant the wait at that checkpoint crosses a published threshold. The contrarian who built the strongest case against conceded this directly: "a well-bounded post-security, non-SIDA trough program adds little load," and "if the recommendation is 'pilot at HOU, not IAH,' my strongest objection weakens considerably." The saturation argument assumes the airport will not discipline the caps and hours. A serious design does not merely hope to discipline them — it hard-codes them, with an operations-center throttle that pauses visitor entry when the checkpoint wait exceeds a set threshold, no approvals required. That is not aspiration. Pittsburgh's operators watched the lines in real time and cut off myPITpass access when it threatened ticketed throughput; that single feature is the difference between a program that serves passengers and one that fights them.[^10]

The suspension objection inverts under the same pressure. Yes, the program will go dark on peak days — and the ability to switch it off unilaterally, instantly, without anyone's permission, is not the program's weakness. It is the entire reason it is safe to run. A benefit the operator can withdraw the moment it costs him is a benefit worth having. The failure mode is not suspension; it is unmanaged suspension — improvising a shutdown in front of angry families in June because no one published the blackout calendar in advance. Seattle telegraphed its World Cup suspensions on specific dates. Houston, a 2026 World Cup host city closing out its own checkpoints in the same year, must publish the blackout logic before launch and message the gap between the marketing promise and the operational reality honestly, up front. Do that, and suspension becomes a known term of service instead of a broken promise.

The security objection, taken at its strongest, actually argues for the program's shape rather than against its existence. The public-safety chair is right that TSA screening is a compliance control, that it says nothing about density and accountability, and that Fort Lauderdale happened in a baggage claim no credential protects.[^13] But notice where that reasoning leads. It does not lead to "no program." It leads to a program capped hard enough that the marginal density is trivial, confined to one location so the population is knowable, backed by a live registry of who is inside the sterile area on a given pass so the evacuation headcount has an answer, and governed by a public-safety veto written into the rules rather than left to discretion. The same chair concedes that a vetted, watch-listed, screened visitor is arguably a lower individual risk than a walk-up passenger, and that pulling some meet-and-greet activity out of the entirely unvetted curbside crowd — the Fort Lauderdale zone — into a monitored channel could modestly reduce landside exposure at the points hardest to protect. The honest security verdict is not "reject." It is "cap it, locate it, account for it, and keep the veto" — which is the recommendation.

Even the staffing-lag objection resolves into a design requirement rather than a veto. It is true that "adequate" LEO coverage under 1542.217 cannot be stood up in a quarter and that sworn hiring runs more than a year.[^38] That is precisely why the pilot is 50 a day at one checkpoint at the smaller airport, not 300 across nine checkpoints at the larger one — because a marginal population that small, that concentrated, and that vetted does not move the staffing requirement in the first place, and because phasing is the mechanism that lets the demonstrated load, not a projection, drive any later expansion. The staffing constraint does not kill the pilot. It sizes it.

So where does that leave the deepest objection — that Houston is choosing the hard version? It leaves it standing, and it is the reason the yes is narrow rather than broad. Houston genuinely lacks the stranded capacity that made Pittsburgh easy and the circulation slack that makes Detroit durable. The correct response to that is not to refuse the program. It is to refuse the hard version of it and build the easy one on purpose: not at Bush, where United's banks and the Domestic Terminal Program make every lane-minute contested, but at Hobby, where a single 24-hour terminal, a near-monopoly signatory who captures the concession upside, and a cleaner accountability boundary make the downside smallest. The contrarian, the operator, the CEO, the regulatory analyst, and the public-safety chair converged independently on the same site. That convergence is the finding. Houston does not have to choose the hard version. It has to choose the small one, at the right airport, and refuse to let anyone scale it into a headline.

The thesis holds, then, under a specific and stated condition: that Houston builds the program the concessions demand rather than the program the marketing wants. Sold as ROI, efficiency, or a revenue line, at Bush, layered onto construction, the program fails, and the counter-case correctly predicts its failure. Sold as bounded community goodwill, at Hobby, capped and sheddable and walled off from the rate base, it clears a low bar honestly — and the counter-case, read closely, agrees.

## Implications for Houston Airports: the pitch to the Director of Aviation

Director Szczesniak, here is the decision in the terms of your chair.

**The strongest case for.** This is the cheapest political capital on the menu. For $300,000 to $1.5 million a year in local dollars — a figure your team must confirm with a bottom-up model, because no peer airport publishes theirs — you can hand City Council and the Houston business community something a city-department airport rarely gets to offer: a constituent-facing amenity that photographs well and costs almost nothing at system scale.[^20] Reuniting a military family before a deployment, walking an 80-year-old to her gate, animating the concessions you just spent $1.468 billion rebuilding. Funded from non-airline revenue, it stays outside Majority-in-Interest, outside airline consent, and the concession spend it generates is yours under the hybrid-compensatory structure.[^16][^30] It is genuinely your decision, and it is a deposit in the political account you will want to draw on in 2030.

**The strongest case against.** Three risks, in order. The precedent, not the pilot: once visitors expect access, withdrawal becomes a political event, and you would be creating an entitlement backed by a checkpoint you neither staff nor fund. The wrong side of the ledger: if cost pressure ever pushes this into rates, it enters Majority-in-Interest and becomes a United lever right before the Terminal E lease expires in January 2030.[^17] And attention: with IIJA airport funds expiring September 30, 2026, the passenger facility charge frozen at $4.50 since 2000, and a $2.92 billion capital program defining how your tenure is judged, management attention spent litigating an amenity incident at a council hearing is attention stolen from the balance sheet.[^39]

**The recommendation.** Yes — as a pilot, not a program, and at Hobby, not Bush.

- **Site:** Hobby. One terminal, simpler geometry, a near-monopoly signatory who captures the concession upside, no Bush-scale construction footprint, the cleaner accountability boundary. Prove the model where the downside is smallest.
- **Cap:** 50 a day, one designated checkpoint, with a real-time remaining-count display and an operations-center throttle that pauses issuance automatically when the checkpoint wait crosses a published threshold.
- **Hours:** midday to evening only. Blackout the morning departure bank entirely; blackout every peak date on a calendar published before launch, tied to demand forecasts and the 2026 World Cup.
- **Vetting:** the Philadelphia model. Advance application, one to seven days out, with TSA adjudicating every applicant before arrival. No same-day curbside kiosks — they push identity checks and vetting exceptions onto your terminal staff at the worst moment and place.[^8]
- **Eligibility:** narrow and sympathetic. Military and USO send-offs, credentialed elder- and first-time-traveler assistance, sanctioned events and tours. Capture perhaps 80 percent of the goodwill at a fraction of the volume and risk.
- **Funding:** non-airline sources only. The moment any cost touches the rate base, you have handed United a grievance for free.
- **Governance:** a written throttle protocol living in the operations center, not a policy binder, and an unreviewable pause switch held jointly by the operations center and the public-safety chair. A live registry of who is inside the sterile area on a given pass, integrated into the Airport Emergency Plan before the first pass issues.
- **Do not** launch a program into the active phases of the Domestic Terminal Program at Bush. Pittsburgh already ran that experiment, and the program lost.[^10]

**Estimated cost range:** $0.3 million to $1.5 million a year, plus a modest one-time stand-up for the digital-pass platform. Confirm with a bottom-up model before go/no-go; the public record will not supply the number.[^20]

**First 90 days:** File the Airport Security Program amendment with the Federal Security Director — treat that approval, not vendor selection, as the critical path, and expect six to twelve months.[^32] Brief City Council members before they read a line item as "spending for non-passengers." Draft the throttle protocol and the blackout calendar with the operations center and the public-safety chair. Negotiate the staffing understanding with HPD and HFD before you book a benefit whose cost you would otherwise be externalizing onto departments you do not command.[^38] Define the stop criteria — the wait threshold, the incident threshold, the utilization floor below which the pilot is not worth continuing — and publish them, so that ending the pilot is a pre-agreed term rather than an embarrassment.

**The test.** Not whether visitors enjoy it. Whether, in 2030, it sits in a footnote or across the negotiating table.

---

Somewhere in that four-hour line in March, the man with the $39 ticket he never used got his coffee and watched his airplane. He did nothing wrong; the system simply had no cheaper way to sell him the thing he wanted, which was to stand for twenty minutes near a departure gate and feel the pull of somewhere else. A visitor pass is a way to sell him that feeling honestly, for a few dollars of concessions and a morning of goodwill, on the days the airport can spare the room — and to tell him plainly, on the days it cannot, that the door is closed. The question for Houston is not whether it can afford the velvet rope. It is whether it has the discipline to keep the rope small, to hang it at the right door, and to take it down without apology on the mornings the line already runs to the arrivals hall.
