# Regulatory and Political Analyst Brief
## Non-Aeronautical Data Center Development at IAD — Approach/Departure Surface Constraints

**Prepared for:** MWAA Planning Department Review  
**Date:** July 29, 2026  
**Classification:** Pre-decisional — for internal planning use  

---

## Key Findings

1. **The RPZ prohibition is guidance, not regulation — but MWAA's grant assurances convert it into a binding contractual obligation.** AC 150/5300-13B (March 2022) defines the Runway Protection Zone as a "trapezoidal ground-level area off the runway end" where sponsors "should take appropriate measures to protect against, remove, or mitigate land uses that introduce incompatible development." For Runway 19R at IAD — a Category C/D precision instrument runway with visibility minimums below 3/4-mile — the RPZ extends 2,500 feet beyond the runway end, with inner width 1,000 feet and outer width 1,750 feet. [Source: AC 150/5300-13B; California RPZ Dimension Table cross-referencing federal standards, https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/title-21/division-2.5/table-1] Grant Assurance 20 (Hazard Removal and Mitigation) then makes avoidance of those incompatible uses a contractual federal obligation. A data center sited within or functionally affecting that zone triggers both dimensions of exposure: advisory guidance defines the violation, and the contractual assurance provides the enforcement hook.

2. **Grant Assurance 29 means any structure added to the Airport Layout Plan requires FAA approval under one of three specific criteria — and all three apply to this project.** Section 743 of the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 limits FAA's ALP review authority to projects that (a) materially impact the safe and efficient operation of aircraft, (b) adversely affect the safety of people or property on the ground as a result of aircraft operations, or (c) adversely affect the value of prior federal investments. [Source: FAA October 3, 2024 Preliminary Instructions Memorandum, https://www.faa.gov/airports/planning_capacity/alp-approval-preliminary-instructions; AAAE Regulatory Alert, https://alerts.aaae.org/latest/regulatory_100524] A data center sited within approach/departure surfaces implicates criteria (a) — aircraft operating in the approach corridor at low altitude — and criterion (b) — potential physical harm from an aircraft incident within the RPZ. The FAA cannot be excluded from review on the basis that this is "non-aeronautical development."

3. **Cooling tower thermal plumes are a documented FAA aviation safety hazard, explicitly recognized in the Aeronautical Information Manual and the FAA's September 2015 Technical Guidance Memorandum.** AIM Section 7-6-16 warns that "results of encountering a plume may include airframe damage, aircraft upset, and/or engine damage/failure" and that plume turbulence effects "can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the height of the top of the stack or cooling tower." [Source: AIM 7-6-16, https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html] The FAA's 2015 Technical Guidance Assessment Tool for Thermal Exhaust Plume Impact states explicitly that "thermal exhaust plumes are incompatible with airport operations in the vicinity of airports and may pose unique hazards to aircraft in critical phases of flight, particularly during takeoff, landing and within the pattern." [Source: FAA Technical Guidance Memo, Sept. 24, 2015, https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf] Data centers require cooling towers that produce exactly these plumes. The characterization of this as a speculative risk is not defensible against the FAA's own published guidance.

4. **Any structure within Part 77 notice thresholds requires an FAA aeronautical study, and the FAA has independent authority to issue a Hazard Determination that blocks the project without reference to grant assurances.** Under 14 CFR § 77.9, any construction within the 100:1 horizontal imaginary surface extending 20,000 feet from an airport with instrument approaches — which covers all three MWAA-solicited data center sites near IAD — triggers mandatory FAA notice and aeronautical study via Form 7460-1. [Source: 14 CFR Part 77, https://ecfr.io/Title-14/Part-77] If the FAA issues a Determination of Hazard under § 77.17, no grant assurance argument is needed: the FAA's aeronautical study authority is an independent bar. The timeline for that review is up to 120 days, and a Hazard Determination can be appealed but rarely reversed.

5. **ILS critical area protection at IAD Runway 19R adds a second independent regulatory bar.** FAA Order 6750.16E (Siting Criteria for Instrument Landing Systems, April 2014) establishes localizer and glideslope critical areas that must be protected from "parking and the unlimited movement of surface and air traffic." [Source: FAA Order 6750.16E, https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/FINAL_SIGNED_Order_6750_16E_ILS_Siting_Criteria_06-09-2014_for_Web_posting%5B1%5D.pdf] Runway 19R at IAD is served by an ALSF-2 approach lighting system — the highest-category system — indicating Cat II/III ILS operations. Data center structures, which combine large metallic mass, electromagnetic emissions, and cooling tower thermal disturbances, represent three distinct interference vectors against localizer and glideslope signal integrity. The Order states: "Placing an object outside the critical area does not guarantee non-interference with the ILS signal in space."

6. **The FAA's December 2023 land use policy (effective January 8, 2024) confirms that non-aeronautical uses on federally acquired or conveyed airport land require FAA consent, and non-aeronautical uses do not carry the priority protection of aeronautical uses.** [Source: Federal Register 88 FR 85474, Dec. 8, 2023, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/12/08/2023-27017/policy-regarding-processing-land-use-changes-on-federally-acquired-or-federally-conveyed-airport] IAD was built on federally acquired land. Any change in land use on that footprint — including a long-term ground lease for a data center — triggers the FAA's land-use-change review authority independent of the grant assurance framework. The policy permits non-aeronautical uses where they do not compromise safety and where the airport receives fair market value — but it does not override the approach-surface protections in Grant Assurances 20 and 21.

7. **Grant assurance enforcement is real, not theoretical: violation places MWAA on the Airport Noncompliance List, triggers Part 16 proceedings, and risks denial of all future AIP grants.** The FAA's Part 16 administrative process (14 CFR Part 16) allows any aggrieved party — including airlines, pilots' associations, or FAA acting on its own — to allege grant assurance violations. [Source: 14 CFR Part 16, https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-16] A Director's Determination finding a violation triggers corrective action requirements. The Watsonville Municipal Airport precedent (February 2026, Santa Cruz County Superior Court) illustrates that failure to maintain approach surface integrity can result in injunctive relief blocking future development approvals, not merely prospective compliance. [Source: General Aviation News, March 1, 2026, https://generalaviationnews.com/2026/03/01/court-hands-victory-to-watsonville-pilots-in-airport-land-use-dispute/] For MWAA, whose capital program depends on AIP formula and discretionary grants, the noncompliance exposure is not an acceptable risk.

8. **The 2024 FAA Reauthorization Act's Section 743 streamlined ALP approval for non-aeronautical development on sponsor-owned land — but it explicitly preserved FAA authority for the three criteria that apply here, and MWAA's solicitation sites include FAA-eligible concerns on all three counts.** The new Notice of Intent (NOI) process gives FAA 45 days to assert authority, after which the airport can proceed. But the 45-day window is a procedure, not a waiver: if FAA asserts authority within 45 days on grounds that the project materially impacts aviation safety, the pre-Reauthorization review obligations apply in full. [Source: AirTAP Briefing, December 2024, https://airtap.umn.edu/publications/briefings/2024/december/landuse] A project sited within approach surfaces does not escape review by filing an NOI — it accelerates the clock on an FAA determination that is likely to be adverse.

## Evidence Section

### Regulatory Framework: What Is Rule vs. Guidance vs. Contract

**Rule (binding, 14 CFR):** 14 CFR Part 77, "Safe, Efficient Use, and Preservation of the Navigable Airspace," is federal regulation. Section 77.9 mandates notice for any construction or alteration exceeding the 100:1 imaginary surface within 20,000 feet of an airport with instrument approaches. Non-compliance is a federal violation, not merely a contract breach. [Source: https://ecfr.io/Title-14/Part-77]

**Guidance (advisory, but treated as safe harbor):** AC 150/5300-13B (Airport Design, March 31, 2022) and AC 150/5200-33C (Wildlife Hazard Management) are advisory circulars — not regulations. However, the FAA uses deviations from advisory circular standards as evidence of grant assurance violations. A sponsor who argues "the AC is only guidance" in a Part 16 proceeding is arguing against a 40-year body of administrative precedent. [Source: AC 150/5300-13B, https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.current/documentnumber/150_5300-13; AC 150/5200-33C, https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentID/1037215]

**Contract (binding on AIP recipients, enforced via Part 16):** AIP Grant Assurances are contractual obligations accepted by airport sponsors upon receipt of any AIP grant. They run with the airport for 20 years from the date of the most recent grant. The relevant assurances for this project:

- **Grant Assurance 19 (Operation and Maintenance):** Requires the sponsor to operate and maintain airport facilities "in safe and serviceable condition." A data center creating electromagnetic interference with navigation aids implicates this assurance directly.

- **Grant Assurance 20 (Hazard Removal and Mitigation):** Requires sponsors to "take appropriate action to assure that such terminal airspace as is required to protect instrument and visual operations to the airport will be adequately cleared and protected by removing, lowering, lighting, marking, or otherwise mitigating existing airport hazards." A structure or thermal plume within an approach surface is a hazard within the meaning of this assurance.

- **Grant Assurance 21 (Compatible Land Use):** Requires the sponsor to "take appropriate action to restrict incompatible land uses around the airport." The FAA has interpreted this to require affirmative sponsorship action against incompatible development within RPZs, not merely passive non-approval.

- **Grant Assurance 22 (Economic Nondiscrimination):** Less directly applicable, but relevant if data center tenants receive non-aeronautical land access on terms that discriminate against aeronautical users competing for the same land.

- **Grant Assurance 29 (Airport Layout Plan):** Requires the sponsor to "keep up-to-date an Airport Layout Plan of the airport, approved by the Secretary." No structure may be placed on the airport that is inconsistent with the approved ALP without FAA approval. [Source: FAA Grant Assurances page, https://www.faa.gov/airports/aip/grant_assurances; Federal Register 2025 revision, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/25/2025-07224/airport-improvement-program-aip-grant-assurances]

### RPZ Dimensional Standards for Runway 19R at IAD

Runway 19R is served by Cat II/III ILS (ALSF-2 lighting) and accommodates Category C and D aircraft at approach speeds above 121 knots. The applicable RPZ dimensions under AC 150/5300-13B Table 2-3 for a precision instrument runway with visibility minimums lower than 3/4-mile are: **Length: 2,500 feet. Inner Width: 1,000 feet. Outer Width: 1,750 feet.** [Source: California Title 21 RPZ Dimension Table (reflecting federal FAA dimensional standards), https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/title-21/division-2.5/table-1; KIAD runway data, https://www.flightaware.com/resources/airport/KIAD/IAP/ILS+RWY+19C+%28CAT+II+-+III%29]

Any structure sited within this footprint — or whose physical or electromagnetic or thermal effects penetrate the approach surface above this footprint — is within the RPZ land-use restriction framework.

### Wildlife and Stormwater: AC 150/5200-33C

AC 150/5200-33C (Hazardous Wildlife Attractants on or Near Airports) recommends that new stormwater management facilities within 10,000 feet of runways serving turbine-powered aircraft (which applies to all IAD runways) be "designed and operated so as not to create above-ground standing water." Stormwater detention ponds serving data center cooling systems that create persistent water features within this 10,000-foot boundary are presumptively non-compliant with this guidance. [Source: AC 150/5200-33C, https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentID/1037215]

### Thermal Plume: The FAA's Own Determination

The FAA's September 24, 2015 Technical Guidance Assessment Tool memorandum states: "the FAA has determined that thermal exhaust plumes in the vicinity of airports are incompatible with airport operations and may pose unique hazards to aircraft in critical phases of flight, particularly during takeoff, landing and within the pattern." [Source: FAA Technical Guidance memo, https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf] The Puente Power Project aeronautical study found that thermal plumes "could pose a significant hazard to small and medium-sized aircraft on departure ... while climbing between 1,000 and 3,000 feet." [Source: California Energy Commission Docket 15-AFC-01, https://efiling.energy.ca.gov/GetDocument.aspx?tn=215584] AIM 7-6-16, current through Change 3 (September 5, 2024), warns pilots that plume turbulence "can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the height of the top of the stack or cooling tower" and that "these hazards are most critical during low altitude flight in calm and cold air, especially in and around approach and departure corridors or airport traffic areas." Large-scale data centers routinely deploy cooling towers; the thermal signature is not distinguishable from a power plant in FAA analysis.

### FAA ALP Review Process After Section 743

The FAA's October 3, 2024 Preliminary Instructions Memorandum established interim guidance under Section 743. FAA retains ALP approval authority — and thus the ability to block a project — when the project (a) "materially impacts the safe and efficient operation of aircraft," (b) "adversely affects the safety of people or property on the ground as a result of aircraft operations," or (c) "adversely affects the value of prior Federal investments." [Source: AAAE Regulatory Alert, Oct. 2024, https://alerts.aaae.org/latest/regulatory_100524] All three apply to a data center within approach/departure surfaces at IAD: (a) thermal plumes and EM interference affect aircraft operations; (b) an aircraft incident within the RPZ would affect people on the ground; (c) the ILS infrastructure represents tens of millions in prior federal investment. The new NOI process gives FAA 45 days to assert authority. That is a procedural acceleration, not a substantive liberalization.

## Case Studies

### Case 1: Watsonville Municipal Airport (2026)
Santa Cruz County Superior Court, February 3, 2026. A 21-unit residential development at 547 Airport Boulevard was approved within airport approach safety zones. The court ruled the City of Watsonville violated the California State Aeronautics Act by failing to incorporate mandatory airport safety standards into its general plan. Injunctive relief blocked future development approvals in airport safety zones until the city updated its general plan. The operative principle: approach-surface protection requirements cannot be waived by local land-use decision without state-level compliance — and the same principle applies to federal grant assurance compliance, which cannot be waived by the airport sponsor's own board resolution. [Source: General Aviation News, March 1, 2026, https://generalaviationnews.com/2026/03/01/court-hands-victory-to-watsonville-pilots-in-airport-land-use-dispute/]

### Case 2: Puente Power Project, California (2015-2016)
California Energy Commission Docket 15-AFC-01. FAA aeronautical study of a proposed power plant near Long Beach Airport determined that cooling tower thermal plumes would "pose a significant hazard to small and medium-sized aircraft on departure from Runways 27 and 21 as they make departure turns very near or over the alternative site while climbing between 1,000 and 3,000 feet." The project required siting modifications to address the plume hazard before the CEC could approve it. The FAA's analysis applied the September 2015 Technical Guidance Assessment Tool. This is the closest documented precedent for a data center cooling system within an approach corridor: same physics, same regulatory framework, same outcome exposure. [Source: CEC Docket 15-AFC-01, https://efiling.energy.ca.gov/GetDocument.aspx?tn=215584]

### Case 3: MWAA Data Center Solicitation (Current)
MWAA has published a solicitation for data center development on three parcels at IAD: Moran Road (~14 acres, 80-160 feet height, FAA-dependent); Route 267 (~21 acres, 140-200 feet height, FAA-dependent); and Pacific Boulevard (~19 acres, 120-140 feet height, FAA-dependent). [Source: MWAA Real Estate, https://www.mwaa.com/business/real-estate-department/iad-data-center-development-opportunity-0] The solicitation itself acknowledges FAA-dependent height limitations, confirming that Part 77 review is already anticipated. The planning memo under review concerns a proposed site within approach/departure surfaces — which is distinct from these three solicitation parcels, all of which appear sited north of the airport rather than within primary approach corridors. The distinction between an approach-corridor site and a perimeter site is the operative regulatory fact: perimeter parcels can clear Part 77 review; approach-corridor sites face a presumptive Hazard Determination.

### Case 4: ILS Critical Area — No Categorical Waiver
FAA Order 6750.16E establishes that large metallic structures and electromagnetic sources "within plus/minus 15 degrees of extended centerline up to 1 NM from the approach end of the runway" require detailed ILS signal analysis, and that "placing an object outside the critical area does not guarantee non-interference with the ILS signal in space." [Source: FAA Order 6750.16E, https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/FINAL_SIGNED_Order_6750_16E_ILS_Siting_Criteria_06-09-2014_for_Web_posting%5B1%5D.pdf] For Runway 19R, which is a Cat II/III ILS approach, interference with the glideslope or localizer could trigger operational downgrades affecting airline schedules and raising liability exposure that no data center revenue stream can offset. The FAA's Technical Operations branch, not the Airports branch, owns ILS certification — and they are not party to any commercial negotiation.

## Analyst Assessment: What Can and Cannot Be Moved

**Cannot be moved without multi-year federal engagement:**

- The Part 77 aeronautical study requirement (14 CFR, not guidance). Filing Form 7460-1 is mandatory; a Hazard Determination is an independent regulatory bar that no airport board resolution, no lease agreement, and no state approval can override.
- The ILS critical area analysis (FAA Order 6750.16E and FAA Technical Operations authority). The FAA's Technical Operations branch has independent authority over NAVAID certification; they are not an Airports branch stakeholder and do not respond to grant-assurance negotiation.
- The AIM plume guidance and the 2015 FAA Technical Guidance memorandum, both of which would be cited by any plaintiff challenging the development or by FAA in a Hazard Determination proceeding.

**Can be managed, but only with sustained investment of time:**

- The Grant Assurance framework is contractual, not statutory. An airport sponsor can request a modification or consent agreement from FAA, or seek a formal deviation. This takes 12-36 months minimum and requires FAA Washington headquarters concurrence.
- The ALP update process under the new NOI framework (45-day response window) gives sponsors a path to initiate action. But that 45-day window is a procedural trigger, not a substantive bar: FAA can and will assert authority within 45 days if the project materially affects approach surfaces.
- Siting a data center on perimeter parcels (north of IAD, outside approach surfaces) does not eliminate Part 77 exposure but substantially reduces the likelihood of a Hazard Determination. MWAA's published solicitation for Moran Road, Route 267, and Pacific Boulevard parcels suggests the planning function has already identified the approach-corridor constraint and sited commercial opportunities accordingly.

**The Strategist's Decision Anchor:** The relevant decision calendar item is not a board vote or a lease execution. It is the Form 7460-1 filing and the FAA's subsequent aeronautical study. Every commitment made before that study returns — to a developer, to a board, to the public — is made against an undefined regulatory outcome. MWAA's bond covenants and use-and-lease agreements cannot compensate for a Hazard Determination that grounds Cat II/III operations on Runway 19R. The planning memo's recommendation against the project is not conservative risk management; it is the correct reading of a regulatory stack that has multiple independent veto points, none of which are within MWAA's control.
