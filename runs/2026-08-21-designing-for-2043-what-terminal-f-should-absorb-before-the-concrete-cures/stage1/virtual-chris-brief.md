# Virtual Chris — Independent Brief

**Run:** Designing for 2043: What Terminal F Should Absorb Before the Concrete Cures
**Agent role:** the connections the other agents will miss — stakeholder lattice, adjacencies, pattern matches, opportunity register, collaboration paths.
**Written independently.** No other Stage 1 briefs read.

## Where I am starting

Terminal F is not one project. It is four projects sharing a slab: a capital delivery project for the DFW Board; a premium-repositioning platform for American while Robert Isom tries to close roughly a $3B profit gap to United and $5B to Delta through cabins, lounges, and widebodies; a civic asset for two city councils that re-litigate ownership balance every budget cycle; and a federal-partner integration where CBP, TSA, and FAA all have knock-on decisions inside the building.

The other agents on this council will each take one of those projects as their frame. My job is to point at the seams between them, because the seams are where the cheap options live. The thesis I am working under — that the terminal must absorb American's premium shift, not be retrofitted against it — is the right thesis. What I want to add is that the same design moves that absorb premium also open doors to other people's projects DFW is not currently spending against. Two initiatives run together often cost less than either run alone.

## 1. The lattice — eight connections this thesis activates

1. **DFW Skylink expansion ↔ AA's 37,000 sq ft Terminal C Admirals Club ↔ Terminal F premium spine.** American just announced its largest lounge ever near the Terminal C Skylink station. The new Terminal F build includes a *centralized* Skylink station. Read together, these are not two lounges and a train — they are the beginning of a premium spine that uses the Skylink as its distribution layer. Terminal F does not need to duplicate the Terminal C club; it needs to be positioned as a *node* on that spine.
2. **CBP FIS reimbursement resolution ↔ AA 787-9P transatlantic deployment ↔ Terminal F widebody gate mix.** Dallas City Council resolution 25-1461 quietly approved a CBP reimbursement agreement for FIS equipment. That is the earliest procedural signal that Terminal F's international throughput is a live design conversation. Every 787-9P American assigns to a DFW–LHR/CDG/FRA rotation instead of PHL or ORD raises the FIS load, and the reversibility window on FIS lanes is short.
3. **AA Fort Worth HQ ↔ DFW Board Fort Worth seats (4 of 11) ↔ Terminal F concessions and vendor narrative.** American is a Fort Worth employer; Fort Worth holds four voting seats on a Dallas-majority board. If Terminal F reads as a Dallas-County win with Fort Worth's carrier paying for it, the 2043 U&L renewal narrative rots in ways that do not show up until the political weather changes. A deliberately Fort Worth-facing concessions and DBE program is cheap insurance that most of the council will not think to buy.
4. **Modular offsite manufacturing ↔ FAA Reauthorization discretionary grants ↔ national exemplar status.** DFW is now on its third modular terminal project and just executed a record-setting module move. The FAA Reauthorization stack is quietly favorable to airports that can demonstrate schedule and cost innovation. There is a soft-power play here: DFW as the national reference case for modular terminals gets DFW on FAA Administrator's speed-dial, and that is worth something on discretionary awards that have nothing to do with Terminal F.
5. **TSA Screening at Speed (CT/AIT) ↔ Flagship Check-In at D30 ↔ Terminal F checkpoint footprint.** TSA's rollout of CT and next-gen AIT compresses checkpoint depth. The classic mistake is over-provisioning checkpoint depth in Terminal F because "we always underbuild." The subtler move is instrumenting the D30 Flagship Check-In line as a data-generating pilot for TSA in 2026–27, then citing that data to negotiate a more compact — and more premium — checkpoint geometry in Terminal F.
6. **eVTOL vertiport pilots ↔ AA premium ground-access gap ↔ Terminal F roof structure.** United (Archer) and Delta (Joby) have vertiport partnerships. American does not, and its Fort Worth home base is actually a favorable eVTOL geography. A load-bearing roof structure and MEP stub on Terminal F headhouse is a *seven-figure* addition today; the vertiport itself is somebody else's capital in 2032.
7. **AA Cargo widebody bellies ↔ Terminal F ramp choreography ↔ DFW cargo revenue line.** Premium long-haul routes carry premium belly cargo. Ramp geometry that lets a 787-9P work a passenger turn and a cargo turn without repositioning is a quiet operating margin that will never appear in the customer-experience deck. AA Cargo is a stakeholder no one in this room is inviting to the meeting.
8. **DBE hospitality operators ↔ concessions RFP timing ↔ AA loyalty narrative.** American's "Lead in Loyalty" pillar cares about story. Terminal F concessions can be structured as a Fort Worth–Dallas hospitality showcase that AAdvantage marketing then amplifies at zero incremental cost to DFW. That is a marketing budget the airport does not have to spend because the tenant will spend it for you.

## 2. Three pattern matches — one precise analogy each

### a. Kansas City International's horseshoe terminals, 1972

MCI opened three horseshoe terminals engineered for a specific era's origin-and-destination geometry — 90-second walks, gate-to-curb elegance, minimal connecting infrastructure. The design was optimized for a hub geometry that did not survive the decade. Braniff collapsed in 1982; TWA left; the terminals became structurally hostile to connecting traffic and ran as a five-decade operational drag until the single new terminal opened in 2023.

**Mapping.** Terminal F is being scoped against today's connecting bank, when about two-thirds of American's DFW customers connect. If American's premium shift succeeds, that number moves. Long-haul premium point-to-point erodes the connecting share; Flagship Suites are less about the O&D who has always flown business and more about the corporate traveler who now can. The reversibility map the run prompt asks for is the insurance policy MCI never bought. The lesson is not "don't build for today"; it is "don't *only* build for today."

### b. CERN's LEP-to-LHC tunnel, 1983–2008

CERN dug a 27-kilometer tunnel in the 1980s for the Large Electron-Positron collider. LEP ran until 2000. Then the same tunnel — the same envelope, the same shafts — was used for a fundamentally different machine, the LHC, which found the Higgs. The tunnel was sized in 1983 for physics not yet designed. That single decision made 25 years of subsequent science possible.

**Mapping.** The Terminal F envelope — floor-plate depth, ceiling heights, structural loads on the headhouse roof, MEP capacity headroom, vertical circulation stubs, knock-out wall locations — is the LEP tunnel. The Flagship Lounge, the Flagship Arrivals suite, the vertiport, the biometric gantries, the second FIS bank, the automated bag drop that is not yet invented — those are the LHC. Nobody is asking DFW to build the future machine now. They are asking DFW to size the envelope so a future tenant can thread it through.

### c. Grand Central Terminal's air rights, 1903–1913

William Wilgus and the Reed & Stem design team, working for the New York Central under the Vanderbilts, did something clever. They electrified the approach — which meant the platforms could go underground — which meant the blocks above them became buildable air rights. Terminal City generated ground rents that financed the New York Central for a generation, and the air rights are still generating value in 2026. The valuable square footage was the square footage they *deliberately did not use*.

**Mapping.** The most valuable square footage in Terminal F may be the footprint you *do not build against* now. The Provisions by Admirals Club footprint is exactly this kind of asset — a small grab-and-go space today, a Flagship Lounge in 2032 if you engineer the slab load, the electrical spine, and the egress paths now. Buying the option is cheap. Foreclosing it is expensive. Every reversible-until-cured decision inside Terminal F is a piece of air rights the airport can sell — to American, to concessions, to the federal partners — over the next twenty years.

## 3. Opportunity register — ranked by 24-month actionability

1. **Convert the Provisions envelope in Terminal F to a "Flagship-ready shell."** Structural slab load, MEP capacity, egress paths, and grease/exhaust risers sized for a full Flagship Lounge conversion. First step, this quarter: joint memo from DFW's Chief Development Officer and AA's SVP DFW Hub Operations directing the design team to preserve the option in the current module cycle. Marginal cost is low; foreclosure cost after pour is order-of-magnitude worse.
2. **Instrument the D30 Flagship Check-In as a TSA data-generating pilot.** First step, within 60 days of opening: MOU with TSA Innovation Task Force so throughput data can be published in a form that supports checkpoint-compression arguments for Terminal F.
3. **Reserve knock-out CBP FIS lanes in Terminal F.** First step: amend the current CBP reimbursement scope (Dallas Resolution 25-1461) to name knock-out lane locations explicitly, so the concrete pour does not extinguish the option.
4. **Vertiport-ready roof structure on Terminal F headhouse.** First step, this design cycle: commission a rooftop load and geometry study from the modular manufacturer. Result goes into the record whether or not eVTOL certification ever matures.
5. **"Skylink Premium" service pattern.** Designate a Skylink car pattern that supports timed transfers between the T-C Admirals Club and T-F premium gates in under 10 minutes. First step: joint operations analysis with AA to determine required headway and station geometry adjustments while the Terminal F Skylink station is still on paper.
6. **Fort Worth-facing concessions RFP structure.** First step: pre-market outreach to Fort Worth-based DBE hospitality operators through the Fort Worth Chamber. Political optics matter more than the revenue math here.
7. **Flagship Arrivals suite in Terminal F, not just Departures.** First step: design the international arrivals corridor with a convertible check-in hall dimension so a Flagship Arrivals lounge can be inserted between FIS clearance and Skylink.
8. **AA Cargo ramp geometry review.** First step: invite AA Cargo to a single design review meeting. They will not have been invited otherwise, and their input is nearly free.

## 4. Collaboration paths for the hardest oppositions

### a. American Airlines' commercial leadership — the "Provisions is enough" position

American just announced its largest-ever Admirals Club in Terminal C and a Flagship Check-In experience in Terminal D. The signal to DFW is that American's premium spending for the near term is anchored to C and D, and Terminal F gets a grab-and-go. AA will resist any framing that requires additional capex on a Flagship-tier lounge in Terminal F while the Terminal C club is still under construction.

**Path from opposed to aligned.** Do not ask American to build a second Flagship Lounge. Ask American to preserve the *option* to build one for the cost of an over-specified shell. Reframe Terminal F Flagship as an **Arrivals** product, not a Departures product — the inbound transatlantic 787-9P premium passenger deplanes into a Flagship Arrivals suite in Terminal F, then either Skylinks to the Terminal C club to connect or to the Terminal D Flagship Lounge to continue premium. That reframe removes the competitive conflict with the new Terminal C club and slots Terminal F into a portfolio role American does not currently have covered anywhere in its system.

**Convener.** DFW CEO Chris McLaughlin, one-on-one with American's SVP DFW Hub Operations, before the joint premium-experience alignment workshop. First formal meeting: a two-hour design session that ends with a signed one-page option memo, not a strategy deck.

### b. Fort Worth city council — the "Dallas got the terminal" perception risk

Fort Worth holds four of eleven voting seats on the Board and has to approve the annual budget and bond sales. The narrative risk is not immediate; it materializes in the run-up to 2043 U&L renewal, when a council member who feels Terminal F was designed as a Dallas project will find the leverage to say so.

**Path from opposed to aligned.** Make the Fort Worth stake physical in the building. Intentional Fort Worth-based DBE concessions. A visible design gesture — Trinity River motif in wayfinding, Fort Worth artist commissions in the central hall, a Stockyards-anchored food and beverage program. Recognize AA's Fort Worth headquarters explicitly in the terminal's public narrative. Make sure the ribbon-cutting has as many Fort Worth speakers as Dallas ones. None of this is expensive; all of it is felt.

**Convener.** DFW Government Affairs, with the Fort Worth Chamber. First meeting: a "Terminal F Fort Worth advisory circle" convened before the concessions RFP drops.

### c. TSA and CBP — the "always want more depth" federal instinct

Federal partners default to asking for more square footage than the airport wants to give. That default is not adversarial; it is rational risk allocation, and it produces terminals with too much checkpoint and too much FIS.

**Path from opposed to aligned.** Instrument first, size second. Deploy CT and next-gen AIT at existing DFW checkpoints — including the D30 Flagship Check-In line — and publish throughput data for 2026–27. Use that data to negotiate a Terminal F checkpoint sized to observed throughput, not to conservative default assumptions. The same argument works for FIS: pilot APC and MPC (Mobile Passport Control) at Terminal D, publish arrival-processing data, then use that data to argue for a Terminal F FIS that is *narrower and deeper-instrumented*, not just larger.

**Convener.** DFW SVP Operations, quarterly, with TSA HQ Innovation Task Force and CBP Field Operations, Dallas Field Office. First meeting: a joint six-month throughput pilot charter, signed before Terminal F checkpoint drawings are finalized.

## 5. Pull-ready observations

1. Terminal F is being designed against a connecting bank while American is being repositioned around a premium cabin. If those two design intents drift more than five degrees, the building will spend twenty years being reshaped by tenants.
2. The most valuable square footage in Terminal F may be the square footage you never build now — the Provisions envelope with a Flagship electrical spine and slab load, priced today and unlocked in 2032.
3. The Skylink is quietly becoming American's premium spine at DFW. Any Terminal F premium strategy that treats the terminal as an island is misreading the network the airline is already building.
4. In modular construction, reversibility is not measured in years. It is measured in the days between when a module lands and when the MEP is closed. That window is the design cycle now.
5. American's Fort Worth headquarters gave DFW a decades-long civic argument. Terminal F is the last chance to turn that argument into a Fort Worth-visible building before the 2043 renewal.
