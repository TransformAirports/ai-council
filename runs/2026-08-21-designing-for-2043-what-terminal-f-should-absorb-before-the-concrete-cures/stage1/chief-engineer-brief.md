# Chief Engineer Brief — Terminal F: What the Concrete Locks In

**Run:** designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures
**Author lens:** 25 years of airside and landside program delivery. Written to help the Council separate "cheap to preserve" from "structurally foreclosed," and to keep the reader honest about what modular really changes and what it doesn't. I hold concrete and software to the same standard: neither is as easy as its advocates say.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **Modular is not a permission slip; it is a tolerance regime.** DFW's six Terminal F mega-modules — the largest 278 ft × 136 ft at 3,320 tons — were assembled offsite and set on a taxiway during a 12-day overnight window. The method compresses on-site duration but tightens the interface tolerances at MEP, structural, and baggage-belt hand-offs; airport-context modular placement has been documented at roughly ¾-inch survey tolerance. Constructability risk migrates from field labor to factory QA and to the joints between modules. [Source: https://www.walshgroup.com/media/dfwterminalfmodularconstructionprecisionandpartnership.html] [Source: https://www.arataumodular.com/app/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Managing-Risk-In-Modular-Construction-Using-Dimensional-And-Geometric-Tolerance-Strategies.pdf]

2. **The genuinely irreversible decisions are gate mix, FIS geometry, riser locations, and BHS spine capacity.** Once a module is set and stitched into utilities, converting a Code C stand to a MARS pair, or widening a sterile corridor to CBP's queue standard, is not a punch-list item. CBP's Airport Technical Design Standard uses a benchmark of about 100 pax/hr per double primary booth and a minimum queue depth of 50–75 feet. Miss the geometry and you buy it back with CBP staffing overtime and connect-time penalties for 30 years. [Source: https://www.sskies.org/images/uploads/subpage/PARAS_0052.PlanningConsiderationsInternationalArrivalsFacilities_.FinalReport_.pdf]

3. **Widebody flexibility is the option worth paying for now — the marginal cost is small if it is designed in before the apron pour.** FAA AC 150/5300-13B governs apron and taxilane geometry for ADG V and VI aircraft. MARS stands (one Code E widebody or two Code C narrow-bodies) require deeper apron, dual PLBs, dual 400 Hz GPU/PCA, and higher-capacity hydrant fuel — but only if the underground and structural provisions are in place. Retrofitting a Code C stand into a MARS stand later requires re-pouring apron, moving hydrants, and reworking blast-fence lines. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC-150-5300-13B-Airport-Design-Chg1-w-errata.pdf] [Source: https://www.tylin.com/insights/destination-mars]

4. **The 30-year lifecycle bill is dominated by systems that wear on 15- and 25-year cycles, not by the shell.** ACRP Research Report 252 uses a 15-year replacement cycle for baggage handling machinery and roughly 10% of purchase cost as annual EDS maintenance. Terminal HVAC, chillers, and BMS controls typically enter major-repair or replacement territory at 15–20 years. Elevators, escalators, moving walks, PLB drives, and 400 Hz converters are mid-life renewals inside a 25-year window. Sponsors who capitalize the ribbon-cutting and underfund O&M generate a deferred-maintenance tail that is visible on FAA inspection findings within a decade. [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/27050/airport-baggage-handling-system-decision-making-based-on-total-cost-of-ownership]

5. **Design standards drive cost in ways financial models regularly ignore.** FAA 150-series ACs (AC 150/5300-13B airport design, AC 150/5360-13A terminal planning, AC 150/5320-17A pavement rating, AC 150/5380-7B pavement management), plus CBP's Airport Technical Design Standard (2021) and TSA checkpoint standards, set the box inside which any "flexible" feature has to fit. A Flagship arrival flow that shortcuts sterile-corridor rules gets redone at cost. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC-150-5360-13A-Airport-Terminal-Planning.pdf] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/150-5380-7b.pdf]

6. **Every well-known airport megaproject failure has a governance signature, not a technology signature.** Denver's Great Hall P3 was terminated 14 months in after contractor projections of ~$311 M overrun on a $650 M base and up to three years of delay; DIA later paid $184 M to unwind. Berlin Brandenburg opened nine years late on fire-protection and baggage-systems commissioning failures. Heathrow T5 was delivered on time and on budget under the T5 Agreement — BAA carried risk, first-tier suppliers were integrated, systems-integration authority was single-owner — but its opening-day operations melted down because operational readiness was undersized. Modular does not remove any of these risks; it changes where they present. [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2019/08/13/denver-international-airport-will-terminate-the-great-hall-renovation-contract/] [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport] [Source: https://infrastructuredeliverymodels.gihub.org/case-studies/heathrow-terminal-5/]

7. **DFW cannot phase Terminal F the way Delta phased LGA Terminal C.** Delta's team openly used the 2020–21 COVID trough to re-sequence concourse and structural steel work and finished LGA Terminal C nearly two years early. DFW ran 85.7 M passengers in 2025, with July at 8.19 M — there is no analogous slack window. Every phasing move at Terminal F is being made against a hub operating at record load, with Skylink and airside movement to preserve, and with American as the single occupant whose consent is required under the U&L for scope changes. [Source: https://www.enr.com/articles/62022-delta-air-lines-inc-and-lga-airfield-reconfiguration-team-has-early-finish-on-28b-airport-project] [Source: https://info.burnsmcd.com/case-study/first-class-arrival-new-delta-air-lines-terminal]

8. **"24–36 months" for anything nontrivial added late in construction is optimism bias.** Flyvbjerg's dataset of 16,000+ projects shows nine of ten megaprojects overrun; airport terminals have posted real-cost overruns of 50–200% (Denver's original 1990s build: 200% on record). The credible late-add at Terminal F is a soft-fit item that lives inside preserved shell capacity — not a new sterile corridor, not a new FIS wing, not a new baggage make-up device. [Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.0003]

## 2. Evidence and analysis

### 2.1 What modular actually changes — and what it doesn't

The design-build JV for the Terminal F concourse and Skylink Station is Innovation Next+ (Archer Western, Turner, Phillips May, H.J. Russell). Mega-modules were fabricated on airport property, moved by self-propelled modular transporter along a taxiway over roughly a two-week overnight window, and set into place. Six modules were placed; the largest was 278 ft × 136 ft at 3,320 tons — described as the largest modules ever moved for a terminal expansion. [Source: https://www.walshgroup.com/media/dfwterminalfmodularconstructionprecisionandpartnership.html]

DFW and its partners cite roughly 30% acceleration versus conventional stick-built. That is credible for on-site duration and for reduction of airside impact windows. What it is *not* is a reduction in the design-lock horizon. Modular pushes the design-freeze earlier: once a module is being cut, welded, and MEP-fit in the factory, the design has to be frozen well ahead of a stick-built equivalent. That inverts the phrase "before the concrete cures." The relevant deadline is closer to "before the module is skinned," and for the first six modules it has already passed. In Stage 3, the Council should ask DFW for the design-lock milestone *per module*, not a generic Terminal F milestone.

The second underappreciated risk is at the joint. Managing tolerance stack-up between two large modules fabricated on separate shifts, transported, and placed to ¾-inch survey is genuinely difficult. When it goes wrong it does not present as a headline; it presents as leaking curtain wall at the module seam, misaligned baggage take-away belts, or life-safety commissioning findings — the exact failure modes that put Berlin Brandenburg nine years late. [Source: https://www.arataumodular.com/app/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Managing-Risk-In-Modular-Construction-Using-Dimensional-And-Geometric-Tolerance-Strategies.pdf]

### 2.2 The reversibility map

Grouped by how expensive the change becomes after the module is set. This is engineering judgment informed by the standards below; the Council should press DFW for the specific per-module freeze dates.

**Category A — Structurally irreversible once modules are set and utilities tied in.** Column grid and floor-to-floor heights; sterile-corridor geometry into the FIS; primary baggage tunnel alignment and depth; apron column-and-jetway grid that determines whether a stand is Code C, Code E, or MARS; hydrant fuel loop capacity and hydrant pit locations; primary electrical service and standby generator sizing; 400 Hz and PCA capacity at the stand.

**Category B — Reversible only at high schedule and airline-consent cost.** Lounge shell subdivisions inside preserved floor plate; Flagship arrival vestibule and dedicated elevator core; CBP FIS booth counts within a preserved hall; TSA checkpoint lane count within preserved queue geometry; BHS in-line EDS matrix within a preserved conveyor spine.

**Category C — Cheap to defer, cheap to change later.** Interior finishes and wayfinding; retail and concession fit-out; furniture and lounge zoning; low-voltage overlays (biometrics, digital signage, sensor networks) *if* the conduit, riser space, and IDF rooms were sized for growth in Category A.

The strongest, cheapest engineering move a sponsor can make now is to buy Category-C flexibility by paying Category-A prices — oversize the risers, oversize the IDF closets, oversize the BHS spine, preserve MARS provisions at a defined minority of stands. That is what "flexible" actually means when it is priced honestly.

### 2.3 The standards that drive the geometry

Four regulatory stacks control what Terminal F can and cannot do. FAA AC 150/5300-13B (Airport Design, 2022, Change 1) governs runway, taxiway, apron, and taxilane geometry — Terminal F apron widths, taxilane object-free areas, and jet-blast setbacks are set here. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC-150-5300-13B-Airport-Design-Chg1-w-errata.pdf] FAA AC 150/5360-13A (Airport Terminal Planning, 2018) is the terminal planning framework every designer references for level-of-service. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC-150-5360-13A-Airport-Terminal-Planning.pdf] AC 150/5380-7B and AC 150/5320-17A define the Pavement Condition Index regime that will grade the Terminal F apron for 30 years and drive grant-eligibility on rehab. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/150-5380-7b.pdf] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5320-17a.pdf] The CBP Airport Technical Design Standard (2021) governs FIS booth counts, queue depth, sterile-corridor design, and hard-wall separation — its ~100 pax/hr per double booth and 50–75 ft primary queue are the numbers Terminal F fits or buys back. TSA checkpoint standards govern CT/AIT deployment, lane length, and secondary screening; any Flagship-priority routing has to be TSA-concurred, which is not automatic. Any novel "Flagship arrival" concept is permitted or foreclosed by these standards. That work is engineering, not marketing.

### 2.4 What premium repositioning implies physically

American has been explicit: 20% premium seat expansion by 2026, roughly 50% growth in widebody premium capacity by end of decade, the 787-9 as the fleet centerpiece, and a shopped RFP with both Airbus and Boeing for the next widebody order. It has committed a 37,000 sf Admirals Club in Terminal C (its largest anywhere, larger than the ~25,000 sf DFW Flagship Lounge in Terminal D) and a Flagship Check-In in Terminal D. Its announced Terminal F lounge posture is a Provisions by Admirals Club grab-and-go. [Source: https://www.dallasnews.com/business/airlines/article/american-airlines-new-admirals-club-dfw-airport-22351656.php] [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/american-airlines-confirms-its-shopping-for-new-widebody-jets/]

The mismatch is the strategic tell. American is buying premium seats and premium lounge everywhere at DFW *except* the terminal being built for the 2043 horizon. The engineering read: Terminal F's lounge program is scoped against 2025 assumptions and will very likely have to be re-scoped inside 15 years. If the shell is not sized for a Flagship-tier lounge on a preserved mezzanine or head-house floor plate — in the 25,000–40,000 sf range with vertical circulation and MEP capacity to serve it — then that retrofit becomes a Category-B or A intervention, not a fit-out.

### 2.5 Lifecycle cost — the second bill

Sponsor boards routinely capitalize a $4 B building and quietly underfund the 30-year O&M profile. Anchors for the Council:

- **Baggage handling.** ACRP 252 sets a 15-year machinery replacement cycle and roughly 10% of purchase cost as annual EDS maintenance. On a Terminal F BHS whose install cost is credibly in the $150–300 M range, that is a $15–30 M annual O&M line and a full replacement wave in the mid-2040s — inside the U&L horizon. [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/27050/airport-baggage-handling-system-decision-making-based-on-total-cost-of-ownership]
- **HVAC, chillers, BMS.** Common guidance treats 15–20 years as the horizon for major rehabilitation and flags the point where annual maintenance exceeds 30–40% of replacement value as a hard trigger.
- **Apron pavement.** Under AC 150/5380-7B, PCI-driven capital timing typically brings a first meaningful apron rehab into the 10–15 year window; widebody upgauge accelerates that clock. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/150-5380-7b.pdf]
- **PLBs, 400 Hz, elevators, walks.** Typically 20–25 year renewal cycles, most requiring a mid-life rebuild.

If DFW capitalizes Terminal F at $4 B and underfunds the second bill by even 15%, the deferred-maintenance tail is a nine-figure liability inside 20 years. That number belongs in the Stage 2 synthesis, not in a downstream capital cycle to discover.

### 2.6 Megaproject pattern-matching

Denver's Great Hall audit laid heavy weight on owner-issued changes and inadequate airport oversight of the delivery partner — the same failure pattern that will present at Terminal F to the extent that owner-driven changes accumulate as American's premium program evolves during construction. [Source: https://denverite.com/2023/04/21/dia-great-hall-audit/] Berlin Brandenburg's fire-protection and baggage-commissioning failures are the modular-terminal failure geometry: the risk lives at the seam between systems, not inside any one of them. And the Heathrow T5 counter-lesson is decisive: the building can be delivered on time and on budget and the opening can still fail if operational readiness is undersized. Terminal F sits in a category where roughly 90% of prior projects have overrun. That is the base rate; the Council should not assume Terminal F beats it without a specific governance reason.

### 2.7 Interdependencies the master plan understates

Terminal F does not sit alone. Concurrent DFW moves — Terminal C and Terminal A renewals adding 14 gates, also modular — compete for the same craft labor, the same fabrication yards, the same crane and SPMT windows, and the same airside impact windows. Airline moves cascade: Terminal F opening pulls American widebody flying from Terminal D, changing gate assignments across the campus and fueling loads at specific hydrant loops. Baggage sortation shifts with the Skylink walking-connection routing; curbside loading shifts with the expanded parking garage. Every one of these has an FAA, TSA, or CBP concurrence path. Programs like this fail not from any single decision but from the aggregate cost of running four to six concurrent major projects at one operating hub. That aggregate cost belongs in the model.

## 4. Evidence gaps I did not close

- The specific widebody vs. narrow-body gate mix inside Terminal F, and how many stands are being provisioned as MARS. Not publicly disclosed.
- The per-module design-lock date. Central to the reversibility map; DFW should be asked directly.
- Terminal F BHS spine capacity (bags/hr), FIS booth count, and sterile-corridor width as designed. All are Category-A decisions the Council needs to see before recommending any premium repositioning fit.
- The offsite fabricator / systems integrator for the mega-modules. The Innovation Next+ JV is public; a specific modular manufacturer was not identified in the sources reviewed.
- Whether the U&L obligates American to upgrade the Terminal F lounge to a Flagship-tier product at any point before 2043.

I have not manufactured evidence to fill these gaps. Any Terminal F recommendation that assumes any of them should be read with skepticism.

---

## 5. Bottom line

Modular is a compression of on-site duration and a redistribution of risk — not a suspension of the physics of retrofit. The concrete-cure metaphor is misleading; the real deadline is factory design-lock, and for the first modules it has already passed. What is still cheap to preserve, and worth buying, is: MARS-capable stand provisions at a defined minority of gates; oversized BHS spine and utility risers; sterile-corridor and FIS geometry sized for CBP throughput at a 2043 widebody peak; preserved mezzanine or head-house floor plate for a Flagship-tier lounge American has not yet asked for but plausibly will; and IDF/riser capacity that lets low-voltage systems be added or replaced without touching the shell. What is not credible is a claim that a nontrivial addition can be executed in the 12–18 months after opening at low cost against a hub running at record volume. My job on this file is to make sure the Council does not confuse a 30% site-duration compression with 30% flexibility in the design.
