# Airport CEO Brief — Terminal F, 2043 Horizon

**Run:** designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures
**Author lens:** Chief executive, top-tier US hub airport — 20 years in airport leadership, 8 in this chair. I have carried a capital program from feasibility through opening, renegotiated a use-and-lease, and sat in more rating-agency conference rooms than design reviews. The recommendations below are framed in the currency the CEO's chair actually spends: bond covenants, board votes, and 30-year amortization schedules.
**Distinctness note:** I have not read the other Stage 1 briefs. This is my independent read on the thesis.

---

## 1. Key findings the board chair can absorb in one read

1. **The 2043 U&L extension is the ceiling on this asset, not the floor.** DFW and American extended the use-and-lease from 2033 to 2043 as part of the May 2025 Terminal F announcement. That extension was bought — American got scope, gate count (from 15 to 31), and $4 billion of committed capital. What DFW got in return is 18 years of scheduling certainty and the political cover to issue debt against it. Every physical option not purchased in the wet-concrete window is a scope amendment that American will negotiate against DFW during the lease, not in the seat of a lease renewal. The CEO's job is to buy those options now, while the leverage is still symmetrical.
2. **The credit story is single-carrier concentration mitigated by a lease that outruns the debt.** DFW carried American at 82.6% of enplanements in 2025. KBRA affirmed AA / Stable in August 2025 on the 2025A-1/A-2/B series; the prior stack sits at Moody's A1 (positive), S&P AA-, Fitch A+. Terminal F sensibly amortizes on the strength of the 2043 lease term, not on the standalone 82.6% concentration. If the fit-out drives premium CPE meaningfully above peer hubs *and* American's premium repositioning underperforms, the same concentration that today reads as an anchor reads to Moody's as fragility.
3. **The modular method converts an interior-fit-out decision into a real option — but only if we write the option into the module contracts now.** DFW is building gates offsite. That construction method's board-level significance is not the 30% cost delta; it is that structural, MEP, and skin decisions freeze at module fabrication, while interiors can defer to module install or later. The CEO recommendation is to instruct the program to publish a written *reversibility register* — module by module — that names the last responsible moment for every premium-driving decision (lounge shell dimensions, upper-level floor plates over gate hardstands, riser capacity for a Flagship-tier kitchen, biometric-lane conduit). No reversibility register, no board sign-off on the next module release.
4. **American's "Provisions by Admirals Club" designation in Terminal F is not a decision. It is a placeholder — and we should treat it that way.** American is opening a Flagship Check-In at Terminal D near D30 later in 2026 and a ~37,000-sq-ft Admirals Club at Terminal C — its largest to date. The premium repositioning is real and it is happening at DFW. The choice to put a "Provisions by Admirals Club" grab-and-go at Terminal F today reflects American's near-term capital allocation, not a 2043 strategic decision. Design the Terminal F lounge shell for Flagship-tier conversion (floor-to-floor height, ceiling loads, MEP shafts, exterior curtainwall breaks) and hand American the ability to make that decision in 2032 rather than the ability to demand a $200M mid-lease retrofit in 2035.
5. **The reversibility map is the single most useful board deliverable of this run.** Everything else the council produces will be litigated for cost or airline consent. A reversibility register is a governance artifact — it survives a board chair transition, it survives a CEO transition, and it gives the next 5 years of board members a defensible framework for saying yes or no to scope changes. This is the artifact I would ask the strategist to draft explicitly.
6. **The federal stack is a schedule risk, not a funding story, for Terminal F.** The FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (PL 118-63) left the PFC cap at $4.50 and authorized $19.4B in AIP over five years — a marginal contribution to a $4B terminal, and slower to appropriate than a bond issuance. The federal decisions that actually gate Terminal F are (a) CBP FIS staffing and any Reimbursable Services Program agreement to backstop international growth, and (b) TSA CT/checkpoint approvals. Both arrive on calendars DFW does not control. The CEO's job is to make the federal calendar the governor of scope, not the excuse.
7. **Board composition — 12 members, Dallas 7 / Fort Worth 4 voting, plus a rotating non-voting neighbor city seat — means the political survivable strategy is one that both mayors can defend in a re-election year.** Chris McLaughlin, appointed by unanimous board vote in April 2025, comes from operations. His political capital will be spent partly on the 82.6%-carrier relationship and partly on making Fort Worth feel Terminal F was not just "American Airlines' terminal in Dallas." Any 2043-horizon design decision that visibly favors originating O&D against connecting geometry will read to Fort Worth's business community as a Dallas-facing decision. That constrains the answer.

## 2. The bond-rating lens: what this thesis does to the credit

DFW's ratings are strong for a hub with 82.6% single-carrier exposure — KBRA AA/Stable on the August 2025 issuance; Moody's A1 (positive), S&P AA-, Fitch A+ on prior public ratings. That structure earned its way to those letters through a hybrid-residual U&L, a rate covenant the agencies trust, and — this is the point — a demonstrated ability to keep coverage above the 1.25x sector floor and days-cash-on-hand at levels the agencies score in the Aa band (Moody's Aa3+ airport median was ~72 days in 2024 per the Airport Monitor). Fitch's methodology sorts single-carrier hubs into >30%, 30–60%, >60% buckets; DFW sits comfortably above the top of that band. Under Moody's framework, "carrier base quality" is a distinct subfactor — American's own corporate credit rating matters to DFW's rating in a way that is not intuitive from operational metrics alone.

The board question is not whether Terminal F changes the *letters.* It won't, in the near term. The board question is what the all-in cost of the next bond issuance looks like if the mix of Terminal F debt, the CIP through FY2030 ($11.3B per DFW disclosures), and the 70th Supplemental Bond Ordinance's $3.0B new-debt authorization (March 2025–February 2026) drives CPE meaningfully above peer hubs *while* American's premium repositioning is still unproven in revenue. That is the fact pattern in which Moody's moves DFW from A1 (positive) to A1 (stable) instead of to Aa3 — a 15–30 basis-point event on a 30-year issuance. On $2B of new-money par, that is $60–120M of lifetime interest. The premium fit-out is cheaper than the credit slip that comes from under-designing it and paying for the retrofit in 2033 with mid-lease debt.

## 3. The airline use-and-lease structure

DFW operates a *hybrid-residual* U&L — the base term runs October 1, 2023 through September 30, 2033, with an April 2025 offer letter granting each signatory the option, exercisable through September 30, 2033, to extend to September 30, 2043. American, as sole occupant of Terminal F, has functionally locked in the extension against Terminal F capital. The MII (majority-in-interest) architecture in a residual-hybrid agreement at 82.6% carrier concentration is essentially an American veto over out-of-scope capital. That is a strategic fact, not a criticism.

The CEO reading: the leverage window closes on Terminal F design at concrete cure. It reopens partially at the 2033 extension trigger (if American walks the option), and fully at 2043. Everything in between is negotiated bilaterally under an existing agreement, and American — headquartered in Fort Worth, sole occupant of a $4B terminal, 82.6% of your traffic — will not lose those negotiations. Design in what you want to see; document the deferrable interiors as options American may exercise; do not assume you can retrofit against American's stated program mid-lease at any price American considers reasonable.

## 4. The federal stack

- **FAA Reauthorization (PL 118-63, 2024)** — five-year, $105.5B, PFC cap unchanged at $4.50, $19.4B for airport infrastructure. Not a Terminal F funding story. The reauthorization matters to DFW's baseline AIP entitlement and to what a future PFC lift (if it happens post-2028) could mean for the debt-service math. The CEO's calendar item: keep the DFW delegation whipping for a PFC lift in the FY2029 reauth cycle without appearing to condition Terminal F on federal money.
- **CBP** — Terminal F expands international operations. CBP FIS staffing is the load-bearing federal decision. The Reimbursable Services Program (established under the Cross-Border Trade Enhancement Act of 2016) is the mechanism that lets DFW backstop CBP overtime and support at airports with 100,000+ arriving international passengers annually. DFW is in the program's precedent set; the question is scope and duration inside Terminal F.
- **TSA** — checkpoint sizing, CT rollout, and Flagship Check-In routing all sit on TSA equipment procurement and staffing calendars. This is where "opens on schedule" gets decided.

## 5. Board governance and what constrains the strategy

The DFW board is 12 members, 11 voting: 7 appointed by Dallas, 4 by Fort Worth in proportion to ownership; the 12th, non-voting seat rotates annually among Irving, Grapevine, Euless, and Coppell. The board approves capital changes and contracts; the two city councils approve the annual budget and bond sales. Chris McLaughlin was appointed CEO by unanimous board vote in April 2025 and started May 19, 2025.

This composition means:

- No design decision survives that visibly favors Dallas O&D over Fort Worth business travel or American's Fort Worth headquarters footprint. The politics of the 4/7 split are that Fort Worth votes as a bloc when it perceives the airport being run for Dallas.
- Term structure is set by ordinance, not statute, and the board has been historically exempted (via Ordinance No. 9272) from the standard nine-member/term-limit framework applied to other Dallas boards. The practical read: turnover happens with mayoral transitions, not on a fixed clock.
- McLaughlin's operations pedigree (Denver COO, TSA PreCheck architect, DFW EVP Ops since 2021) is a signal that the board wants operational credibility in the seat during a $4B build. That is protective of a design-in-now thesis: an operations CEO knows the cost of retrofitting a live terminal.

## 6. The host region

Fort Worth is American's hometown. Dallas is the political and financial center of the metroplex. A design bet on premium concentration inside a single American-only terminal reads differently depending on which side of the airport code you sit on:

- **Fort Worth business community:** Terminal F premium buildout is a Fort Worth win — American's flagship experience is anchored at the airport its headquarters sits ten miles from. This alliance is real and worth using.
- **Dallas business community:** Wants the airport's non-carrier revenue growing and Terminal F not becoming a monoculture at the expense of Terminals A/C/D renewal that serves the broader ecosystem.
- **Texas delegation:** PFC and CBP staffing are the federal touch points. The delegation is bipartisan on airport infrastructure but sensitive to being told American is the reason a federal decision needs to move faster.

The CEO's political-capital allocation for this thesis is not principally with the board — it is with American's C-suite, the two mayors, and the Texas delegation, roughly in that order.

## 7. Long-tenure strategic patterns — what large-hub CEOs got right and wrong, 2000–2025

Three patterns from a quarter century of large-hub CEO tenure that discipline this decision:

- **Under-scoping a fortress-hub build is the more expensive mistake than over-scoping.** ATL, DEN, and DFW itself have each paid retrofit premiums for terminals scoped to yesterday's fleet mix. The mid-lease retrofit runs 2–4x the marginal cost of the same feature designed-in during initial construction — an estimate I hold as professional judgment, not as a single-source number, but one that is consistent with public post-mortems on capital programs.
- **De-hubbing risk is real but survivable when the credit was structured for it.** CVG absorbed Delta's de-hubbing (peak ~90% carrier share falling to ~35% concentration) and Fitch affirmed A+/Stable in July 2024 with DSCR of 11.6x. MEM's Northwest/Delta de-hubbing is the cautionary counter-example. The variable that separates them is whether the airport used the anchor-carrier decade to diversify non-aeronautical revenue and to protect coverage headroom. DFW's answer to that question over the Terminal F decade will matter more than the terminal itself.
- **"Flexibility" is a word capital plans use when they cannot afford the option they claim to be preserving.** The Rams-inflected discipline is: name the option, name its price, name the last responsible moment to decide, name who decides. Anything else is a design memo that will not survive the value-engineering pass. The reversibility register in Finding 3 is this discipline made operational.

## 8. The financial and political case, as the CEO sees it

**For the thesis (design premium capacity in now):**

- Option value is cheapest at wet concrete. Every deferrable interior decision that a modular build makes cheap is a scope amendment DFW will otherwise negotiate against American mid-lease, at American's price.
- The 2043 U&L extension amortizes the design bet over 18 operational years — well aligned to a 30-year bond profile. Under-designing forces mid-lease debt at a moment we do not control.
- American's public actions in 2026 — Flagship Check-In at Terminal D, the largest-ever Admirals Club at Terminal C — validate the premium repositioning as capital-committed, not merely as slide-deck strategy. The signal is credible.
- Rating agencies reward stability and predictability. A written reversibility register is the artifact that makes the design bet defensible to Moody's and KBRA in the next credit review — it is evidence of governance discipline, not just capital ambition.
- Fort Worth mayoral politics benefit visibly from a flagship-tier American terminal; this is a rare capital decision where the harder political sell is under-scoping, not over-scoping.

**Against the thesis (be careful with the premium bet):**

- American's own signal — "Provisions by Admirals Club" at Terminal F, not Flagship lounge — is the near-term truth about how American conceives Terminal F today. Building shell capacity for a decision American has not made obligates DFW's balance sheet without securing American's commitment.
- Every dollar of unamortized premium fit-out is stranded if American's premium strategy misfires. The MEM and pre-merger CO/IAH precedents warn that the anchor-carrier's strategic pivots do not always land.
- CPE headroom is finite. Terminal F on top of an $11.3B CIP is exactly the fact pattern that moves Moody's from A1 (positive) to A1 (stable) instead of upward — a 15–30bp cost on the next issuance.
- The MII structure at 82.6% carrier concentration means American can veto premium build-out DFW wants. Design intent that American has not signed to is design intent that will get value-engineered.
- Board turnover between now and 2030 is guaranteed. Design decisions locked today will be operationally judged and politically owned by board members not yet appointed. Reversibility as documentation matters as much as reversibility as engineering.

## 10. What I would tell the strategist to draft

The reversibility register — module by module, decision by decision, last-responsible-moment, cost of preserving the option, who owns the decision, whether American's consent is required. That is the artifact that turns this thesis from a design memo into a governance instrument the board can approve and the rating agencies can cite. Everything else in the report should be evidence *for* that register.
