# Council Brief — Rams

**Run:** designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures
**Author lens:** Dieter Rams — good design is as little design as possible; honest, understandable, unobtrusive.
**Written for:** Airport executives and capital planners who will not be moved by aesthetic argument alone. Assume they have already been sold on the building. My job is to name the users the building forgets and the decisions that harden into the concrete over the next twenty-four months.

---

## Reframe the customer, or you will design for the wrong one

The DFW Airport Board's press release names customers who do not exist in bulk at Terminal F. It names "state-of-the-art amenities," "expanded shops and restaurants," and "public art installations." American's press release names three products — Flagship Check-In in Terminal D, a 37,000 square-foot Admirals Club in Terminal C, and a "Provisions by Admirals Club" grab-and-go in Terminal F. The building being designed is a connecting bank, occupied exclusively by an airline whose own materials tell you what Terminal F is for: it is the moving belt.

Start there. Every principle I hold about design begins with the person actually using the thing. At DFW, roughly two-thirds of American's customers connect, and at least eighteen of the announced thirty-one gates will be plugged into that connecting geometry from opening day. The originating premium passenger — the one Flagship Check-In and the C-terminal Admirals Club are aimed at — is a small share of the terminal's daily throughput and, per American's own announcement, is not being invited to spend her landside time in F at all. She is being routed through D and C.

So the primary user of Terminal F, in raw headcount, is a customer with a specific and time-bounded task: land, walk (or Skylink), read, walk, board. Forty-five to ninety minutes. Frequently under stress. Almost never noticing the terminal, and that is the highest compliment you can pay a hub-side building. If she notices it, something has gone wrong. My analysis proceeds from that user.

Three of the ten principles do the load-bearing work here. I will apply them and no others: **honesty**, **understandability**, and **as little design as possible**. The other seven are true but do not earn their airtime in this argument.

---

## 1. Honesty (Principle 6): let the building admit what it is

American has already told you, through its product placement, that Terminal F is not tier-one premium real estate in its own hierarchy. The Flagship Check-In lives in Terminal D. The largest-ever Admirals Club lives in Terminal C. Terminal F gets a Provisions — a grab-and-go, explicitly "designed for customers who want high-quality options while on the move." That sentence, from American, is the most honest thing anyone has yet written about Terminal F's role. Believe it.

An honest building matches its promise to the user's need. If the daily user of Terminal F is a connecting customer with less than ninety minutes, then the building's honest promise is: get through here quickly, comfortably, and without confusion. Not: dwell here, be delighted here, shop here. The dishonesty risk is the standard airport-authority instinct to over-sell the terminal as a "destination" in itself — public art, signature retail, gestural roof form — because that is what capital campaigns are built to advertise. That instinct is not evil, but it is a category error, and it costs money now that will be worth nothing to the passenger later.

The specific dishonesty to guard against is the one American has already narrated. If the Provisions is the current signal, do not design in a shelled Flagship Lounge site on a 2043 rumour. Design in the ability to become a Flagship Lounge — structural loading, kitchen exhaust risers, water and grease-waste stacks, dedicated back-of-house egress — but do not build a Flagship Lounge that opens as a Provisions and pretends to be something it is not. The building that tries to be premium and connecting at once, without commitment from the tenant, will read as neither.

*Judgment: The most useful honesty test the Board can apply, module by module, is: "If American never upgrades this space beyond what they have currently announced, is it still a well-made room?" If the answer is yes, build it. If it depends on a premium retrofit that has not been contractually committed, do not build the promise. Build the option to keep the promise.*

## 2. Understandability (Principle 4): design the interaction, not the plan view

The measure of a good terminal, from the connecting passenger's point of view, is not square footage or ceiling drama. It is the number of decisions she has to make between jet bridge and jet bridge, and the cognitive cost of each.

Two evidence points that should sit at the front of every design review:

- ACRP-influenced work at Charlotte Douglas found that consolidating five alpha-coded security zones into three numeric zones "simplified choices and reduced cognitive load," and that travelers systematically overestimate walking-time distances. The intervention that helped was fewer labels, not more signage — plus real-time walk-time indicators that told passengers what they wanted to know (will I make it?), not what the airport wanted to say (here are the amenities).
- Signage-density research using causal machine-learning methods finds that high information density and text-only formats *increase* cognitive load and *reduce* decision confidence under time pressure. Text plus a single graphic outperforms elaborate signage. There is a lower bound on information — you need to know your gate — but above that, more signal reduces performance.

Translate this into Terminal F decisions that are alive right now:

**Landmark before language.** In a linear pier terminal with thirty-one gates, the passenger orients to her gate by a stable, memorable feature before she reads the sign that confirms it. Rams' rule: pick one legible spatial device — a change in ceiling height, a colour, a light quality, a view corridor to the airfield — and use it consistently at every decision point. Consistently. Not five different design gestures because each was interesting on its own.

**One dominant sign at each decision node, not four.** Every decision point in a terminal is a place where a passenger, moving at pace, has to choose. Adding a wayfinding sign, a retail sign, a public-art label, and a digital advertisement to that same node destroys all four of them. The digital signage vendor's incentives are opposed to the passenger's, and if that conflict is not resolved in the design contract, it will be resolved on the passenger's back.

**Walk-time in seconds, at eye height, at the top of every escalator to the concourse.** This is the single cheapest wayfinding intervention available. It answers the connecting passenger's actual question — "will I make it?" — which no map answers.

**The Skylink station is the deep interior of the building for the connecting passenger.** She enters through it and she leaves through it. The centralized Skylink station in Terminal F is therefore the most important room in the building from the user's point of view, and the one most likely to be treated as an infrastructure appendage in the design review. Give it the geometric clarity and the sightline discipline that the ticketing hall usually gets. From the Skylink platform, the connecting passenger should be able to see, without a sign, whether her gate lies right or left of her.

*Judgment: If the design team has spent more hours on the ticketing hall's aesthetic than on the Skylink arrival experience, the design is upside down for the actual user. That is a testable proposition — ask for the hours.*

## 3. As little design as possible (Principle 10): the modular method is a discipline, not a savings

Modular construction, in its industrial form, is not principally a cost play. It is a discipline that forces you to define the interface between modules, and to hold that interface stable across the entire program. The 30 percent cost saving DFW is reporting is the visible surface of that discipline — you cannot ship a football-field-sized module down a taxiway if you are still changing your mind about column spacing, MEP risers, or floor-to-floor height. The method compels commitment early and rewards you for having thought clearly.

The design opportunity — and the risk — is the same fact seen from two sides.

The opportunity: because the interface between modules is standardized, you can hold the *fit-out* of any given module deliberately shallow. A module's structural, MEP, fire-suppression, life-safety, and vertical-circulation systems get built in the factory to a design that has to work everywhere. But its concourse-side fit-out — the ceiling plane, the lighting, the seating, the flooring pattern, the podium hardware, the digital signage — can be treated as a separate, softer layer, deferred and re-specified as late as opening minus twelve months. That is the reversibility dividend of standardization: because the industrial base is stable, the visible surface can move.

The risk: if the industrial discipline is not paired with a matching restraint at the fit-out level, you will get a terminal that is highly standardized structurally and wildly decorated visually — modular columns wearing thirty-one different hats. The passenger will experience that as noise, and it will read as expensive noise. She will not know why she is confused, but she will be. This is the failure mode of modular that airport-authority design reviews are particularly bad at catching, because each individual decoration reads as "one nice thing" in isolation.

Rams' rule, applied literally: pick one visual system for the concourse — one lighting language, one wayfinding language, one seating family, one material palette — and do not let it vary between modules. Every proposed variation carries a burden of proof against the base case: "why does this passenger, in this module, need to be told something visually different from every other passenger in every other module in this same terminal?" Most of the time the honest answer is: no reason, but the vendor offered it.

*Judgment: The Provisions grab-and-go is a gift to the modular method, not a compromise. A small, sharply designed grab-and-go with a fixed menu and a fixed footprint is exactly the kind of thing modular construction excels at: replicable, low-variance, high per-square-foot value. Design it beautifully once and it will read beautifully anywhere. Design one Flagship-with-a-view-of-the-airfield mockup and you will spend two years fighting over it.*

## The reversibility map (what the run prompt actually asked for)

The most useful thing I can produce, per the run prompt, is a reversibility map. I have organized it by user consequence, not by construction discipline, because that is the only ordering the Board will remember.

**Dies with the concrete pour — buy the option now, or never:**

- **Structural bay depth, column grid, floor-to-floor height inside the module.** These fix what you can put inside forever. A shallow floor-to-floor kills a future Flagship Lounge with a proper double-height space over the airfield. Buy the height now on at least one module positioned near the head-of-pier and near international operations, even if you fit it out flat.
- **Vertical MEP risers, kitchen exhaust stacks, grease-waste and potable-water rough-ins to potential lounge and premium F&B locations.** Add them where you might want a lounge, a table-service restaurant, or a Flagship-tier facility later. The marginal cost during factory fabrication is trivial. The cost of coring an operating terminal to add them is measured in seven figures and lost gate-days.
- **Loading capacity on select roof structure for future PV, mechanical, or observation deck.** Add now; ignore later at your discretion.
- **Curbside geometry and roadway bridge grades at the enplaning drive.** Once the ground plane is set for the terminal, the future Flagship pre-security drop-off arrival — a separate curb, a private lane, a covered pull-through — is either possible or impossible. Preserve the option even if American has not yet asked for it here.
- **Vertical clearance and structural bay layout above the future FIS.** If the FIS grows, it has to grow where you put the steel now.
- **Skylink station column grid, platform width, and sightline geometry.** This is the single most important room for the connecting passenger. It has to be right on day one because it can never be right afterward.

**Cheap to defer without penalty — do not spend on these now:**

- Lounge fit-out inside a properly shelled space. Interior lounge design in 2027 is not the design you will want in 2035.
- Digital signage hardware, MUFIDS displays, gate podium technology. These will be a full generation obsolete by opening. Buy at opening minus twelve months, or later.
- Restaurant tenant selection and interior fit-out. Tenant desire in 2027 will not match tenant desire in 2033.
- Seating families, ceiling finishes, wall coverings, most lighting. Cosmetic and replaceable.
- Public art. This is a soft-money layer added last, and should be. It should not drive structural decisions.
- Biometric hardware at the gate. Standards are moving; the physical rough-in (power, data, mounting) is the thing to buy, not the current cameras.

**Middle band — expensive to change, but doable, and where most fights will happen:**

- Restroom stack locations and count. Very hard to move. Over-provision on stack count, especially in the connecting-heavy middle of the pier, before pour.
- Baggage system routing between the module and the CBRA / makeup / claim spaces. Modular helps at the module boundary but not at the system spine.
- Structural loading for future automated tug lanes, autonomous ramp equipment, or airside movement systems. If you might allow airside automation to reach the module by 2035, the module needs to be able to accept the equipment. This is a decision the design team is unlikely to raise unprompted.
- Contact stand geometry that permits future MARS (multiple aircraft ramp system) reconfiguration. If the apron layout does not accommodate a swap of two narrowbodies for one widebody at select gates, the terminal's flexibility to absorb American's widebody premium shift is capped on day one.

*Judgment: The reversibility map should be a formal appendix in the next Board memo on Terminal F, and the "buy the option" line items should be priced explicitly and either funded or rejected with a written finding. The failure mode is silence — decisions made by default at the design-team level, invisible to the Board until they are physically impossible to reverse.*

## Concrete interventions in the last mile — construction tail and first eighteen months

These are things that fit the discipline of "as little design as possible" and would materially improve the passenger's experience without adding building.

1. **Publish a real minimum connect time by product and by concourse pair, at every Skylink station and every escalator into F.** Not "MCT: 60 min." Actual, live, "your connection in Terminal C, walking pace, is 14 minutes." Airports treat this as an airline problem. It is a wayfinding problem.
2. **Delete one sign for every sign added.** Adopt this as a written design principle for the terminal, enforced by the airport's own design review, not by the vendor. The best-case Terminal F opens with less signage per linear foot than the retrofit terminals it connects to, not more.
3. **Ship the Provisions with an honest menu, an honest footprint, and honest hours.** A grab-and-go that is open when the connecting bank is full and closed when it isn't. Do not staff a Flagship-tier lounge that isn't one. American's product placement already tells you what this space is for.
4. **Stage the opening around the connecting passenger, not the ribbon cutting.** Heathrow T5 opened everything on day one and 42,000 bags went missing in the first days. The T5 lesson was not "modular is dangerous"; it was "you cannot commission a hub terminal against a fixed political date without testing every interface with the actual carrier under actual load." Ramp Terminal F into the connecting bank in stages, over months, with agreed-in-advance rollback triggers, and put that in the delivery contract.
5. **Instrument the passenger, not the building.** The single most useful post-opening capital investment is a lightweight passenger-flow measurement system — bluetooth, camera-based, mobile device sensing, whichever survives privacy review — designed to answer one question: how long is the actual walk from Skylink to gate, by hour, by day, versus what the wayfinding claims. Everything else follows from that data.

## What has to be true in 2035 for the design to look wrong

The run prompt asked for this. Five conditions, roughly in order of the damage they would do.

1. **American does not close the premium gap.** If AA's premium repositioning stalls and the airline retrenches around cost, Terminal F's connecting-passenger geometry looks correct but its premium-adjacent options — reserved kitchen risers, shelled lounge space, dedicated curbside geometry — look like sunk decoration. Cost of this being wrong: modest, if the options were preserved rather than built out. High, if the airport built shelled Flagship spaces that never get occupied.

2. **The connecting share falls materially.** If long-haul point-to-point behavior returns (post-pandemic direct routings, or A321XLR-scale ultra-long narrow-body flying that bypasses hubs), the two-thirds connect ratio softens toward 55 percent. Terminal F's design bias toward connecting geometry looks over-committed. Cost of this being wrong: moderate. Connect-heavy terminals age worse than origin/destination-heavy ones because the airport captures less ground-side revenue per passenger.

3. **AA's widebody long-haul migrates away from DFW.** If American's premium widebody flying consolidates at JFK, LAX, or MIA rather than DFW, Terminal F's widebody-capable gates and its FIS look oversized. Preserving MARS flexibility hedges this.

4. **Biometric identity and off-airport check-in eliminate the check-in hall entirely.** If, by 2035, curb-to-gate for the confirmed passenger takes place without a ticketing hall, the terminal's ticketing and check-in footprint reads as anachronism. Rams says: this is why you do not over-invest in an aesthetic ticketing hall. Build the space simply and cheaply enough that its later re-purposing to Flagship arrival, meet-and-greet, or ground-transportation nexus does not cost you the building.

5. **AA merges, is acquired, or exits the DFW hub commitment before 2043.** The lowest-probability, highest-impact scenario. Terminal F becomes a common-use asset with a modular skeleton and a Flagship-shaped bathroom footprint. This is the reason not to build fixed American branding into structure. Every American-specific element in Terminal F should be a fit-out layer, replaceable in weeks, not months.

## What I would say to the Board, if given three minutes

Terminal F is being designed for a customer American Airlines does not intend to bring here at scale. That is not a mistake — the connecting passenger is a real customer with a real need, and she is the customer this building will serve every day. But the design language, the amenity narrative, and the capital plan's public-facing pitch are all borrowed from a different terminal type: the origin/destination flagship terminal, the destination shopping mall, the branded premium hub.

Design honestly for the customer you have. Build the option to serve the premium customer American may or may not decide to bring here later. Refuse the visual noise that modular construction, unchecked, invites. Publish a reversibility map before the next pour, and price the options. Do all this with less, not more.

Nothing about that is inconsistent with a beautiful building. It is what a beautiful building for this program actually looks like.
