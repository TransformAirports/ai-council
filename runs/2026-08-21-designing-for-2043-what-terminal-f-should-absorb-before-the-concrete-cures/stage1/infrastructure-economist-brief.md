# Infrastructure Economist Brief — Terminal F Against the Historical Record

**Run:** `designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures`
**Author:** Infrastructure Economist (Stage 1, independent)
**Date:** 2026-08-19

The Council asked what should be designed into Terminal F before the concrete cures and what stays cheap to defer. That question sits inside a much larger one that the airport-industry balance sheet is now forced to answer: whether a decade of terminal-scale capital programs is producing terminals that are still fit-for-purpose at commissioning. The empirical record — the one a bond analyst actually reads — argues that terminals delivered against a rigid airline-of-the-day program tend to be retrofitted within their first business cycle, and that the retrofit costs more than the option would have cost to preserve up front. Terminal F's $4 billion, 31-gate, single-carrier configuration is a large enough bet that the marginal cost of preserving a few optionality reservations during the pour is a rounding error against the base program, and against the write-downs of the case studies below.

This brief documents the economics; the design implications are the province of the chief engineer and airline strategist agents.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **U.S. airport CapEx demand has grown 15% in two years and roughly 4× in a decade.** ACI-NA's 2025–2029 Infrastructure Needs Study puts five-year needs at **$173.9 billion**, up from $151 billion in the 2023–2027 study [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/press_release/new-aci-na-reports-highlight-economic-impact-and-growing-infrastructure-needs-of-u-s-airports/]. The FAA's 2025–2029 NPIAS estimates **$67.5 billion in AIP-eligible needs across ~18,100 projects**, up from $43.6 billion identified in 2020 [Source: https://alerts.aaae.org/latest/regulatory_093024]. The gap between ACI's industry number and FAA's grant-eligible number — roughly $106 billion — is what airports and their airline signatories are being asked to fund out of debt, PFCs, and rates.

2. **The terminal-scale program is now the modal U.S. airport bet, and its base rate for on-time / on-budget delivery is unflattering.** Berlin Brandenburg opened **nine years late at ~€6.5B against a €2.83B initial budget** — roughly 2.3× [Source: https://www.airportwatch.org.uk/2020/10/nine-years-late-and-x3-over-budget-due-to-problems-berlins-brandenburg-airport-finally-opens-during-a-pandemic/]. Denver's automated baggage system alone escalated from $193M to $311M and contributed to a **$560M airport-wide overrun and 16-month delay**; United abandoned it in 2005 after a decade of failed use [Source: https://www5.in.tum.de/persons/huckle/Drexel_DIA.htm] [Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna8135924]. LAX's Automated People Mover has slipped from a 2023 opening to 2026 and from a **$1.9B contract to roughly $3.34B after settlements** [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report] [Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/losangeles/news/city-council-approves-additional-400-million-to-complete-laxs-automated-people-mover-settle-legal-claims]. Two positive counterexamples exist and deserve equal weight: Heathrow T5 delivered on schedule and within its **£4.3B budget** in March 2008 [Source: https://infrastructuredeliverymodels.gihub.org/case-studies/heathrow-terminal-5/], and Delta's LaGuardia Terminal C opened its first concourse in June 2022 and completed the four-concourse **$4B program two years ahead of schedule** [Source: https://aviationweek.com/air-transport/airports-networks/delta-air-lines-opens-new-4-billion-laguardia-terminal] [Source: https://news.delta.com/delta-debuts-dazzling-terminal-c-facility-new-yorks-laguardia-airport]. Both were governed by a single, sophisticated airline counterparty operating under a rigorous integrated project delivery contract — the same governance geometry Terminal F actually has.

3. **DFW's cost per enplanement has been rebuilt from the FY20 stress peak and is now running well below large-hub norms, which is exactly what buys the capacity to lever up.** DFW CPE was $18.29 in FY20 (pandemic denominator collapse), fell to $11.56 in FY23, and is $13.86 in FY25 — a 24% decline from the pre-Terminal-F baseline [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/DFW]. This matters because CPE is the airline veto lever inside the U&L agreement; airport programs that push CPE above ~$25 (JFK trajectory) tend to trigger airline pushback and MII objections. DFW is starting Terminal F from an unusually low CPE base.

4. **The debt stack that funds Terminal F is already visible in the ratings agencies' models, and it approximately doubles by FY29.** Outstanding DFW debt was **$7.224B when S&P upgraded to AA- in August 2024** and is projected to reach **$12.4B by fiscal 2029** [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/dfw-bonds-get-rating-upgrade-positive-outlook-ahead-of-sale]. Debt per enplanement stood at **$193.33 in FY25** [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/DFW]. Debt service coverage sits at **1.51×** and has been remarkably stable in a 1.43–1.51 band through the pandemic cycle [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/DFW]. The 70th Supplemental Bond Ordinance authorizes up to **$3.0 billion in new debt** in a single twelve-month issuance window (March 2025 – February 2026) — a signal that the CapEx pipeline is intended to be front-loaded [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md, Section 3]. KBRA rated the 2025A/B series **AA, Stable** in August 2025, and S&P and Moody's ratified equivalent grades [Source: https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240809398174/en/KBRA-Rates-Cities-of-Dallas-and-Fort-Worth-TX-Airport-Joint-Revenue-Refunding-and-Improvement-Bonds-AA-Stable].

5. **Terminal F's scope has already inflated 2.5× before the pour, from $1.6B/15 gates to $4B/31 gates.** The May 2025 acceleration announcement moved the program from a $1.6 billion, 15-gate facility to an approximately **$4 billion, 31-gate facility** [Source: https://www.dfwairport.com/dfwnewsroom/dfw-airport-and-american-airlines-announce-acceleration-and-expansion/] [Source: https://www.constructiondive.com/news/dallas-fort-worth-airport-new-terminal-f/747247/]. On a per-gate basis that is roughly $107M/gate at $1.6B/15, versus roughly $129M/gate at $4B/31 — a modest per-gate premium consistent with an expanded international/widebody mix and a larger central processor. The scope change happened *before* concrete; the observation for the Council is that program scope has already moved once, and the reversibility clock started at that moment.

6. **The modular delivery method is the plausible reason DFW's per-gate math is holding together.** Contemporary reporting places modular savings at roughly **30% versus conventional construction** at DFW [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md, Section 5] [Source: https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/usa/2025/09/dfw-airports-4-billion-terminal-f-is-a-modular-marvel/]. The comparable benchmark for the pain avoided is SLC, which has spent **~$5.1–5.2B over a 2014–2026 program** for a rebuild that delivers 94 total gates in a conventional stick-built cadence [Source: https://slcairport.com/thenewslc/rebuild-highlights] [Source: https://constructionreviewonline.com/rehabilitation-of-salt-lake-city-international-airport-in-utah-contact-awarded/]. The economist's read: modular is a schedule tool as much as a cost tool, and its principal option-value is that late-cure decisions on modules not yet placed remain genuinely deferrable — a property conventional terminals do not have.

7. **The opportunity-cost frame is unforgiving. $4 billion buys visible alternatives.** Against DFW's stated $11.3B CIP through FY 2030 [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md, Section 3], Terminal F is roughly 35% of the total. In counterfactual terms, $4B is approximately: 1.3× the entire JFK New Terminal One 2025 green bond issuance ($3.9B across two years [Source: https://www.portauthoritybuilds.com/redevelopment/us/en/jfk/planned-projects/terminal-1/news/the-new-terminal-one-at-jfk-successfully-concludes--1-37-billion.html]); 80% of the entire SLC rebuild cost of ~$5.1B [Source: https://slcairport.com/thenewslc/rebuild-highlights]; and roughly 6× the annual size of DFW's typical bond issuance window. Every deferrable dollar preserved as option is a dollar not committed to a program whose scope moved 2.5× in eighteen months. The Council's reversibility map is, in dollar terms, the highest-return artifact this run can produce.

8. **The industry cost curve is still moving.** DWU Consulting's synthesis of large-hub disclosures documents airport construction cost inflation running well above general CPI, with terminal-project cost benchmarks moving from roughly **$2 billion to $3 billion** for comparable scopes over the past several years [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airport-construction-cost-inflation]. In a program with an FY30 back end, the risk that today's $4B pencil becomes tomorrow's $5B pencil is the operative reason to preserve reservation stubs — not vagueness about "future needs."

## 2. Evidence in context

### 2a. The macro CapEx picture

ACI-NA's biennial Infrastructure Needs Study is the closest thing the industry has to a bottom-up demand figure. The 2025 study reports **"at least $173.9 billion over the next five years"** in needed U.S. airport infrastructure investment, up 15.1% from the 2023 study's $151 billion figure [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/press_release/new-aci-na-reports-highlight-economic-impact-and-growing-infrastructure-needs-of-u-s-airports/]. Long-horizon demand is anchored to **~1.4 billion U.S. enplanements by 2040 and 1.7 billion by 2050** in the same source. The FAA's NPIAS — the grant-eligible slice — is much smaller and grew from **$43.6B (2021–2025) to $62.4B (2023–2027) to $67.5B (2025–2029)** [Source: https://alerts.aaae.org/latest/regulatory_093024]. The delta between the two figures — well over $100B over five years — is the private-market debt burden airports and airlines are collectively absorbing, and it is the reason bond issuance windows like DFW's 70th Supplemental Ordinance keep getting larger.

The ASCE 2024 economic study, cited by ACI-NA, projects a funding gap of **$114 billion between 2024 and 2033 if IIJA investment continues, rising to $162 billion if federal investment reverts to pre-IIJA levels** [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/press_release/new-aci-na-reports-highlight-economic-impact-and-growing-infrastructure-needs-of-u-s-airports/]. Terminal F is being built into a cost environment where federal grants are structurally short of demand and where PFC caps have not moved since 2000.

### 2b. Cost overruns and schedule slips — the base rate

The historical record on terminal-scale programs is closer to a base rate than to a set of one-off horror stories.

**Berlin Brandenburg (BER):** Opened 31 October 2020, roughly nine years after the initial 2011 target; final program cost widely reported at €6.5–7.3B against a €2.83B initial budget — approximately 2.3–2.6× [Source: https://www.airportwatch.org.uk/2020/10/nine-years-late-and-x3-over-budget-due-to-problems-berlins-brandenburg-airport-finally-opens-during-a-pandemic/]. Root causes included fire-suppression, cable, and door problems that would have been caught in a full design cycle before pour. The economist's read: BER is the paradigm case of pouring against an incomplete design because a political deadline required it.

**Denver automated baggage system (DIA):** Original budget $193M, actual close to $311M, plus ~$80M for a manual backup system after the fact and >$100M in terminal-design changes to accommodate the equipment [Source: https://www5.in.tum.de/persons/huckle/Drexel_DIA.htm]. Airport opened 16 months late with an estimated $560M in program-wide overruns [Source: https://www5.in.tum.de/persons/huckle/Drexel_DIA.htm]. United abandoned the system in 2005 after roughly a decade of use [Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna8135924]. This is the canonical case of a technology commitment locked into building geometry that later had to be dismantled.

**Heathrow Terminal 5:** Delivered on time and within its **£4.3B budget in March 2008**, after approximately 19 years of planning [Source: https://infrastructuredeliverymodels.gihub.org/case-studies/heathrow-terminal-5/]. This is the industry's most-cited counterexample and worth studying: single-airline occupant (British Airways), integrated project agreement that pushed risk to BAA, and an unusually long design phase before construction commitment.

**LaGuardia Delta Terminal C:** Opened first phase 4 June 2022, four-concourse **$4B build completed by end of 2024, two years ahead of schedule** [Source: https://news.delta.com/delta-debuts-dazzling-terminal-c-facility-new-yorks-laguardia-airport] [Source: https://aviationweek.com/air-transport/airports-networks/delta-air-lines-opens-new-4-billion-laguardia-terminal]. Delta contributed roughly $3.5B, Port Authority roughly $500M. Single-airline governance again correlates with delivery discipline.

**JFK New Terminal One:** $9.5B project inside a **~$19B JFK-wide transformation**; phased opening 2026 through 2030 [Source: https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-groundbreaking-95-billion-new-terminal-one-major-step-forward-port]. As of 2025, NTO had closed **$3.9B in green bonds across 2024–2025** and secured $6.5B in bank financing [Source: https://www.portauthoritybuilds.com/redevelopment/us/en/jfk/planned-projects/terminal-1/news/the-new-terminal-one-at-jfk-successfully-concludes--1-37-billion.html]. NTO is not yet a proven success or failure and belongs in the base rate as an active data point.

**LAX Automated People Mover:** Budget escalated from **$1.9B to approximately $3.34B**; opening slipped from 2023 to 2026 [Source: https://laist.com/news/transportation/lax-people-mover-delay-grand-jury-report] [Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/losangeles/news/city-council-approves-additional-400-million-to-complete-laxs-automated-people-mover-settle-legal-claims]. A 2024–25 L.A. County Grand Jury attributed the failure to strained LAWA/LINXS relations and inadequate conflict-resolution processes — governance failures, not engineering ones.

**IAD AeroTrain:** Original system opened January 2010 at **$1.5B**, missing a Concourse D stop that has forced continued mobile-lounge operations for 16 years [Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna35066459]. The AeroTrain extension is budgeted at ~$3.75B inside a $22.5B renovation program [Source: https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322123/20260729/dulles-airport-mobile-lounges-scrapped-after-64-years-225b-redesign.htm]. IAD is the cautionary tale most directly applicable to Terminal F: a mid-century decision (mobile lounges) that was cheap at pour and expensive to reverse for six decades.

**Salt Lake City International (SLC):** Program cost **~$5.1–5.2B; construction 2014–2026** for a full replacement of the 1960s terminal complex [Source: https://slcairport.com/thenewslc/rebuild-highlights] [Source: https://constructionreviewonline.com/rehabilitation-of-salt-lake-city-international-airport-in-utah-contact-awarded/]. Delta hub, largely conventional construction. Useful benchmark for what per-gate economics look like when modular is not used.

**Terminal F itself:** The scope has already moved once, from **$1.6B / 15 gates to $4B / 31 gates** in the May 2025 acceleration announcement [Source: https://www.dfwairport.com/dfwnewsroom/dfw-airport-and-american-airlines-announce-acceleration-and-expansion/]. Dallas Innovates and Construction Dive report the program as **the largest modular airport project ever undertaken**, with modules built offsite and installed on foundations [Source: https://dallasinnovates.com/dfw-airports-terminal-f-rises-with-record-setting-modular-construction-and-a-scaled-up-4b-plan-to-meet-global-demand/] [Source: https://www.constructiondive.com/news/dallas-fort-worth-airport-new-terminal-f/747247/].

### 2c. CPE and financial-health picture

DFW's CPE trajectory is the single most important financial fact for the Council's argument. FY20 was $18.29 (pandemic denominator effect), FY21 $15.63, FY22 $12.19, FY23 $11.56, FY24 $13.44, FY25 $13.86 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/DFW]. The FY25 CPE is roughly **24% below the FY20 stress peak** and comfortably inside American Airlines' preferred U&L economics. The MII trigger economics inside the U&L agreement are not public, but the shape of the CPE curve is what makes a $4B additional bond commitment politically survivable inside the airline relationship.

Debt service coverage has been notably stable at **1.43–1.51×** through the pandemic cycle [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/DFW], which is what earned the S&P upgrade to AA- in August 2024 [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/dfw-bonds-get-rating-upgrade-positive-outlook-ahead-of-sale]. **Outstanding debt is projected to grow from ~$7.2B in 2024 to ~$12.4B by FY29** — a ~72% increase [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/dfw-bonds-get-rating-upgrade-positive-outlook-ahead-of-sale]. In airport-finance terms, this is aggressive but not out of pattern for a large hub committing to a decade-scale renewal.

### 2d. Opportunity cost

The strongest economic argument for preserving optionality at Terminal F is that the incremental cost of reservation stubs — structural provisions, MEP capacity, vertical circulation cores, apron loading capacity — is small as a share of the base program, and the opportunity cost of retrofitting them later is unforgiving. IAD's Concourse D omission has cost approximately $1.5B in AeroTrain original spend plus $3.75B in a planned extension, in part because of a routing decision made at pour [Source: https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322123/20260729/dulles-airport-mobile-lounges-scrapped-after-64-years-225b-redesign.htm] [Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna35066459]. DIA spent >$100M on terminal-design changes to accommodate a baggage system that later failed [Source: https://www5.in.tum.de/persons/huckle/Drexel_DIA.htm]. In each case, an early-stage design choice — cheap to change while paper — became the most expensive line item in a subsequent capital cycle.

## 3. Caveats and limitations

- **The DFW-specific unit economics of Terminal F are not public.** Per-gate cost, widebody share, Flagship lounge square footage, gate-mix flex reservations, and the specific modular-manufacturer contract structure are not in the sources I reviewed. All Terminal F per-gate math in this brief is derived arithmetically from published totals ($1.6B/15 and $4B/31) and should be treated as an order of magnitude, not a rate.
- **CPE comparisons across airports are noisy.** Different airports allocate concession, parking, and O&M offsets differently. The FY25 CPE of $13.86 for DFW is not directly comparable to CPE at JFK or LAX without an adjustment for how each airport defines the metric.
- **The 30% modular savings figure is a DFW-supplied claim** that has moved through the trade press [Source: https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/usa/2025/09/dfw-airports-4-billion-terminal-f-is-a-modular-marvel/]. I did not find an independent audit of the actual delta versus a conventional-construction counterfactual. Treat as directional.
- **BER, LAX APM, and DIA baggage are not perfect analogs for a single-airline, modular, U&L-governed terminal.** They are useful as boundary cases, not as forecasts. The LGA-C and Heathrow-T5 counterexamples are structurally closer to Terminal F and get equal weight in the base rate.
- **The KBRA and S&P ratings actions predate the $4B/31-gate scope acceleration.** I have not found a post-scope-change rating action that specifically re-models the additional debt implied by the doubled scope. This is an evidence gap worth flagging for the airport-CEO and airline-strategist agents.
- **The ACI-NA 2025 Infrastructure Needs Study PDF did not decode via WebFetch.** Category-level breakdowns (terminal vs. airfield vs. access) inside that report are not sourced in this brief. If the strategist needs those splits, someone should pull the PDF locally.

## 5. The economist's judgment (not for the evidence file)

The historical record does not say Terminal F will overrun. It says that among terminal-scale programs of this magnitude, the ones that arrived on time and on budget shared three properties: a single sophisticated airline counterparty (LGA-C, T5), integrated project-delivery contracts that pushed real risk to the airport authority (T5), and an unusually long design cycle before pour (T5, 19 years; LGA-C, staged phasing). Terminal F has the first. It appears to have the second in the modular-manufacturer relationship but the contract structure is not public. It has the shortest design cycle of the successful analogs, driven by the May 2025 acceleration.

The most valuable investment the Council can recommend is the one the run prompt already names: the reversibility map. Every dollar of stub, sleeve, and structural reservation preserved during the pour is measured against the >$100M IAD paid to add an AeroTrain extension that was routing-locked in 1962, or the >$100M DIA spent on terminal-design changes to fit a baggage system that ultimately failed. The base rate is on the side of the option-buyer. The modular method makes the option unusually cheap to preserve.

The economist's specific advice to the strategist: frame the recommendations not as "what to build" but as "what to reserve." Reservations cost pennies on the base dollar; retrofits cost multiples. That is the argument the CFO, the ratings agencies, and the American Airlines counterparty can all sign.
