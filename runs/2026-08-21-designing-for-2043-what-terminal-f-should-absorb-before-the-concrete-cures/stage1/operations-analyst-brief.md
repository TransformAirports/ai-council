# Operations Analyst Brief — Terminal F, 2043 Horizon

**Run:** `designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures`
**Author lens:** Airport operations — throughput, turn dynamics, connecting-bank geometry, checkpoint and BHS reality, and the operational-vs-physical reversibility line
**Date:** 2026-08-19

---

## Framing

The operator did not commission this brief to argue whether airfield capacity or terminal capacity is more binding at DFW; both are being worked simultaneously. It commissioned the operations lens on a narrower question: which of Terminal F's design decisions are foreclosed the moment concrete cures, which stay cheap to defer, and which of the operational moves American is already making (a 13-bank restructure, a premium-arrival product, a widebody rebank toward Asia and the Middle East) demand physical hooks in the building before pour.

Two facts set the frame. First, DFW's airfield-side latent capacity has been substantially harvested. Wake recategorization, end-around taxiways, and surface-management work have already been done or are near-done; the FAA's 2018 DFW Capacity Profile listed wake RECAT as a future improvement, and it has since been implemented. Second, the terminal-side latent capacity has not. The next 10–15% of throughput at DFW will come from gate reuse, connection reliability, checkpoint modernization, and bank-compression tools — most of which route through decisions being made right now on Terminal F.

The modular offsite-manufacturing method DFW is deploying is the single most consequential operational fact about Terminal F, because it collapses the cost of designing flexibility in. Under stick-built construction, MARS-ready stands, dual-jetbridge foundations, and oversized MEP capacity cost 8–15% more per position; under modular manufacturing, that premium is amortized across identical factory runs. This is a rare arbitrage and the operations lens argues it should be spent, not banked.

## Key findings

1. **The 13-bank Forever Forward restructure is not yet moving DFW departure OTP.** DFW's Q2 2026 departure rate rose about 1% year over year, and DFW remained the second-worst departure performer among the country's busiest airports, ahead of only Dallas Love Field [Source: https://airlinegeeks.com/2026/07/31/dfw-trails-americans-other-hubs-in-first-quarter-of-new-schedule/]. AA also lengthened scheduled block times to help the ratio [Source: same]. Terminal F should be designed against a thick-peaked, bank-shaped operation — not a fantasy of smoothed loading.
2. **DFW's own construction record proves the modular arbitrage.** DFW moved six pre-fitted modules — the largest 204 ft × 68 ft, 1,200 tons — across two closed runways overnight and delivered nine Terminal C gates two years after design lock [Source: https://www.hok.com/news/2025-05/dfw-airport-completes-move-of-innovative-megastructure-modules-across-airfield-for-terminal-c-expansion/]. Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection were pre-installed. The cost of adding flexibility upstream at the factory is a design-tooling cost, not a site-labor cost.
3. **Widebody turns cost 60–90 minutes; narrowbody 30–45.** Legacy carrier long-haul widebody turns run 90–180 minutes, and AA's 777 turn variance was reported at 56% [Source: https://simpleflying.com/widebody-aircraft-turnaround-times-analysis/]. Every widebody-capable stand at Terminal F costs one narrowbody turn per bank in throughput terms. The stand-mix decision is a throughput decision, not a lounge decision.
4. **MARS geometry is the highest-value pour-locked flexibility available.** MARS gates accommodate one widebody or two narrowbodies at a single stand [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. ORD Terminal 5 is deploying MARS; Helsinki's West Pier and Terminal 2 have used it for years. Under modular manufacture, MARS geometry is a design-once decision; under stick-built, it is an every-stand decision.
5. **Wake RECAT capacity gains are largely booked.** Memphis, the first RECAT implementation, saw up to 22 additional arrivals per hour, roughly a 20% peak-capacity boost [Source: https://www.boldmethod.com/blog/article/2015/01/faa-new-wake-turbulence-recategorization-rules/]. MIT Lincoln Laboratory's DFW-specific analysis estimated $4.7M/year in savings from reducing wake separation from 110s to 90s on arrival, and about +3.5 arrivals per hour on runway 35C from combined wake and runway-occupancy-time reductions [Source: https://www.ll.mit.edu/r-d/publications/potential-benefits-reducing-wake-related-aircraft-spacing-dallasfort-worth]. The airfield-side arbitrage window is closing; the terminal-side one is not.
6. **A-CDM is proven, cheap to preserve, and expensive to add later.** EUROCONTROL's impact assessment across 17 fully implemented A-CDM airports reported reductions in taxi time, fuel burn, flow-management delay, and gate-change churn, and over 34% of ECAC departures now originate from an A-CDM airport [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/airport-collaborative-decision-making-cdm-impact-assessment]. Independent EUROCONTROL data snapshot reports taxi-out savings between 0.25 and 3 minutes per departure at implemented airports [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/eurocontrol-data-snapshot-40-taxi-times]. The physical hooks — cabling, rack rooms, ramp-tower sightlines and CCTV coverage — are pre-pour work.
7. **CT-based checkpoint lanes are pour-locked at the room dimension, not the machine.** DHS S&T's target for next-generation lanes is 300 passengers per hour per lane [Source: https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/300-people-hour-lane]; live CTiX deployments run about 200/hr average and 330/hr peak [Source: https://www.vanderlande.com/news-insights/passenger-throughput-how-airports-can-improve-performance-at-the-checkpoint/]. Casework can be swapped; roller conveyor length, secondary search depth, ceiling height, and structural bay width cannot. Terminal F should be dimensioned to next-generation lane geometry, not to 2015-era ETD geometry.
8. **Skylink is not the constraint; the walk to Skylink is.** Skylink runs 2-minute headways with a 9-minute maximum interterminal transit [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylink_(Dallas_Fort_Worth_International_Airport)]. AA's DFW hub carries roughly two-thirds connecting passengers at the carrier level, and roughly 60% at the airport level [Source: airport-context.md §4]. Terminal F's connecting geometry — vertical circulation core count, distance from farthest gate to Skylink station, and BHS crossover to Terminal D — will determine whether AA can hold sub-45-minute domestic-domestic MCTs at the 2035 volume, not the 2027 volume.

## Evidence discussion

### What premium repositioning implies operationally, not physically

The prompt's premium framing lists lounges, Flagship arrival, widebody mix, and connecting-versus-originating space. Read through the operations lens, three of those four are BHS, FIS, and stand-geometry decisions long before they are amenity decisions.

Premium at scale means widebody utilization goes up: more international rebank toward Asia and the Middle East, more premium-heavy fleets that turn slower. Simple Flying's turn-variance analysis put widebody turns at 60–90 minutes and long-haul widebody turns at 90–180, with AA's 777 turn variance at 56% [Source: https://simpleflying.com/widebody-aircraft-turnaround-times-analysis/]. Every widebody-capable stand at Terminal F costs one narrowbody turn per bank. The stand mix is the load-bearing decision — and it is pour-locked at the fuel pit, at the PCA/400 Hz sizing, at the jetbridge foundation, and at the ramp lead-in line geometry.

American's Terminal F lounge posture, as publicly announced, is a Provisions by Admirals Club grab-and-go, not a Flagship-tier lounge [Source: airport-context.md §6]. American's own scale move — a 37,000 sq ft Admirals Club in Terminal C, its largest anywhere [Source: airport-context.md §6] — is the direction of premium travel. The operational question for Terminal F is not "build the Flagship lounge American isn't asking for." It is "build the mechanical shells, roof-load capacity, vertical circulation, and utility risers that let American convert Provisions into Flagship in 2033 without gutting the building." MEP oversizing is roughly 3–5% marginal during construction and 40–60% as a retrofit against live operations (chief-engineer judgment domain; not sourced to a single publication).

### Gate flexibility is the operational option worth paying for

MARS stands accommodate one widebody or two narrowbodies at the same position [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/2024/09/03/enhancing-airport-operations-the-power-of-mars-gates/]. ORD Terminal 5's MARS deployment [Source: https://blog.adbsafegate.com/adb-safegate-to-provide-automated-aircraft-parking-on-new-terminal-5-gates-at-chicago-o-hare/] and Helsinki West Pier's MARS design [Source: airport-context.md context] are the reference cases.

The reversibility read is stark: MARS lead-in lines, dual-jetbridge foundations, dual fuel pits, and per-stand PCA/400 Hz capacity are pour-locked. Once the fuel pit is set, the stand is committed for the asset life. Because DFW is modularly manufacturing the gate structures, MARS-ready is a factory-tooling decision, not a per-stand carpentry decision. The design-for-flex premium collapses.

Judgment (mine, not sourced): a defensible baseline is that any stand within reasonable widebody reach at Terminal F should be MARS-provisioned to the fuel pit and PCA riser even where the initial fit-out is narrowbody. That preserves the option to swing to widebody in a 2035 rebank without touching the deck.

### The 13-bank restructure is proof of ops-first, but also a warning

Cranky Flier reported AA's shift from 9 to 13 banks per day at DFW [Source: https://crankyflier.com/2026/01/05/american-prioritizes-operational-improvement-with-dfw-hub-changes/]; the AA COO framed it as reducing cascading gate-block effects [Source: https://www.dallasnews.com/business/airlines/article/american-airlines-improving-dfw-airport-operation-22191799.php]. AA's own release described "measurable improvements in on-time arrivals and departures, customer connections and baggage handling" two weeks post-launch [Source: https://news.aa.com/news/news-details/2026/Forever-Forward-at-DFW-Enhanced-flight-schedule-unlocks-countless-customer-benefits-OPS-INF/default.aspx].

The Q2 2026 audit is more sober. Airline Geeks reported ~1% year-over-year improvement in DFW's departure rate and continued bottom-quartile ranking among busy US airports; block times were lengthened, which mechanically improves scheduled OTP without changing airborne speed [Source: https://airlinegeeks.com/2026/07/31/dfw-trails-americans-other-hubs-in-first-quarter-of-new-schedule/]. USAFacts reported DFW at 71% on-time departures in 2024, ranked 30th among major US airports [Source: https://usafacts.org/articles/what-are-the-best-and-worst-airlines-for-on-time-performance/].

The operations reading: schedule surgery is delivering less than the release copy implied. Terminal F should not be designed on the assumption that Forever Forward flattens the profile. The peak-bank sizing case — FIS staffing model, CBRA capacity, checkpoint lane count, contact-stand count — should reflect the operation as-observed, not as-hoped.

### Airfield is nearly booked; terminal is the frontier

Wake RECAT at Memphis delivered up to 22 additional arrivals per hour [Source: https://www.boldmethod.com/blog/article/2015/01/faa-new-wake-turbulence-recategorization-rules/]. MIT Lincoln Lab's DFW analysis quantified the value of tighter wake spacing and shorter runway-occupancy times in dollar and arrivals-per-hour terms [Source: https://www.ll.mit.edu/r-d/publications/potential-benefits-reducing-wake-related-aircraft-spacing-dallasfort-worth]. LaGuardia's DSAS work showed +9 departures per hour at peak with little arrival impact [Source: https://doi.org/10.2514/atcq.23.4.245]. The airfield playbook is mature.

The remaining terminal-side moves are: gate-reuse discipline, MCT reliability, connection-bag routing speed, checkpoint throughput, and A-CDM's gate-hold and taxi-metering discipline. All of them route through choices being made on Terminal F today.

### Skylink and the connecting geometry

DFW's connecting share is roughly 60% airport-level; AA's carrier-level share at DFW is closer to two-thirds [Source: airport-context.md §4]. Skylink's 2-minute headway and 9-minute maximum interterminal time [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylink_(Dallas_Fort_Worth_International_Airport)] make the technical MCT achievable — 40 minutes domestic-to-domestic is AA's published standard at DFW, and 40 minutes is the domestic-to-international minimum [Source: airport-context.md §7 context].

The operational stake for Terminal F: the distance from the farthest F gate to the F Skylink station, the escalator core count at that station, and the BHS priority-bag crossover to Terminal D determine whether AA can hold those MCTs at 2035 bank density. Terminal F's stated walking connection to Terminal D [Source: airport-context.md §5] is only as useful as the vertical circulation feeding it. All of those dimensions are pour-locked.

### TSA checkpoint sizing is pour-locked at room geometry

DHS S&T's benchmark for next-generation lanes is 300 passengers per hour [Source: https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/300-people-hour-lane]; industry deployments are hitting 200 average, 330 peak [Source: https://www.vanderlande.com/news-insights/passenger-throughput-how-airports-can-improve-performance-at-the-checkpoint/]. Legacy ETD lanes typically clear 150–180 (operations judgment; specific numbers vary by airport and configuration).

The physical decisions that lock next-generation lane throughput into the building are lane width, roller conveyor run length, secondary search area, ceiling height, and structural bay depth for future lane additions. Those are all pour-locked. Casework depth and divest-lane count can be adjusted after opening; the room the casework sits in cannot.

## Counterexamples — where operations could NOT save the building, and infrastructure was the right answer

Three domains where the operations lens explicitly cedes to concrete:

1. **CBP/FIS hall sizing.** Once the primary hall floor plate, escalator core count, and exit-corridor geometry are committed, no A-CDM, biometrics, or MPC kiosk deployment recovers from an undersized primary. MIA's 2010s FIS retrofits and JFK T4's serial expansions are cautionary. Terminal F's international growth strategy needs the FIS sized to the 2035 CBP staffing plan, not the 2027 one.
2. **Baggage handling trunk lines and CBRA.** BHS mainline cross-section and CBRA turn-vault radii are pour-locked. Denver Airport's automated baggage saga is the reductio ad absurdum, but the operational point is narrower: at peak connecting bank, CBRA is the constraint, and it is set by concrete.
3. **Vertical circulation between gate level, Skylink level, and BHS level.** Escalator count, elevator shaft count and capacity, and stair-return geometry are among the least reversible decisions in a terminal. Undersize them and you get chronic peak-hour throughput failure that no scheduling program fixes. This is where Council should be most conservative — spend the money now on one extra escalator core per pier.

## Reversibility map (operations lens)

**Pour-locked — decide before concrete cures:**
- Stand geometry and MARS provisioning (fuel pit, PCA/400 Hz sizing, jetbridge foundation, lead-in line)
- BHS trunk cross-section and CBRA footprint
- CBP/FIS floor plate and exit corridor
- Vertical circulation core count and shaft capacity (gate to Skylink to BHS)
- Skylink station platform length and escalator provision
- Structural bay depth for future TSA lane growth
- Roof/deck load for future rooftop mechanical (enables lounge upgrade)
- Cable trays, rack rooms, and ramp-tower sightlines for A-CDM instrumentation

**Cheap to defer — do not spend the option now:**
- Lounge tier fit-out (Provisions ↔ Flagship swap inside a provisioned MEP shell)
- Retail and F&B tenant mix and casework
- Boarding-gate biometrics and automated boarding lane count
- Jetbridge model within an already-provisioned dual-bridge footprint
- Dynamic signage and wayfinding technology
- FIDS content and passenger-app integration

## Innovations plausible in late construction or the first 18 months

Within an operations lens, not a technology-scout lens:

1. **A-CDM stand-up at day one.** Cabling, tower sightlines, and CCTV coverage installed pre-pour; process integration with AA operations control, FAA TRACON, and DFW ramp tower stood up in the first six months. EUROCONTROL evidence base suggests 0.25–3 minutes per departure taxi-out savings and material reductions in flow-management delay [Source: https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/eurocontrol-data-snapshot-40-taxi-times].
2. **Gate-hold discipline as a formal operating rule.** The A-CDM literature repeatedly shows that gate-hold moves burn less fuel than taxi-hold and free the airfield; Terminal F's remote apron layout should include gate power/PCA sufficient for extended gate-hold without APU.
3. **Bank-compressed connecting-bag routing pilot.** Priority-bag rules for premium and international connections routed on dedicated BHS diverters. This requires only software and diverter configuration if the BHS trunk was sized for it — hence the pour-locked BHS argument above.
4. **First-18-months MARS activation on 2–4 stands.** Prove the concept at Terminal F, tune ramp handling procedures, then extend as widebody rebank matures.
5. **Instrumented turn-time telemetry.** Every stand instrumented for on/off block, chock in/out, boarding start/end, door close. AA already collects most of this; publishing it back to airport ops enables real gate-reuse arbitrage at 2035 density.

## What would have to be true in 2035 for this brief to look wrong

- If the 13-bank restructure delivers what the release copy claims by 2028 — say, DFW moves from 71% OTP to 82%+ and misconnects fall by a third — the case for extra checkpoint bay depth and BHS trunk capacity weakens. My read is that the Q2 2026 data does not yet support that projection.
- If American's premium rebank plateaus at Terminal C and Terminal F remains a domestic connecting operation through 2035, MARS provisioning at F was overspent. The operational hedge is asymmetric: the cost of MARS-ready is low; the cost of not-MARS is a locked stand geometry for 30 years.
- If CT lane throughput underperforms — 200/hr peak rather than 330/hr — and TSA moves back to a bins-and-belts model, extra bay depth is wasted. The DHS S&T and Vanderlande evidence argue against that scenario, but it is real.
- If Skylink is replaced by a different intra-airport connector in the 2035–2043 window (unlikely but not impossible), the argument for Skylink-facing vertical circulation cores collapses to Terminal-D-facing walking connectors. This is a governance question the operations lens does not resolve.

## Bottom line

The operations lens on Terminal F is not "operations will save the building." It is: the modular manufacturing method DFW is using collapses the cost of encoding operational learnings into the physical asset, and the airfield-side arbitrage is nearly booked. That combination makes it a design malpractice not to spend the flexibility premium on stand geometry, BHS trunking, CBP/FIS floor plate, vertical circulation, and checkpoint bay depth right now. The lounge, the retail mix, the biometrics, and the boarding-gate technology can all wait. The concrete cannot.
