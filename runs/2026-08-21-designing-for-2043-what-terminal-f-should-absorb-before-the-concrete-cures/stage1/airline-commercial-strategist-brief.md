# Airline Commercial Strategist Brief — Terminal F, 2043 Horizon

**Run:** designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures
**Author lens:** Twenty years airline commercial, ten years airport-side. I have watched five hubs get dehubbed and every one of them was overbuilt for a carrier whose route economics had already decided.
**Central argument I will defend in Stage 2:** Terminal F's fate is determined more by what American does between now and 2043 than by anything DFW pours between now and 2030. Any Terminal F design choice that has not been stress-tested against "what will American do in response" is incomplete. That question is my veto.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **American's premium repositioning is a real strategic shift and it is behind Delta, not ahead of it.** Delta's Q4 2025 premium ticket revenue ($5.70B) exceeded main cabin ticket revenue ($5.62B) for the first time in the company's history; full-year 2025 premium reached $22.1B against $23.4B main cabin, and premium climbed from ~45% of ticket revenue in Q1 2024 to ~50% by Q4 2025. American's public target is to grow premium seats at roughly twice the rate of main cabin through 2030, lifting narrowbody premium share from ~25% to ~40% and international lie-flat count by more than 50%. American is chasing a moat Delta already dug.
2. **Terminal F is being scoped against a connecting-hub geometry that American is simultaneously reinforcing, not softening.** American's December 2025 restructure moves DFW from 9 to 13 daily banks effective April 2026 — this is a doubling-down on connecting-bank complexity, not a shift toward origin-and-destination premium. Design assumptions built on the run prompt's "two-thirds connect" figure are consistent with carrier behavior; design assumptions built on the airport-cited 60% are within 5 points of the same picture. The 30% figure quoted in some AA press coverage refers to a narrower slice and should not drive gate mix.
3. **DFW's cost-per-enplanement gives American an unusual amount of tolerance for capital additions — but that headroom is spending down fast.** DFW 2024 CPE was $13.44 and 2025 was $13.56–$13.59, versus $29.84 at ORD, $33.85 at LAX, $38.37 at JFK, and $35.35 at EWR. But DWU Consulting's projection puts DFW at $16.99 by 2026, a ~25% jump in a single year, before Terminal F debt service fully arrives. CPE is the single number airlines watch when deciding whether to divert growth. American's willingness to sign to 2043 is priced against a CPE that has not yet absorbed the full $4B Terminal F stack.
4. **The 2043 use-and-lease extension is real commitment, but the MII architecture means American controls what gets built inside Terminal F.** DFW's uniform U&L runs to 2033 base term with a Signatory-Airline option through 2043; the extension amends FY2034–2043 revenue sharing to eliminate the Upper Threshold and split net revenue 45/55 DFW/airlines. Every scope change inside Terminal F requires American's consent under exclusive-use terms. The airport controls the envelope; the carrier controls what fills it. Any "we could add this later" design assumption is only true if American wants it added.
5. **American's own lounge signal for Terminal F contradicts the premium-repositioning thesis.** American announced a Provisions by Admirals Club grab-and-go for Terminal F — explicitly a scaled-down concept — while placing its largest-ever 37,000-sq-ft Admirals Club in Terminal C and Flagship Check-In in Terminal D. That is a revealed preference. The airport should design Terminal F to preserve the option to upgrade the lounge concept later, and it should assume, until American says otherwise in writing, that American does not currently plan a Flagship-tier lounge in Terminal F.
6. **The modular gate method is genuinely differentiated on cost and schedule — approximately 30% cost savings per DFW and secondary sources — but "modular" does not mean "swappable." The reversibility premium is smaller than the marketing suggests.** Structure, MEP risers, fixed FIS geometry, baggage-handling backbone, apron gauge-mix commitments, and jet-bridge apron footprints become effectively permanent when the module is placed. The reversibility map the council needs is finer-grained than "modular equals flex."
7. **Five dehubbing cases (CVG, PIT, STL, MEM, CLE) share one pattern: the airport built or committed to major infrastructure for a specific carrier whose route economics had already turned marginal.** Pittsburgh's 1992 Midfield Terminal was underwritten with roughly $600M in county bonds, ~$85M PA, and ~$101M FAA; US Airways dehubbed in 2004 citing costs, and the stranded debt was not extinguished until 2019. CVG went from 600+ daily flights and 22.7M passengers in 2005 to under 180 daily and under 6M passengers by 2013. STL fell from 500+ daily AA/TWA flights in 2001 to 207 by 2003, driven by the ORD slot restriction lifting in 2002. In each case the airport's warning signal was visible in the carrier's route economics 18 months out; the airport's capital plan was not.
8. **The strongest single instrument the airport has for 2043 durability is not a building, it is the contract terms and the option value inside them.** Whether Terminal F becomes a stranded asset in a 2040 dehub scenario depends less on lounge square footage than on: what CPE trajectory it forces on the residual signatory pool, whether the Terminal F cost basis is isolable from the rest of the residual formula in a workout, and whether MII structure allows the airport to reprogram gates to a second signatory carrier if American's DFW footprint contracts. The airline-strategist read is that scope, cost, and use-agreement architecture matter more than architecture.

## 2. Evidence — the airline-side dynamics that determine what "works"

### 2.1 US airline industry structure post-consolidation

The four-major oligopoly (American, Delta, United, Southwest) that emerged from the 2008–2016 merger wave — Delta/Northwest 2008, United/Continental 2010, Southwest/AirTran 2011, American/US Airways 2013, Alaska/Virgin America 2016 — inverted the carrier-airport leverage that had prevailed under deregulation-era fragmentation. A single-carrier fortress hub is now the modal structure at DFW (American 82.6% passenger share, 82% of departures per DFW airport statistics [Source: airport-context.md §4, citing DFW statistics]). That concentration is Terminal F's opportunity and its risk: it is why $4B for one carrier makes commercial sense, and it is why the downside case (contraction or dehub) is more binding than at a multi-carrier facility.

### 2.2 Hub-and-spoke economics and bank structure

A real hub — as distinct from a "large focus city" like Southwest at BWI or MDW — earns its economics by aggregating traffic across banks and routing it through gate proximity. American's move from 9 to 13 daily banks at DFW, effective April 2026, is a doubling-down on connecting complexity [Source: https://news.aa.com/news/news-details/2025/Doubling-down-on-DFW-American-further-strengthens-its-Flagship-hub-OPS-OTH-12/default.aspx]. American publicly reports a 50% reduction in missed connections tied to the rebanking effort, which is the operational signature of a real hub investment [Source: https://simpleflying.com/american-airlines-dfw-hub-improvements/]. Bank density and gate contiguity are the two variables that determine whether a terminal supports the hub or degrades it. A terminal built with generous originating-passenger flows and long walking distances between contiguous banked gates would erode connection economics faster than any lounge choice would compensate.

### 2.3 CPE — where American's tolerance starts to bend

DFW's 2024 CPE of $13.44 sits below the peer-hub band ($29–$38 at gateway hubs and ~$10–$17 at most connecting hubs) [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/info/data/cpe]. Reference points at 2024: ATL $3.94, CLT $4.74, DEN $12.76, IAH $10.66, DTW $9.20, MSP $11.06, MIA $16.83, PHL $15.03 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/info/data/cpe]. American's largest-hub CPE peer set — CLT ($4.74), ORD ($29.56), MIA ($16.83), PHL ($15.03), DCA/LGA/JFK (variable) — puts DFW in the second quartile of American's own network. DWU projects DFW to $16.99 in 2026 [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/info/data/cpe], with Terminal F debt service not fully layered in.

My professional judgment: airlines start to change behavior — divert growth, resist capital additions through MII, negotiate offsets — when a hub CPE crosses roughly $20–$22 for connecting traffic, higher for O&D. DFW is likely to breach that band during Terminal F's ramp, and American's willingness to absorb it is priced against the premium revenue lift American forecasts from Terminal F traffic. That is a fragile assumption to lean the building on.

### 2.4 The 2043 U&L agreement and MII

DFW operates a residual-hybrid U&L with a base term of Oct 1, 2023 through Sep 30, 2033 and an April 2025 offer letter giving each Signatory Airline the option to extend to Sep 30, 2043; the extension amends FY2034–2043 revenue sharing to eliminate the Upper Threshold and split net revenue 45/55 DFW/signatory airlines [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airports/DFW]. MII clauses typically require both a majority-by-number and a majority-by-activity of signatories to consent to major capital additions [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-use-agreements]. With American at 82% of activity, majority-by-activity is a single-carrier veto in practice.

Consequence for Terminal F design: anything American does not want inside its exclusive-use terminal will not be built at DFW's discretion. Anything the airport wants to preserve the right to add later must be either (a) contractually pre-approved with agreed rate treatment, or (b) housed outside American's exclusive-use envelope. This is a legal-architecture question that has to be answered before the concrete cures.

### 2.5 American's premium repositioning — what the fleet plan actually commits to

American's disclosed plan is to grow premium seats at roughly 2x the rate of main cabin through 2030, with narrowbody premium share moving from ~25% to ~40% of capacity, and international lie-flat count growing more than 50% by the end of the decade [Source: https://news.aa.com/news/news-details/2026/American-continues-retrofitting-fleet-to-offer-customers-more-premium-seating-than-ever-FLT-06/default.aspx]. Specific configurations: 787-9P at 51 Flagship Suites / 32 premium economy / 18 MCE / 143 economy; 777-300ER retrofit at 70 Flagship Suites / 44 premium economy / 216 economy; A321XLR carrying Flagship Suite; A319 to 12 premium seats and A320 to 16 [Source: https://premium-flights.com/american-airlines-flagship-suites-routes/].

The Delta benchmark is unambiguous. Delta Q4 2025 premium ticket revenue of $5.70B surpassed main cabin at $5.62B — a first in company history — with full-year 2025 premium at $22.1B (+7%) versus main cabin at $23.4B (–5%), and premium/loyalty/cargo/maintenance/travel-products together now 60% of total revenue [Source: https://onemileatatime.com/news/delta-premium-cabin-revenue-exceeds-economy-revenue/]. American reported record full-year 2025 revenue of $54.6B with premium unit revenue outperforming main cabin, but has not disclosed a comparable premium-vs-main-cabin crossover [Source: https://americanairlines.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/american-airlines-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025].

**Physical implications for Terminal F, priced against that fleet plan:**

- **Widebody gate count is the load-bearing question.** More Flagship Suites means more widebody rotations means more Group V/VI apron footprint, dual jet bridges, and deeper hardstand geometry. Widebody vs. narrowbody gate mix is a Section 9 gap in the airport context packet — I strongly recommend the council pin down the current planned mix and its flex range before making any lounge or check-in recommendation.
- **Premium check-in geometry.** Flagship Check-In in Terminal D is the near-term commitment. Terminal F's ticket lobby should preserve a physical envelope (footprint, curb access, dedicated screening path) for a Flagship-caliber check-in even if the initial fit-out is standard. This is the highest-value option to purchase now: the marginal cost of preserving 8,000–12,000 sq ft of curbside-adjacent shell and a dedicated screening lane is far below the cost of retrofitting.
- **Lounge shell before lounge fit-out.** The announced Provisions concept is scaled-down. The design should preserve mechanical, structural, and views-of-airfield conditions to convert a Provisions footprint into a Flagship-tier lounge later. Lounges are ceiling-height, HVAC-load, and glazing decisions before they are furniture decisions. Those decisions are made when structure is designed, not when tenant fit-out is bid.
- **Connecting vs. originating floor plate ratio.** With ~60% connecting share airport-wide and American's rebank amplifying that, Terminal F should be scoped closer to a connecting-heavy floor plate than to a JFK T4-style originating gateway plate. Vertical circulation, seating density at gate holdrooms, and airside dwell amenities should be sized for a passenger who has just deplaned, not one who has just entered the terminal.

### 2.6 International widebody and gateway implications

American announced six new international routes for summer 2026 including DFW–Budapest, DFW–Prague, DFW–Athens, DFW–Milan, DFW–Zurich, plus expanded DFW–Buenos Aires [Source: https://aviationa2z.com/index.php/2025/08/07/american-airlines-6-new-international-routes-summer-2026/]. American also announced a 6% year-over-year DFW capacity expansion for Q3 2026 alongside a 13% reduction at London Heathrow [Source: https://www.travelandtourworld.com/news/article/0cuztcfm87uv/]. The pattern is clear: American is shifting long-haul weight into DFW and out of joint-venture partner geography where the economics do not support unilateral American metal.

Design consequence: Terminal F's FIS (Federal Inspection Services) sizing, CBP consultation, and international-arrivals corridor are more load-bearing than the lounge decision. FIS capacity is a hard cap on international-arrival growth once poured. Under-sizing FIS in 2027 to save capex could cap American's DFW international growth in 2032. That is the single easiest way to accidentally strand Terminal F's value proposition.

### 2.7 Dehubbing case studies — the mechanism

**CVG (Delta, 2005–2013):** 22.7M passengers and 600+ daily flights in 2005, down to under 6M and under 180 daily by 2013 [Source: https://simpleflying.com/what-happened-delta-air-lines-hub-cincinnati/]. Comair closure announced July 2012, operations ended September 2012 [Source: https://aviation-airport.fandom.com/wiki/Cincinnati/Northern_Kentucky_International_Airport]. Public reason: "high costs associated with rising oil prices." Actual reason: fifty-seat regional-jet economics collapsed when fuel crossed $80 and Delta's Northwest merger created ATL/DTW/MSP redundancy. The airport's error was capital committed against the pre-merger network.

**PIT (US Airways, 1992–2004):** Midfield Terminal opened 1992, ~$1B total, ~$600M county bonds, ~$85M state, ~$101M FAA [Source: https://slate.com/business/2025/11/pittsburgh-international-airport-us-airways-american-airlines.html]. US Airways dehubbed 2004 citing costs; stranded debt remained until roughly 2019 [Source: https://www.pressreader.com/usa/pittsburgh-post-gazette/20190921/281762745972638]. The building was designed to US Airways spec. When the carrier left, the airport carried the debt for 15 more years and CPE stayed elevated the whole time. The lesson is not "don't build for the carrier" — it is "don't let carrier-specific customization outlive the carrier's ability to fill it."

**STL (American/TWA, 2001–2003):** AA/TWA operated 500+ daily flights in July 2001. The ORD slot restriction that had propped up STL as a reliever was lifted in 2002. AA cut 210 flights in 2003, closed Concourses B and D, retreated to Concourse C [Source: https://simpleflying.com/what-happened-american-airlines-hub-st-louis/]. Public reason: post-9/11 traffic. Actual reason: American never needed STL as a hub once ORD constraints eased. The airport's error was believing a merger-driven hub-of-convenience was a permanent hub.

**MEM (Northwest/Delta, 2013):** Delta announced downgrade to non-hub in 2013 after Delta/Northwest merger created ATL/DTW/MSP overlap. The airport had invested in Concourse B, subsequently demolished/repurposed. The Delta press language cited "underperforming route economics."

**CLE (Continental/United, 2014):** United announced dehub February 2014, cited "sustained losses." The airport had invested in expanded gates for the merged carrier. United shifted connecting traffic to ORD and EWR.

The common pattern across all five: the airport's capital commitments matured after the carrier's route-economic decision was already implicit in fleet plans, unit revenue trends, and network overlap disclosed in the carrier's 10-K. In every case, an airline commercial strategist reading the carrier's Form 41 and 10-K could have called the dehub 18 months before it was announced.

### 2.8 What American would do in response — the veto question applied

If Terminal F is built with:
- **Under-sized FIS →** American caps international growth at DFW around 2032, redirects widebody deliveries to MIA (already a Latin/Caribbean gateway with FIS headroom), CLT margins erode further.
- **Insufficient widebody gate count or apron gauge →** American holds narrowbody schedule but caps long-haul; the "largest single-carrier hub in the world" claim contracts.
- **Provisions lounge as the permanent Terminal F concept →** premium originating passengers self-select to Terminal D and Terminal C, Terminal F becomes the connecting-traffic terminal by default; the premium repositioning proceeds without Terminal F.
- **Connecting-bank walking distances that break the 13-bank timing →** American absorbs the cost through banked scheduling looseness and margin loss, then presses DFW on CPE credits; if unresolved, revisits DFW growth vs. CLT/PHX growth in the 2030 fleet plan.
- **Curbside geometry that cannot support Flagship Check-In envelope →** foreclosed forever once poured.

The council's reversibility map should be scored against these five carrier-response scenarios explicitly.

## 4. Evidence gaps I could not close with public sources

- Terminal F widebody vs. narrowbody gate mix and preserved flex-gate positions (airport context §9).
- Confidential U&L economics: MII trigger thresholds for capital additions, and any Terminal-F-specific rate treatment carve-outs.
- Whether the 2025 U&L amendment obligates American to a Flagship-tier lounge in Terminal F at any point before 2043.
- Design and permitting status of Terminal F FIS.
- CBP staffing model committed for Terminal F international arrivals.
- Reversibility window per modular gate unit — how many days after placement can structural and MEP decisions still change without schedule impact.

These are not questions the airline-commercial-strategist lens can resolve. They should sit with airport-CEO, chief-engineer, and airport-COO agents. But every one of them is load-bearing on whether the design absorbs American's repositioning cleanly.

---

## 5. The one-line thesis I bring to Stage 2

**Terminal F should be scoped as a connecting-heavy building with rigorously preserved premium options — widebody gate flex, FIS capacity headroom, Flagship-caliber check-in envelope, and lounge shell — because American's revealed strategy is to grow premium through the fleet and the 13-bank, not through the terminal, and the airport's job is to make sure it can catch American's premium play if the carrier decides to route it through DFW after 2030 rather than around it.**
