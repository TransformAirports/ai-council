# Narrative Options — Designing for 2043: What Terminal F Should Absorb Before the Concrete Cures

**Run:** `designing-for-2043-what-terminal-f-should-absorb-before-the-concrete-cures`
**Producer:** creative-director
**Date:** 2026-08-20
**Inputs:** run prompt; `outputs/run-manifest.json` (12 research agents, all briefs complete); `outputs/context/airport-context.md`; `outputs/stage1/evidence-map.md`; `outputs/evidence-ledger.jsonl` (257 records)

Three genuinely different spines for the same evidence base. None invents a fact. Each changes what the reader sees first, what the argument asks of them, and which decision it walks them toward. Evidence IDs use the ledger's namespaced form (`agent::ev-…`); bare record numbers follow the evidence map's numbering.

A constraint that shapes all three: the strongest single counterweight to the run-prompt thesis is the tenant's own behavior — American put its largest-ever Admirals Club in Terminal C, Flagship Check-In in Terminal D, and a Provisions grab-and-go in Terminal F (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-d063ac6892ae`; `contrarian::ev-e0e801801c25`; twelve corroborating records across eight agents). Any narrative that ignores this is dishonest; any narrative that surrenders to it entirely produces a report with no recommendation. Each option below metabolizes that tension differently.

---

## Option 1 — Board-ready: "The Reversibility Register"

**Thesis.** DFW should govern Terminal F's remaining design decisions through a module-by-module reversibility register — a short, priced list of options bought before each module's fabrication release, each with a named owner, a cost order of magnitude, and where possible a signed American cost-recovery mechanism.

**Communication job.** By the end, the DFW Board and CEO's office should be able to direct staff to produce and maintain the register as a standing governance artifact, because the record shows the operative design-lock dates arrive module by module and some have already passed.

**Opening scene.** The first Thursday of a month at 2400 Aviation Drive, 8:30 a.m. The Board is voting on a change order. The question the scene poses: for which Terminal F decisions does this room still hold live authority, and for which is it ratifying something a fabrication yard settled weeks ago? Nobody in the room currently has that list. The register is that list.

**Argument sequence.**

1. The frame: $1.6B/15 gates became $4B/31 gates in May 2025, with the U&L extended to 2043 (`infrastructure-economist::ev-9df5b63ec5a6`; `airline-commercial-strategist::ev-d7a668bca9ed`). The decision window runs from now to each module's release-for-fabrication date.
2. The deadline is not concrete-cure. Chief Engineer and Quantitative Analyst converge: release-for-fabrication per module is the operative lock, weeks to months earlier than pour (evidence map §6; Tension E). Six mega-modules were placed in August 2026, the largest 278×136 ft at 3,320 tons to ¾-inch tolerance (record 156).
3. What is genuinely pour-locked: column grid, floor-to-floor heights, baggage tunnel alignment, apron gate geometry (Code C/E/MARS), hydrant fuel, FIS floor plate and sterile corridors, Skylink node structure (evidence map §7; records 199–200).
4. What the register should buy: a Flagship-convertible lounge shell — structure, MEP risers, curtainwall breaks, not the lounge itself (seven agents; Quant arithmetic: a built Flagship lounge displaces 1.6–4.0 gate positions, $207–600M in foregone gate value); MARS-capable stands at a defined minority of positions (records 80–81, 203); FIS geometry sized to CBP's 100 pax/hr-per-double-booth standard (record 199) against AA's international shift to DFW (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-6f157cc0ecd1`, `ev-5b76a2fb6b0f`); Skylink-node redundancy (records 87, 161).
5. The discipline: the Contrarian's option-premium constraint. Options accumulate into shopping lists no future budget funds; each register line needs a price and, where it serves AA-branded space, a signed AA cost-recovery MOU (evidence map §5).
6. The affordability boundary: CPE $13.59 actual FY25 → $16.99 projected FY26 per the 2025 A/B Official Statement (`evidence-curator::ev-cpe-fy26-os`); debt $7.2B → $12.4B by FY29 (`infrastructure-economist::ev-85be22c1fe28`). The register is also the instrument that keeps option spending visible inside the rate base.
7. Close: under-scoping has historically cost more than over-scoping (IAD, DIA precedents; seven agents, presented as directional judgment) — but only for the specific, priced options on the register, not for speculative flexibility.

**Strongest objection and where it enters.** American's own siting decisions say Terminal F is not its premium flagship, so why buy premium options at all? It enters at beat 4, before the register's contents are listed, and is answered by the shell-not-lounge distinction plus cost-recovery conditionality — the register buys American the ability to change its mind at American's expense.

**Analogy.** A term sheet, not a blueprint: the airport is not designing American's 2035 product; it is negotiating which options survive to 2035 and who pays the premium on each.

**Signature visual.** A two-axis register chart: each Terminal F decision plotted by lock date (release-for-fabrication, per module, on a timeline through 2030) against retrofit-cost order of magnitude, with the already-passed dates shaded. One image that shows the Board what is still alive.

**Airport decision it leads toward.** Board directs staff to publish and maintain the reversibility register; CEO's office opens the AA conversation on cost-recovery MOUs for the two or three premium options; staff confirms with AA whether the U&L already obligates any Flagship-tier space before 2043 (open gap, evidence map §8).

**Risk of this frame.** It is the expected report. Eight of twelve agents landed here; a sophisticated reader senses consensus and discounts it. It also under-dramatizes the strongest material in the record — the modular-flexibility myth and the hub-terminal graveyard — by treating them as supporting beats rather than the story.

## Option 2 — Counterintuitive: "The Concrete Is a Decoy"

**Thesis.** The run's title has the deadline wrong and the industry has modular backwards: Terminal F is being locked in a fabrication yard, not on a pour schedule, post-release modular is more change-hostile than stick-built — and the cheapest hedge against 2043 is not premium provisioning but generic hub geometry, the one design choice that has historically outlived every carrier strategy.

**Communication job.** By the end, an executive who believes "modular buys flexibility" and "the decision point is the pour" should have reversed both beliefs and concluded that the highest-value design instruction is carrier-agnostic bones under carrier-specific finishes, because that is what the only surviving purpose-built hub terminal actually did.

**Opening scene.** August 2026, overnight, a two-week window: self-propelled transporters move a 3,320-ton module — 278 feet by 136 feet — into position to a ¾-inch survey tolerance (records 156, 192, 227, 243). The reader is invited to notice what this beautiful operation means: every consequential decision about that module was finalized in a factory, months ago. The concrete-cure framing everyone is using, including this run's own title, describes a deadline that already passed for part of the building.

**Argument sequence.**

1. The reveal: release-for-fabrication is the real lock, per module, weeks-to-months before pour; for some modules it is behind us (Chief Engineer, Quantitative Analyst; evidence map §6, Tension E).
2. The inversion: a late change on a conventional site costs roughly its share of scope; on a modular line it triggers resequencing across pods — post-fabrication modular is more change-hostile than stick-built. "Modular = flexible" is marketing (evidence map §6, §9; `contrarian::ev-3cd6d9a7b69b`, `ev-18d0e99795f7`). The audited fact set behind the 30% savings claim is DFW-supplied and unverified (`infrastructure-economist::ev-56a69208d46e`, caveated).
3. So what should fill the shrinking decision space? Not a bet on American's premium marketing. American's revealed posture puts Terminal F third in its own premium hierarchy (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-d063ac6892ae`); its premium history includes closing every Flagship Lounge but JFK in 2020 (`contrarian::ev-02c3980fc7b3`); Delta — the leader American is chasing (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-ed425c651135`) — has itself delayed a Delta One lounge at its ATL connecting hub (`contrarian::ev-bbfb17e8f0b1`).
4. The historical spine: of six post-1978 purpose-built hub terminals, four failed when their carrier de-hubbed — PIT (records 41, 174–177), CVG (43–44, 178–179), STL (45, 180–181), CLE (182–183). The one that absorbed a carrier strategy change without retrofit, DTW McNamara, encoded a hub form, not a Northwest form (record 184).
5. The application: generic geometry is nearly free at design time — MARS-capable apron grid, FIS sized to the CBP standard rather than to one carrier's schedule (records 199–200), lounge-capable MEP risers anyone could fit out, a Skylink node any tenant mix could load. Carrier-specific character lives in finishes, which stay cheap forever.
6. The honest concession: DFW is not PIT. American is 82.6% of passengers on top of a real two-city O&D economy (records 30, 68; evidence map §1.9 weakness), and the §365 record makes lease-rejection legally cheap for airlines but the 2043 U&L makes departure commercially remote (`contrarian::ev-80e5b480748b`; `airline-commercial-strategist::ev-854a1201ffed`). Generic geometry is not de-hub insurance; it is strategy-shift insurance — and American shifts strategy more often than it shifts hubs (9→13 banks in April 2026: `airline-commercial-strategist::ev-e4842e2ba510`).
7. Close: what would make this wrong by 2035 — A321XLR-scale point-to-point erosion of connecting share (Tension B), or an American premium build-out so specific that generic bones under-serve it. Both are named, neither is likelier than the base case.

**Strongest objection and where it enters.** "You are designing for American's departure while American signs through 2043 — that reads as bad faith toward the tenant funding the building." It enters at beat 6, deliberately late, after the graveyard evidence has made the reader want the insurance — and is answered by reframing: generic geometry protects American too, because American's own strategy at DFW changed twice in eighteen months (rebank; premium tiering).

**Analogy.** Shipbuilding, not construction: once a hull section leaves the fabrication hall, you do not move a bulkhead — you build the next ship differently. Terminal F is a fleet of hull sections on a delivery schedule, and the design authority that matters is at the yard gate, not the slipway.

**Signature visual.** A paired timeline: top band, the public story (groundbreak → concrete → 2027 opening); bottom band, the real decision calendar (release-for-fabrication dates per module, with passed dates struck through). The gap between the two bands is the argument.

**Airport decision it leads toward.** Same instrument as Option 1 — the module-by-module register — but with a different first line: instruct the program team to disclose per-module fabrication-release dates to the Board (currently a gap, evidence map §8) and to bias every still-open geometry decision toward the DTW test: would this work for a tenant we have not met?

**Risk of this frame.** It corrects the operator's own title, which is provocative in exactly the way the run prompt requests — but if the correction lands as a gotcha rather than a finding, it burns goodwill in the first 500 words. It also leans on a six-case historical sample the map itself flags as rhyme, not law; the fact-checker will hold every de-hub claim to "directional."

## Option 3 — Operational: "6:15 A.M., Single Loop"

**Thesis.** Terminal F should be designed for the specific morning it will fail — the first-wave connecting bank arriving while Skylink is still on overnight single-loop service — because the pour-locked decisions that survive that morning (Skylink node redundancy, MARS gate concurrency, FIS queue depth, checkpoint geometry) are the same ones that carry the building to 2043.

**Communication job.** By the end, capital planners and the COO's office should treat one stress scenario — morning bank, degraded Skylink, weather day — as the sizing case for every still-open Terminal F geometry decision, because the record shows the connecting bank is the metric both DFW and American must defend.

**Opening scene.** 6:15 a.m. at DFW. The first arrival bank of American's 13-bank day is on the ground. Skylink runs 22:00–06:00 on a single loop for maintenance — transit up to ~15 minutes instead of nine (records 87, 161) — and the second loop is only just resuming. Now replay January 2023: a winter storm, 1,100+ cancellations day one, 600+ day two (record 162). Put Terminal F's 31 gates and its Phase 1 Skylink station into that morning. What does the building need to already have?

**Argument sequence.**

1. Terminal F is a connecting machine before it is anything else: ~60% airport-level connecting share, contested up to roughly two-thirds carrier-level — cite both, name the denominator (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-d3646f7a1477`; evidence map Tension B).
2. American thickened the peak, it did not flatten it: 9→13 banks from April 2026, bank contiguity concentrating gate concurrency (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-e4842e2ba510`; `operations-analyst::ev-c330a5acbfca`, `ev-0f74c40d0b6e`) — while Q2 2026 departure rate improved only ~1% and DFW remained second-worst among busy US airports, with block times lengthened +6 minutes on 145 markets (`operations-analyst::ev-6ad1dbdb3363`). The rebank's benefit is claimed, not yet proven (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-c8d187d1b0ba`, AA-reported).
3. Walk the failure chain through the pour-locked geometry: Skylink node (platform edges, vertical circulation, queue depth — none confirmed in the public record; COO judgment), FIS primary queue (CBP standard: ~100 pax/hr per double booth, 50–75 ft queue; records 199–200), checkpoint room dimensions (CT lanes ~200 average/330 peak pax/hr; records 84–85), gate concurrency (MARS lets one stand serve a widebody or two narrowbody bank turns; records 80–81, 251).
4. The commissioning warning: Heathrow T5 — governance model worth copying (`infrastructure-economist::ev-70c2c7cae06f`) — opened with a 42,000-bag failure (records 132–133). LGA Terminal C is the counter-example of a single-airline delivery that worked (`infrastructure-economist::ev-3ad44367f57d`). A 2027 Phase 1 opening into a live 13-bank operation is the highest-exposure moment of the program.
5. The first-eighteen-months agenda that falls out of the stress case: turn-monitoring conduit and mounts pre-provisioned (vendor ROI figures directional only; evidence map §2.11), CUPPS-capable hardware as IROPS insurance — flagged honestly as a U&L question, not a settled scope (Tension F) — and commissioning rehearsals against the 6:15 scenario before cutover.
6. The premium tie-back: a building that protects the connection is the premium product for a hub. Delta's premium revenue crossover (`airline-commercial-strategist::ev-ed425c651135`) monetizes reliability as much as lounges; Terminal F's version of Flagship is making the 45-minute connection survivable on a bad morning.
7. Close: the same 2035 falsifiers as the thesis (connecting-share erosion; a genuine AA premium build-out), plus one specific to this frame — if the 13-bank structure is walked back, the sizing case changes, which is why the stress case should be re-run annually, not enshrined.

**Strongest objection and where it enters.** "This is an operations memo wearing a strategy report's clothes — the Board is being asked to approve geometry, not admire a scenario." It enters immediately after the opening scene and is answered by the chain in beat 3: every element of the scenario terminates in a pour-locked or fabrication-locked decision the Board still partly controls.

**Analogy.** Hospital design: you size the emergency department for Saturday night, not Tuesday afternoon, because the building's worst hour is the one that defines whether it works. Used once, at beat 3.

**Signature visual.** A single annotated cutaway of the Terminal F Skylink node and connecting spine at 6:15 a.m. under single-loop service: passenger flows, queue depths, and the four pour-locked chokepoints marked with their lock dates. The building's worst morning as one drawing.

**Airport decision it leads toward.** Adopt the degraded-morning stress case as the formal sizing scenario for all remaining Terminal F geometry decisions; direct the program team to confirm Skylink node redundancy and FIS queue depth against it before the affected modules' fabrication release.

**Risk of this frame.** It under-serves two of the run prompt's five requested areas (the premium-shift physical implications and the reversibility map become supporting cast), and its most vivid claims sit on professional judgment — the Skylink node design is publicly unconfirmed (evidence map §9: do not claim the second platform edge exists or that CUPPS is in scope). The fact-checker will require heavy "recommended, not confirmed" labeling exactly where the imagery is strongest.

**Recommendation: Option 2 — "The Concrete Is a Decoy."**

It is the only frame built on the evidence map's own highest-ranked non-obvious finding: the operative deadline is release-for-fabrication, and post-release modular is more change-hostile than stick-built. That finding corrects the run's title — which is exactly the "slightly provocative, intellectually honest" register the prompt asks for — and it converts urgency from rhetoric into dates. Option 2 also metabolizes the Provisions tension most honestly: instead of arguing past American's revealed posture (Option 1's exposure) or setting it aside (Option 3's), it accepts the signal and shows why generic geometry is the correct response to a tenant whose strategy moved twice in eighteen months. It ends at the same decision instrument as Option 1 — the module-by-module register — so no decision power is sacrificed for the surprise. Two conditions for the Strategist: keep the six-case hub-terminal history labeled as directional, per the map's §9 discipline; and carry Option 3's 6:15 a.m. scene into the body as the stress case that makes the geometry argument concrete. The frames are compatible; the spine should be Option 2's.
