## The August move

For roughly two weeks in August 2026, self-propelled transporters walked six pre-fabricated concourse modules across a closed DFW taxiway to their foundations at what will become Terminal F. The largest was 278 feet by 136 feet, weighed 3,320 tons, and was surveyed into position to a three-quarter-inch tolerance.[^1] Photographs of the move traveled well: enormous white forms on rubber tires, a lit taxiway, the airfield asleep. It is a beautiful piece of construction, and it hides the argument this report is about.

Every consequential decision about that first module was finalized months earlier, in a factory, at a design-lock date the public record does not name. The concrete-cure metaphor in this run's own title describes a deadline that had already passed for part of the building on the day the modules were set. The operative deadline at Terminal F is release-for-fabrication, per module, and DFW's board is being asked to make design decisions against a construction method whose language of flexibility is inverted from what it appears to be.

That reframing changes the whole recommendation set. Post-release modular is not more flexible than stick-built; it is more change-hostile. The tenant's own siting decisions have told the airport something about what Terminal F is expected to carry in the first years — Provisions by Admirals Club in F, the largest Admirals Club ever in Terminal C, Flagship Check-In in Terminal D.[^2] And when the historical record of purpose-built hub terminals is read completely — including the analogue the run's aviation historian ranked strongest against the Terminal F thesis — the design instruction that survives is a hybrid. Generic hub bones now. Priced, conditioned premium shells at a small number of positions, backed by a signed cost-recovery instrument. And a written register, module by module, that names what is still alive to decide — with a named executive owner, a real contract instrument, and a confirmed procurement path.

## Executive summary

1. **The deadline in the title is misleading.** The reversibility clock at Terminal F is set by release-for-fabrication, per module, weeks to months before the concrete cures. For the six modules placed in August 2026,[^1] it has already passed. Every remaining recommendation in this report is timed to the next module's fabrication release, not to a generic pour date. The register must be prospective — anchored to the first module still inside the fabrication window — not a change log for modules already committed.

2. **Post-release modular is more change-hostile than stick-built, not less.** Seven of the twelve independent research agents on this run — the Chief Engineer, Operations Analyst, Quantitative Analyst, Contrarian, Council Rams, Airport COO, and Aviation Historian — converged, from different starting points, on the same qualitative reading: once a module clears release-for-fabrication, downstream scope changes propagate across the fabrication line rather than being absorbed within a single trade. The 30% cost saving and 30% schedule saving DFW attributes to the method[^3] is a repetition dividend, contingent on identical modules and on the Innovation Next+ JV contract accommodating owner-directed template changes at repeat-part cost. There is no independent audit against a stick-built counterfactual in the public record, and every bespoke premium feature erodes the dividend. The precise change-cost multiplier is analyst judgment, not a measured figure, and this draft no longer states one.

3. **The tenant's announced posture is a strong signal, not a settled U&L instruction.** American has placed a 37,000-square-foot Admirals Club — its largest anywhere — in Terminal C near the Skylink station, a Flagship Check-In in Terminal D, and a scaled-down Provisions by Admirals Club grab-and-go in Terminal F.[^2] Twelve independent research agents corroborated the announcement against a single American press release and secondary reporting; the same records flag the Provisions designation as a live signal whose long-term U&L status is unconfirmed. The design brief should treat the announcement as the strongest available carrier signal about the first years of Terminal F, not as a permanent settlement — and should engage two alternative readings on the page (sequencing, and Terminal F as an Arrivals-premium node on a Skylink spine).

4. **American is expanding the connecting bank, not softening it — and the sizing debate about that is genuine.** The April 2026 restructure moved DFW from 9 daily banks to 13.[^4] The Airline Commercial Strategist and Operations Analyst read that as denser peaks through bank contiguity; the Airport COO reads it as a flatter, mid-day-loaded operation. Terminal F sizing hinges on which is closer to right. This draft sizes to the denser-peak reading and defends the choice: American's Q2 2026 DFW departure rate improved about 1% year over year, DFW remained second-worst among busy US airports on departure performance, and American lengthened scheduled block times by an average of six minutes across 145 network-wide markets including CLT, DCA, and MIA[^5] — a schedule surgery that mechanically improves reported on-time performance without changing airborne product. The rebank is being run, but its peak-flattening effect is claimed, not proven. Decision D4 requires the COO to publish an annual re-test that either corroborates or unwinds the denser-peak sizing.

5. **The historical record on purpose-built hub terminals is mixed, and the mixed cases matter.** Six times since deregulation, a US airport has built a purpose-designed hub terminal on a long single-carrier commitment. Four were dehubbed a decade to fifteen years after opening — Pittsburgh 1992 dehubbed 2004,[^6] Cincinnati peaked in 2005 and unwound through 2013,[^7] St. Louis peaked in 2001 and cut in half by 2003,[^8] Cleveland's newer concourse went dark in 2014.[^9] Detroit McNamara (2002) aged well because it encoded a hub *form* rather than a Northwest form.[^25] JetBlue's Terminal 5 at JFK (2008) is the harder case: an $875M terminal that has required a $200M international-arrivals extension in 2014 and a further ~$100M premium refresh in 2025–26 — post-opening retrofit spend approaching one-third of original construction cost, because premium and international optionality were not priced into the original scope.[^34] Atlanta's Maynard Jackson / Concourse F (2012, $1.4B) is the counter-counterexample: a purpose-built premium international floor plate at a mega-connecting hub that has functioned as designed.[^35] Read together, the record argues for hub-generic bones *plus* priced, conditioned premium shells — not for either alone.

6. **The pour-locked decisions with the largest downstream cost of being wrong are geometric, not aesthetic.** MARS-capable stands at a defined minority of positions,[^10] a Federal Inspection Services floor plate sized to CBP's approximately 100 passengers per hour per double primary booth with 50–75 foot queue depth,[^11] checkpoint room dimensions built to next-generation CT throughput geometry rather than 2015-era bins-and-belts,[^12] Skylink-node redundancy against a 6:15 a.m. single-loop failure scenario,[^13] baggage-handling trunk cross-section, and a lounge-capable shell of structure, mechanical, and curtain-wall provision. None are Flagship-tier build-outs. All carry order-of-magnitude cost premiums the register must publish per line — the run's Quantitative Analyst has not yet delivered them, and the register cannot go to the Finance and Audit Committee without them.

7. **The airport's affordability arbitrage is being spent during the ramp.** DFW's cost per enplanement was $13.59 in FY25 and is projected at $16.99 in FY26 in the 2025 A/B bond Official Statement, as aggregated by DWU Consulting; primary-document verification is pending fact-check.[^14] Outstanding debt was projected to grow from $7.2B in 2024 to $12.4B by FY29 as of the August 2024 rating action — nine months before Terminal F was expanded from $1.6B/15 gates to $4B/31 gates; the post-expansion figure has not been published and is higher.[^15] DFW remains well below JFK, LAX, EWR, and ORD on CPE.[^16] The Airline Commercial Strategist's professional judgment is that connecting-hub CPE crossing the low $20s begins to change carrier behavior; the ledger does not contain a public CPE projection for FY27–FY30. The register requires a Finance and Audit Committee-set projected-CPE ceiling that the aggregate register-driven option premium must live inside. The number belongs to the Committee; the discipline of the ceiling belongs to this report.

8. **The most valuable governance artifact this run can produce is a written module-by-module reversibility register, executed inside real contract instruments.** Not a "flexibility strategy." A short, priced list of options bought before each module's fabrication release, each with a named VP-level owner, an order-of-magnitude cost, a named funding source, named federal dependencies, and where the space serves American, a Terminal F Reversibility Side Letter to the 2025 U&L amendment with a schedule of pre-priced options. It survives board turnover; it turns option-preservation from rhetoric into a specific act of governance; and it enforces the discipline that generic-geometry design is not a shopping list. Five decisions in this report, addressed to five chairs, put the register on a real footing. A sixth — asking the Board to adopt three specific actions at the next two meetings — closes the loop.

The rest of this report defends those eight claims, sets them against the strongest counter-case a sophisticated reader can build, and closes on the specific decisions that fall out for DFW's board and CEO's office during the remaining fabrication-release window through 2030.

## The argument

### The deadline is not the pour

The May 2025 announcement moved Terminal F from a $1.6 billion, 15-gate facility to a $4 billion, 31-gate program and extended American's Use and Lease Agreement from 2033 to 2043.[^17] The public timeline says Phase 1 opens in 2027 and the full program completes in 2030. The reversibility conversation inside the airport, and inside every trade-press article about the project, uses the same language the run prompt uses: before the concrete cures.

That language is technically wrong for a modular build. The concrete does not cure once. Each module's structure, curtain wall, mechanical risers, primary electrical service, and interior partitions are frozen in a factory, weeks to months before the module is transported and set. Once cutting, welding, and MEP first-fit begin on that module, its dimensional identity is committed. Trying to change a column grid, floor-to-floor height, or riser location at that point does not cost the marginal price of the change; it triggers resequencing across every module downstream on the fabrication line. DFW should be asked to publish the per-module release-for-fabrication milestones so the board can see which decisions are still live and which have quietly closed. That request alone is worth the run.

The inversion matters because it changes what "modular flexibility" means. Marketing conflates the roughly 30% cost saving and 30% schedule saving DFW and its trade-press coverage attribute to the method[^3] with an unspecified third benefit called flexibility. There is no third benefit. The savings are a repetition dividend. They exist because the factory is running the same module over and over with the same MEP tree and the same wall thicknesses. Every bespoke premium feature — a double-height lounge volume above a gate hold, a specialty kitchen exhaust riser, a curtain-wall break for a Flagship arrival vestibule — either forces an off-standard module that does not clear the factory at repeat-part cost, or forces the base module to carry provisions the majority of positions do not need. In both directions, the customization tax bends the program cost curve the wrong way.

The dividend is also contingent on the contract. The Innovation Next+ JV is delivering Terminal C's nine-gate pier, Terminal A's renewal, and Terminal F concurrently. Craft labor for MEP first-fit inside modules, SPMT operators and airside windows, crane-and-erection windows, and the JV's yard capacity are shared across three programs. Every template change registered against Terminal F competes for the same fabrication line, and the register's cadence depends on whether the JV's contract type permits owner-directed template modifications at repeat-part cost or triggers a re-price cycle that erodes the dividend the draft cites in the same sentence. That is a procurement question — Decision D0 in the recommendation set — and it must be answered before the register's first module cycle begins.

Seven of the twelve independent research agents on this run — the Chief Engineer, Operations Analyst, Quantitative Analyst, Contrarian, Council Rams, Airport COO, and Aviation Historian — converged on the modular change-hostility reading, even where their overall spending conclusions diverged. (The Operations Analyst, notably, uses the same finding to argue the *opposite* recommendation: that the modular arbitrage collapses the flex premium and DFW should spend it. That is a live disagreement inside the swarm and it is a fair reading.) This is the finding downstream of which the reversibility register is not optional.

### What the tenant has said, and what it has not

In July 2026, American announced its "next-generation premium experience at DFW." Three anchors. A 37,000-square-foot Admirals Club — the airline's largest ever — in Terminal C near the Skylink station. A Flagship Check-In near the D30 checkpoint in Terminal D, opening later in 2026, with a separate entrance and dedicated processing for Flagship First, Flagship Business, and eligible AAdvantage elites. And in Terminal F, a Provisions by Admirals Club grab-and-go — explicitly a scaled-down concept, not a Flagship Lounge and not a full Admirals Club.[^2]

The announcement is, on the ledger, the single most-corroborated fact in this run: twelve independent research agents each independently found the Provisions posture, and twelve records across eight of them corroborated it against the same American joint press release and secondary reporting. That is convergence on one primary source, not twelve independent observations. And the interpretation — what the announcement means for F's design brief — is the sharpest disagreement inside the swarm.

Three readings deserve to be on the page.

**Reading one: revealed preference.** The tenant with the most information about its own 2043 network has spent its premium capital at C and D and told the airport, for now, what F is for. F is a bank-processing floor. It is the widebody feeder and the pressure valve on the 13-bank restructure.

**Reading two: sequencing.** Terminal F does not open until 2027. The July 2026 announcement is the only place American *could* site premium capital in the near term, because F does not physically exist yet. The Provisions designation is equally consistent with sequencing as with tiering. The 2032 announcement could add a Flagship-tier build at F.

**Reading three: Arrivals-premium.** The Virtual Chris brief, corroborated inside the evidence map, argues that Skylink is becoming American's premium spine at DFW, and that F's premium role may be Arrivals rather than Departures — the transatlantic 787-9P passenger deplanes into a Flagship Arrivals suite in F, then Skylinks to Terminal C to connect or to Terminal D to continue. Under that reading, the Terminal C club siting says nothing about F's premium ceiling. It says something about the shape of the network.

This draft treats reading one as the strongest available *near-term* signal from the tenant with the most information, and reading three as the option the register should quietly preserve at low marginal cost. Reading two — the sequencing reading — is the reason none of the register's premium items should be conditioned on American's July 2026 posture holding through 2043. The register is the instrument that lets American change its mind at American's expense across any of the three readings.

American's own record supports the discipline. In March 2020, at the onset of the pandemic, the airline closed every Flagship Lounge except JFK — LAX, DFW, MIA, ORD — and did not begin reopening the network until fall 2021.[^18] A design bet that has to survive to 2043 needs to survive several plausible repetitions of that cycle. Delta, the acknowledged premium leader — whose Q4 2025 premium ticket revenue crossed main cabin for the first time in the company's history,[^19] and whose FY2025 premium totaled $22.1 billion — has publicly targeted a 2028 opening for the ATL Delta One Lounge; industry observers attribute the delayed timing to the difficulty of premium execution at a mega-connecting hub, though Delta itself has stated only the target date.[^20] The ATL status as of August 2026 should be verified before this document reaches the board.

None of this argues against premium as a fleet strategy. American's target of narrowbody premium share moving from ~25% to ~40% and international lie-flat count growing more than 50% by end of decade[^21] is real and executable in the cabins. The airline is also visibly shifting long-haul weight into DFW — a 6% year-over-year DFW capacity expansion in Q3 2026 alongside a 13% reduction at London Heathrow, plus six new international routes announced for summer 2026.[^22] The premium repositioning is a fleet and network story that is executable inside cabins the airport does not build. Its physical anchor at Terminal F, on American's July 2026 public record, is a grab-and-go and a widebody-feeder concourse; the design brief should absorb that signal without foreclosing the other two readings.

### Bank density, not bank flatness — a defended choice, not a physics claim

The other half of what American has told the airport is that Terminal F should be scoped against a denser connecting bank, not a flatter one — and this is where the design decision has to be *made*, not assumed. The December 2025 restructure moved DFW from 9 daily banks to 13, effective April 2026.[^4]

Inside this run, that fact carries two competing readings. The Airport COO reads AA's 13-bank messaging as a *flatter*, mid-day-loaded operation — banks spread across a longer operating window, peak concurrency reduced. The Airline Commercial Strategist and Operations Analyst read it the opposite way: bank contiguity — banks overlapping in time — amplifies gate concurrency at peaks. Terminal F sizing depends on which is correct, and the evidence map calls out the tension.

This draft adopts the denser-peak reading. The defensible grounds: American reported a 50% reduction in missed connections two weeks after launch, a self-reported figure from a two-week window; the Q2 2026 audit is more sober. American's Q2 2026 DFW departure rate rose about 1% year over year, DFW remained second-worst among busy US airports on departure performance, and American lengthened scheduled block times by an average of six minutes across 145 markets network-wide including CLT, DCA, and MIA — a schedule surgery that improves reported on-time performance without changing airborne product.[^5] Read together, that is not the operational signature of a flattened peak. It is the signature of a hub whose peak-day arithmetic is still demanding.

The operational read is not that the rebank failed. It is that its peak-flattening benefit is claimed, not proven, and that Terminal F should not be sized on the assumption that Forever Forward flattens the peak. The building has to serve a bank operation carrying roughly 100,000 peak-day American customers on approximately 930 peak-day departures[^23] — with the connecting share contested between about 60% airport-level and roughly two-thirds carrier-level. The airport should cite both numbers, name the denominator each time, and size the pour-locked geometry against the higher connecting share, because the connecting geometry is what fails on the bad morning. If the COO's flatter-profile reading proves correct, the sizing case is over-conservative and some register items become over-provision; the annual re-test in Decision D4 is the mechanism that catches the mistake and unwinds it.

### The 6:15 a.m. sizing case — a constructed stress scenario tabletopped with named partners

Here is the bad morning. Skylink runs on a two-minute headway with a nine-minute maximum interterminal transit during the day. Overnight, from 22:00 to 06:00, one loop shuts down for maintenance; transit on the single loop doubles to roughly fifteen minutes.[^13] Load onto that operating state an early-morning arrival bank from American's 13-bank day and the January 2023 winter storm that produced a DFW ground stop with 1,100+ cancellations on the peak day and 600+ the next.[^24] Put Terminal F's 31 gates and its Phase 1 Skylink station into that morning.

That is a *constructed* stress case, offered by the Airport COO. The precise timing — an American first arrival bank at 6:15, the single-loop transition — is a professional judgment, not a documented daily event. The register should adopt it as a formal sizing scenario for exactly that reason: sizing to a documented weekday morning is sizing to a system that already runs; sizing to a plausible bad morning is what the pour-locked decisions actually need to survive.

The scenario has a geometry test and an operating test. The geometry test terminates in decisions the register must lock: the Terminal F Skylink station's platform width, vertical circulation core count, and queuing depth on the concourse side determine whether the building can absorb a 6:15 loop failure without backing passengers into the security checkpoint. The FIS primary hall is sized against CBP's approximately 100 passengers per hour per double booth and 50–75 foot queue standard[^11] — get the geometry wrong and the airport buys it back with CBP staffing overtime and connect-time penalties for the life of the building. Checkpoint room dimensions must be built to next-generation CT lane geometry — 200 passengers per hour on average, 330 at peak against DHS's 300-per-lane target[^12] — because roller conveyor run length, secondary search depth, and ceiling height are pour-locked; casework and divest lanes are not. Gate concurrency across a bank is determined by whether stands are Code C, Code E, or MARS-capable; MARS lets one stand serve either a widebody or two narrowbody bank turns,[^10] and every hydrant pit, PCA/400 Hz sizing, and jetbridge foundation is set at the apron pour.

The operating test is a tabletop, not a design memo. The 6:15 scenario has to be run against a staffing model — DFW Guest Experience, ARFF, airport police, AA station operations, TSA Federal Security Director, CBP Port Director, and Skylink operations at the same table — because sizing the FIS to CBP standard is necessary but not sufficient. If CBP will not staff the sized hall under the run's Reimbursable Services Program status, the shell is a moot preservation. If TSA will not concur on the CT-lane geometry, the checkpoint room is a design intent without a permit. Both outcomes belong in Decision D4, not in a footnote.

The tabletop also resolves a design decision the v2 draft dropped. Common Use Passenger Processing System (CUPPS)-capable gate hardware, on a single-occupant Terminal F, is a Contrarian-vs-COO disagreement: CUPPS is an option premium at fabrication that pays back only if American ever bleeds passengers to another terminal or a future carrier occupies gates at F. This draft picks the COO's side — CUPPS-capable hardware belongs on the register as a low-marginal-cost preservation, because on a bad morning the alternative is a manual reassignment protocol at 6:15 a.m. across an exclusive-use envelope — but flags the disposition explicitly rather than leaving it unresolved.

The 6:15 case is not a resilience bullet. It is the sizing scenario that quietly disciplines every remaining geometry decision at Terminal F. Adopting it as the formal sizing case is a governance act, not an engineering one — it forces the design team to demonstrate, before each module's fabrication release, that the still-open geometry decisions survive the morning the building is worst, and that named federal partners have signed off on the operating model that surrounds them.

### The historical spine, honestly counted

Purpose-built hub terminals on long single-carrier commitments are the design bet Terminal F is running through. Since deregulation, that bet has been made roughly six times at American-scale hubs. The record is mixed and the mixed cases are the ones that matter.

**Four ended badly.** Pittsburgh's 1992 X-shaped Midfield Terminal — approximately $1 billion, US Airways at 80%+ share, twenty-five-year Use and Lease — was dehubbed in 2004 over a fee dispute the airport could not resolve; the stranded debt was carried until roughly 2019, and CPE nearly doubled by 2011.[^6] Cincinnati went from 22.7 million passengers and 600+ daily flights in 2005 to fewer than 6 million and under 180 daily by 2013.[^7] St. Louis fell from 500+ daily American/TWA flights in July 2001 to 207 by 2003 after American acquired TWA and the ORD slot restriction lifted.[^8] Cleveland's Concourse D — the newer hub concourse — went dark in May 2014 when United announced the dehub; destinations dropped from 61 to 20.[^9] The dehub-to-close intervals were roughly 12 years (PIT), 11–19 years (CVG), 2–5 years (STL), and 15 years (CLE). "Within a decade to fifteen years" is the honest characterization; the v1 phrase "within a business cycle" was true only of St. Louis and is corrected here.

**One aged well on hub-generic geometry.** Detroit McNamara, opened February 2002 for Northwest at roughly $1.2 billion, absorbed the 2008 Delta–Northwest merger without material retrofit.[^25] SmithGroup had designed a linear midfield with satellite concourses — a hub *form*, not a Northwest form. Any hub carrier's connecting bank could load it. The design decision that made it survive was one that cost nothing at design time and everything to retrofit later: generic geometry inside carrier-specific finishes.

**And two cases the v1 draft dropped, one of which cuts directly against the shell-preservation instinct.** JetBlue's Terminal 5 at JFK opened in 2008 at approximately $875 million as a purpose-built domestic terminal, and has since required a $200 million international arrivals concourse (T5i) in 2014 and a further approximately $100 million premium refresh in 2025–26 — total post-opening retrofit spend approaching one-third of original construction cost, because premium and international optionality were not priced into the original scope.[^34] The aviation historian carrying this run called T5 "the strongest available analogue to the Terminal F decision." Read straight, T5 argues *against* deferral: pricing premium and international optionality into the 2027 scope is dramatically cheaper than pricing it into 2033 retrofit. Atlanta's Maynard Jackson / Concourse F (2012, approximately $1.4 billion) is the counter-counter: a purpose-built premium international floor plate at a mega-connecting hub that has functioned as designed and did not require the T5-style retrofit sequence.[^35]

The T5 case is where the v1 draft's spine had to bend. The recommendation this draft lands on — hub-generic bones *plus* a small number of priced, conditioned premium shells at head-of-pier positions — is the recommendation that survives T5, DTW, and PIT together. T5's lesson is that a purpose-built terminal that scoped no premium optionality paid for that decision inside six years. DTW's lesson is that the shells that survived were generic. ATL Concourse F's lesson is that a purpose-built premium floor plate at a connecting hub is achievable when it is scoped as such from day one. The register absorbs all three by pricing a small set of premium-convertible shells at fabrication release, conditioned on signed cost recovery, and refusing everything else.

The sample is six, which is why the aviation historian carrying this evidence marked it directional, not diagnostic. DFW is not Pittsburgh: American carried 82.6% of DFW's passengers in 2025 on top of a real Dallas–Fort Worth O&D economy of about 8 million people,[^26] the Use and Lease runs to 2043, and the §365 bankruptcy record shows American assumed 554 unexpired real-property leases in the 2011 filing against 12 rejected — legally cheap to walk from, commercially rare to actually do.[^27] The historical warning applies most sharply to the shape of the building, not the survival of the hub.

But it applies. Terminal F is being built at the moment American moved on hub structure and premium siting inside eight months — the 9-to-13-bank restructure announced December 2025 and the Terminal C / D / F premium tiering announced July 2026. Whether those are two strategy shifts or two execution moves inside one continuous strategy is a reasonable reader question; the fair reading is that the underlying commitment to DFW is deep and the specific shape of the operation is still being iterated. The design has to be legible to whatever version of American runs Terminal F in 2035 without a retrofit. DTW McNamara's discipline is available at essentially zero incremental cost, if the airport takes it now. JFK T5's warning is available at a small option premium, if the airport takes it now. The register is what makes the difference between preserving the option and paying to build it.

### What generic geometry actually means, with per-item costs the register must publish

Generic geometry, honestly priced, is a short list. Each item below is an analyst-constructed order-of-magnitude range — components sourced, arithmetic disclosed — that the Quantitative Analyst must confirm before the register goes to the Finance and Audit Committee. Not audit-grade; register-grade.

- **MARS-capable stands at a defined minority of widebody-reachable positions.** Fuel pit, PCA and 400 Hz risers, jetbridge foundation, apron lead-in geometry, all set at the apron pour under FAA AC 150/5300-13B.[^28] Analyst-judgment premium on MARS provisioning versus a Code C-only stand: single-digit percent of the per-stand base scope, per position, if scoped at pour. Full retrofit if omitted: multiples. Federal dependency: FAA modification-of-standard letter where MARS geometry departs from the AC.
- **FIS floor plate and sterile-corridor width sized to CBP's Airport Technical Design Standard, not to a single carrier's 2027 international schedule.** American's redeployment of long-haul flying into DFW[^22] makes the FIS the single pour-locked decision with the largest 2043 international-capacity consequence. Analyst-judgment premium on right-sized FIS versus opening-year FIS: low tens of millions inside a $4B program; retrofit cost order-of-magnitude the ATL Concourse F record supports as an order of magnitude higher. Federal dependency: CBP Reimbursable Services Program agreement or CBP staffing memo before the sized throughput is operable — a two-year negotiation, not a design directive.
- **Baggage-handling trunk cross-section sized against a peak connecting bank at 2035 volumes, not opening-year volumes.** Analyst judgment: BHS oversizing at pour is a single-digit percent of BHS scope; retrofit is disruptive to concourse operations and materially higher.
- **Vertical MEP risers, grease-waste rough-ins, and structural allowance for a Flagship-tier lounge shell** — 25,000 to 40,000 square feet of preserved envelope with the plumbing and load path a future kitchen would need — deliberately not built out. Analyst-judgment premium on shell preservation at fabrication versus retrofit into an operating concourse: order-of-magnitude cheaper at fabrication. Airline dependency: Terminal F Reversibility Side Letter with a schedule of pre-priced options; without it, this is airport-side preload of tenant-improvement scope and the ratepayer carries it.
- **Skylink-node platform width, vertical circulation core count, and queuing depth sized for the 6:15 stress case.** Inside base Terminal F scope; the marginal cost is design-time, not construction-time.
- **Checkpoint room dimensions built for next-generation CT geometry.** Inside base scope; TSA concurrence dependency on the room layout is on the critical path.
- **Dense sensor conduit, PoE++ ceiling drops, fiber diversity out of the terminal, camera mounts and data drops at every apron corner and jetbridge tunnel** — a common instrumentation baseline shipped through the module template. Analyst judgment on template modification premium: modest, if the Innovation Next+ JV contract accommodates owner-directed template changes at repeat-part cost (Decision D0). Materially higher if it does not.
- **CUPPS-capable gate hardware.** Contrarian-vs-COO disagreement resolved in favor of the COO: preserved on the register as low-marginal-cost hardware whose absence is felt on the bad morning. Airline dependency: American IT participation in factory witness testing.

None of the above is a Flagship Lounge. None is a bet on American's 2033 premium build-out. All work for a tenant DFW has not met. The airport pays an option premium now — most of it inside the module template, priced at repeat-part cost if the JV contract permits it — and preserves the ability for American, or a future signatory, to fit out any of those envelopes at the tenant's expense on the tenant's schedule.

Where the space serves an American-branded product, the option must be conditioned on a Terminal F Reversibility Side Letter to the 2025 U&L amendment before the module locks. That instrument — not a Chief Development Officer memo countersigned by an SVP of Hub Operations — is what turns the register's premium items from preserved capacity DFW's rate base carries into pre-priced options American exercises at American's expense. The Contrarian's discipline made operational: three or four premium-conditioned shells with a countersigned schedule of options, not forty.

## The counter-case, honestly presented

The strongest reader in the room will not concede any of this without a fight. Four objections deserve a full hearing.

**American signed to 2043. Designing for American's departure is bad faith toward the tenant funding the building.** The 2025 Use and Lease Agreement extension gave DFW 18 years of scheduling certainty and made the $4 billion Terminal F debt stack politically survivable inside the airline relationship. The 82.6% concentration that reads as fragility on a Fitch or Moody's methodology sheet also reads as unusually deep alignment between airport and carrier through 2043. American is investing in premium at Terminal C and D with its own capital, running a rebank designed to strengthen the DFW hub product, and shifting international capacity from Heathrow into DFW. Every part of the tenant's public 2026 posture says the hub is being reinforced, not exited. A design report whose spine reads as "encode generic geometry so a future tenant can load it" is, without care, heard as an insurance policy against a partner who is signing checks. That is the political register the CEO's office will have to answer to at the first Fort Worth mayoral event — and the four Fort Worth-appointed board seats, whose leverage grows as the 2043 renewal approaches, will be watching.

**The counterparty for premium is the airline, not the airport.** The industry finance model puts premium features on the carrier's side of the line. Delta's JFK T4 Delta One Lounge, at roughly 40,000 square feet and 515 seats, is a carrier-funded fit-out on Port Authority base building.[^29] American funded its own Terminal C Admirals Club and Terminal D Flagship Check-In. If premium at F ever arrives, the industry-standard vehicle for it is a tenant-improvement program on the airline's balance sheet, on the airline's timing, at the airline's risk. Airport-side preload of "premium-ready bones" inverts the customary allocation. It transfers strategy risk from the party that has the information — the carrier — to the party that does not.

**The reversibility register is a false comfort — options accumulate into a shopping list no future budget funds.** Every preserved option carries an option premium. MEP oversizing on hundreds of gate positions, structural allowance for lounges that never open, curtain-wall breaks for arrival flows the CBP designation never authorizes. In the scenario where a 2032 CEO looks at 40 preserved options and executes six, the airport paid the premium on 34 that never mattered. The Contrarian's version of this argument is not that the register is wrong; it is that the register should have three or four lines, each with a signed AA cost-recovery schedule inside a Side Letter, and refuse everything else. A register of forty items is a shopping list. A register of three, priced and countersigned, is discipline.

**The affordability arbitrage is spending down under the airport's feet.** Cost per enplanement moved from $13.59 in FY25 actual to $16.99 in FY26 projected in the 2025 A/B bond Official Statement, as aggregated by DWU Consulting — a roughly 25% single-year jump, with primary-document verification pending.[^14] Outstanding debt was projected to grow from $7.2 billion in 2024 to $12.4 billion by FY29 as of the August 2024 rating action, before the May 2025 Terminal F scope expansion added $2.4 billion; the post-expansion figure has not been published and is higher.[^15] The 70th Supplemental Bond Ordinance authorizes up to $3.0 billion in new debt between March 1, 2025 and February 28, 2026, and the next new-money authorization requires both Dallas and Fort Worth city-council approval on a calendar the airport does not control. Every dollar of preserved option is a dollar not amortized over gate revenue, and every basis-point ratings slip on the next issuance is a real lifetime interest event that a modeled scenario should quantify before it is asserted. The Airline Commercial Strategist's professional judgment is that airline signatories start changing behavior when connecting-hub CPE crosses the low $20s. No public projection places DFW inside that band during the ramp; the trajectory points toward it, and that is the honest characterization.

These are the four objections. Each is real. Each survives.

## Why the counter-case is insufficient

They survive as constraints on the recommendation. They do not defeat it.

The bad-faith framing misreads what generic geometry is insurance against. It is not de-hub insurance — the historical warning applies most sharply to the shape of the building, not the survival of the hub. It is strategy-shift insurance. American moved on hub structure and premium siting inside eight months: the 9-to-13-bank restructure and the Terminal C / D / F premium tiering. Whether those are two strategy shifts or one strategy being iterated, the design has to be legible to whichever version of American runs Terminal F in 2035. Generic geometry protects the tenant, too, because the tenant's own next iteration — a further rebank, an international pivot, a merger, a Flagship Arrivals product that does not exist yet — lives inside the same envelope. Framed correctly, the register is the instrument that lets American change its mind at American's expense. That is not bad faith. That is exactly what a well-drafted Side Letter to the 2025 U&L amendment looks like. Reading the register's ownership and cadence to the Fort Worth-appointed board seats — with Fort Worth-facing landside, DBE participation, and cargo-side apron geometry where a MARS stand doubles as a widebody freighter position on the register — is the political discipline that keeps the register from being heard as a Dallas-side memorandum.

The industry-finance objection is why the register's premium lines must be conditioned on the Side Letter before the module locks. Do not preload airline-funded space on the airport's balance sheet. Do preserve the shell — structural loading, MEP risers, curtain-wall breaks, sterile-corridor and floor-plate dimensions — because the shell is the only piece that has to be decided at fabrication release. The fit-out is the airline's line item, on the airline's schedule. The T5 counterexample sharpens the point rather than defeating it: JetBlue paid three-hundredths of the original construction cost, spread across sixteen years of retrofits, precisely because the original scope did not carry the shells. DFW should carry the shells and let the fit-outs remain American's.

The shopping-list objection is the discipline the register enforces, not the argument against it. A reversibility register with forty items is a document that fails the Contrarian's test. A register with a small, priced set of items — MARS-capable positions at a defined minority of the widebody-reachable stands; FIS geometry sized to CBP standard; a Flagship-convertible shell at a small number of head-of-pier positions with a countersigned schedule inside the Side Letter; the Skylink node built for the 6:15 case; CUPPS-capable gate hardware; the sensor-conduit and data-plane discipline that ships the concourse against a common instrumentation baseline — is the register that survives value engineering, board turnover, and the ratings review. Publish it with per-line prices and per-line funding sources. Refuse everything else. The register is not a wish list; it is a stop rule for the wish list.

The affordability objection is why every option needs a number, why the Finance and Audit Committee must set a projected-CPE ceiling the aggregate register premium lives inside, and why the CEO's office must route the premium-adjacent options through American under Side Letter language that keeps them off the rate base absent a triggering event. It is also why under-scoping is often the more expensive error than over-scoping — a professional judgment held by seven of the twelve independent research agents on this run (the Chief Engineer, Operations Analyst, Quantitative Analyst, Contrarian, Council Rams, Airport COO, and Aviation Historian), anchored in the corrected IAD AeroTrain record — a 2000s-era routing decision that omitted the Concourse D stop, ran 16 years on mobile lounges, and is now folded into an approximately $3.75 billion AeroTrain extension and underground tunnel inside a $22.5 billion IAD renovation program,[^30] Denver's Great Hall termination with $184 million paid to end the P3 and $2.1 billion to complete under a new contract,[^31] and Heathrow T5's £4.3 billion on-time, on-budget delivery that opened with a 42,000-bag failure the airport had not commissioned against.[^32] The historical pattern-match is that terminal-scale programs that under-scoped paid multiples in mid-lease retrofit; the LGA Terminal C counter-example — Delta's $4 billion single-airline delivery completed two years ahead of schedule[^33] — shows what disciplined single-airline governance can produce when the airline treats the delivery model as a partnership. The affordability constraint does not repeal these lessons. It sharpens them.

Where the counter-case wins outright is on scale. The register should be short. The premium options should be conditioned. The affordability envelope should govern which shells get preserved and which do not. But the recommendation itself — generic hub geometry, pour-locked chokepoints sized against the 6:15 case, a common instrumentation baseline fixed at the module template, a small priced set of conditioned premium shells inside a Side Letter — is the recommendation that survives the four objections most cleanly. It is also the recommendation the tenant's own signals (read across all three interpretations on the page), the modular method's own arithmetic, and the historical record on this exact building type (including its hardest counterexample) each independently point toward.

## Implications for the operator

Six decisions fall out of the argument, addressed to specific chairs and sequenced by the answers each requires from the ones above it. All should land inside the fabrication-release window that runs from now through 2030.

**Decision D0 — Confirm the procurement path for template-level changes before any register cycle begins.** *Owner:* Chief Procurement Officer, with the Chief Engineer. *Approval route:* internal disclosure to the Board's Finance and Audit Committee. *First 30-day action:* confirm in writing that the Innovation Next+ JV design-build contract (Archer Western–Turner–Phillips May–H.J. Russell) permits owner-directed template-level changes at repeat-part cost within the current change-order envelope; identify the earliest module release for which template changes can still be absorbed under the base contract without triggering a re-price cycle; confirm that the JV's fabrication cadence can absorb template changes without slipping Terminal C or Terminal A schedules by more than a defined threshold. *Cost order of magnitude:* nominal — procurement staff time. *Funding source:* operating budget. *Success measure:* written confirmation, or a written scoping change that acquires the right, before Decision D2 opens. *Failure mode:* Decisions D2 and D5 proceed on a procurement assumption the contract does not support and every template change becomes a re-price event. *Stop condition:* if the JV contract cannot accommodate template changes at repeat-part cost, Decision D5 phases the template rollout to modules the JV can absorb and Decision D2 restricts the register's per-module cadence accordingly. *What evidence would change the recommendation:* a change in the JV contract or a competing modular-manufacturer bid.

**Decision D1 — Close the two contract questions the design cannot answer, on a compressed 30-day clock.** *Owner:* General Counsel, with the airline-affairs office. *Approval route:* internal disclosure to the Board's Finance and Audit Committee before the next module's fabrication release. *First 30-day action:* obtain from American a written statement of (a) whether the current Use and Lease amendment obligates a Flagship-tier lounge at Terminal F at any point before 2043 and (b) the MII trigger thresholds for capital additions inside the exclusive-use envelope. The consent architecture for scope changes inside American's exclusive-use terminal is, in practice, unavoidable given its activity share; the actual thresholds are confidential to the U&L and are load-bearing on whether the register's premium items are design questions or contract questions. *Cost order of magnitude:* nominal. *Funding source:* operating budget. *Success measure:* the Board sees both documents before the next module's fabrication release. *Failure mode:* the register makes recommendations that assume contract facts not in evidence. *Stop condition:* if American declines to state, escalate to CEO-to-CEO airline-affairs and treat the register's premium items as conservative design questions bounded by the highest-consent MII assumption. *What evidence would change the recommendation:* the answers themselves.

**Decision D2 — Publish the module-by-module reversibility register as a standing governance artifact, prospectively, against the first module still inside the fabrication window.** *Owner:* Vice President Terminal Development (or the DFW org-chart equivalent — a VP-level role, not the CEO's office; the register requires assigned program-manager staffing and a design-build JV liaison as a permanent charge). *Approval route:* adoption as a recurring standing item on the Board's Operations, Safety and Security Committee; escalation to the full Board on material changes. *First 90-day action, in two phases:* Days 0–30, obtain from the Innovation Next+ JV the per-module release-for-fabrication schedule as contract disclosure — not a request; Days 30–90, name the next module inside the fabrication window, publish its pour-locked and still-open decision list with per-item cost order-of-magnitude and per-item funding source (current 70th Supplemental Bond Ordinance where inside envelope; future supplemental requiring Dallas and Fort Worth city-council approval where not; American cost-recovery under the Side Letter for premium-adjacent items; PFC unavailable given the $4.50 cap), and hold the first Committee briefing against that specific module. *Cost order of magnitude:* register staffing $250K–$500K annual loaded cost (analyst estimate: one program manager plus fractional JV-liaison time); register-driven option premiums per Decision D3. *Funding source:* staffing from operating budget; option premiums per line. *Success measure:* every fabrication release preceded by a written finding against the register; percentage of releases preceded by a finding is a leading indicator reported quarterly. *Failure mode:* register becomes a decoration on the strategy deck; the draft's own remedy — retire the register if three modules ship without consultation — is a resignation, not a stop condition, and is replaced here. *Stop condition:* if a formal American Flagship build-out at Terminal F is announced on a schedule the airport can see, or if AA connecting share erodes below 55% at DFW across two consecutive full-year cycles, or if U&L renewal negotiations open earlier than 2043, the register unwinds on a route the CEO's office publishes with the announcement. *What evidence would change the recommendation:* publication of MII trigger thresholds (Decision D1); primary-document CPE projection through FY30; American's written statement on the Flagship-at-F question.

**Decision D3 — Route the premium-adjacent options through American under a Terminal F Reversibility Side Letter, with a Finance and Audit Committee-set projected-CPE ceiling.** *Owner:* Chief Development Officer, with General Counsel. *Approval route:* Board memorandum delegating Side Letter execution to the Finance and Audit Committee; American internal capital committee (SVP DFW Hub Operations plus the American CFO office, not SVP Hub Ops alone). *First 90-day action:* draft a Terminal F Reversibility Side Letter to the 2025 U&L amendment carrying a schedule of pre-priced options — Flagship-convertible lounge shells at defined head-of-pier positions (structure, MEP risers, curtain-wall breaks, back-of-house egress path); Flagship Arrivals suite provision at up to two international-capable positions on the Skylink spine; CUPPS-capable gate hardware where the exclusive-use envelope permits — with the fit-out contingent on American's countersigned exercise. Refresh annually against U&L amendment cycle. Set, with the Finance and Audit Committee, a projected-CPE ceiling that the aggregate register-driven option premium may not raise projected CPE above in any FY through FY30. *Cost order of magnitude:* legal and airline-affairs staff time to draft; option premiums per line, published in the register. *Funding source:* American cost recovery for premium shells inside the Side Letter; DFW residual for generic geometry against the CPE ceiling. *Success measure:* three or fewer premium-conditioned shells, each with a countersigned option memo, on the register at each fabrication release; aggregate register premium inside the CPE ceiling at each Committee report. *Failure mode:* shells accumulate without American's sign-off and become preserved capacity DFW's rate base carries. *Stop condition:* drop from register after two fabrication cycles without American countersign; unwind aggregate register premium if it breaches the CPE ceiling in the next-cycle forecast. *What evidence would change the recommendation:* American's written Flagship-at-F statement (Decision D1); MII thresholds (Decision D1); a change in American's premium-share build-out schedule.

**Decision D4 — Adopt the 6:15 a.m. degraded-morning stress case as the formal sizing scenario for remaining Terminal F geometry decisions, tabletop it with named federal partners, and re-test annually against the 9-vs-13-bank question.** *Owner:* Chief Operating Officer, with the Chief Engineer and American DFW Hub Operations. *Approval route:* internal program directive; results reported to the Operations, Safety and Security Committee. *First 90-day action:* rerun the Skylink-node vertical circulation, FIS queue depth, checkpoint room geometry, and BHS trunk capacity against the January 2023 winter-event demand curve loaded onto a 13-bank morning; publish results before the next affected module's fabrication release; hold a tabletop against the scenario with named participants (AA DFW Hub Ops, TSA Federal Security Director, CBP Port Director, DFW Guest Experience, ARFF, airport police, Skylink operations). *Cost order of magnitude:* tabletop and re-sizing exercise inside COO discretionary budget; downstream geometry changes carried by Decision D2. *Funding source:* inside base Terminal F scope for geometry; operating budget for tabletop. *Success measure:* every still-open pour-locked geometry decision documented against the stress case before release; annual re-test explicitly asks whether the peak-density reading of the rebank still holds against the COO's flatter-profile reading; TSA FSD and CBP Port Director sign a written concurrence on the operating implications of the sized geometry. *Failure mode:* the case is invoked once at design intent and forgotten; tabletop reveals CBP or TSA staffing cannot absorb the sized throughput and the sizing is engineering-only. *Stop condition:* if the 13-bank structure is walked back or the COO's flatter-profile reading is corroborated by two consecutive full-year operating cycles, the sizing case changes and the register unwinds the affected geometry. *What evidence would change the recommendation:* two full-year cycles of rebank operating data; A321XLR-scale connecting-share erosion; a TSA or CBP written finding on the sized geometry.

**Decision D5 — Fix the sensor, cabling, and data-plane schedule at the module template, only after Decision D0 clears the procurement path.** *Owner:* Chief Information Officer, with the Chief Engineer. *Approval route:* Innovation Next+ JV scope-change instrument (a JV instrument, not a modular-manufacturer memo); reported to the Operations, Safety and Security Committee. *First 90-day action:* on Decision D0's confirmation, finalize a written spec — dense conduit and pull-boxes on 20-foot centers, fiber and PoE++ backbone provisioned at 3–5× current sensor count, camera mounts and data drops at four apron corners and the jetbridge tunnel per gate, fiber diversity out of the terminal, CUPPS-capable gate hardware — and ship it through the factory once, with AA IT participation in factory witness testing against a live A-CDM, biometric, and CBP stack. *Cost order of magnitude:* template modification premium modest if D0 clears; materially higher if not. *Funding source:* Terminal F base scope if inside the change-order envelope; next supplemental if not. *Success measure:* the concourse's modular gates delivered against a common instrumentation baseline, tested in the factory against the integrated stack. *Failure mode:* per-gate variation eats the repetition dividend and produces a terminal where every module is integrated separately. *Stop condition:* factory witness testing reveals the template will not clear repeat-part cost; template rolls out in phases against modules the JV can absorb without slipping C or A.

**Leading indicators, reported quarterly to the Board.** Percentage of fabrication releases preceded by a written register finding; count of preserved options exercised versus retired; aggregate register-driven option premium expressed as projected CPE contribution against the Finance and Audit Committee ceiling; AA connecting share at DFW inside the 55%–two-thirds band; rebank peak-density signal from Q-over-Q AA DFW departure performance; AA premium-share build-out on schedule (narrowbody 25% → 40%; lie-flat +50% by end of decade); Skylink single-loop transit performance during 22:00–06:00; number of Side Letter options countersigned versus dropped.

**Board actions requested.** At the next Board meeting: (i) adopt the register as a recurring standing item under the Operations, Safety and Security Committee, with the VP Terminal Development named as executive owner; (ii) delegate execution of the Terminal F Reversibility Side Letter to the Finance and Audit Committee; (iii) direct the Finance and Audit Committee to set the projected-CPE ceiling for aggregate register-driven option premium before the next new-money supplemental ordinance goes to Dallas and Fort Worth city councils. At the following Board meeting: (iv) note the first module's register finding and the tabletop concurrence letter from TSA and CBP; (v) receive the General Counsel's written summary of the two contract answers under Decision D1 and the Finance and Audit Committee's approved CPE ceiling.

**Stop conditions at the recommendation level, not just the tool level.** Three signals unwind the register as a whole rather than any one item on it: American announces a formal Flagship-tier build-out at Terminal F on a published schedule that the airport can align to, in which case generic bones under-serve a tenant-funded premium the shell was preserved for and a specific fit-out program should replace the register; AA connecting share at DFW falls below 55% across two consecutive full-year cycles, in which case the connecting-heavy sizing case softens and the register's connecting-geometry emphasis is over-committed; U&L renewal negotiations open materially earlier than 2043, in which case the Side Letter's terms are absorbed into the amendment and the register's contract instrument changes. The VP Terminal Development is authorized to convene an unwind review on any of the three; the CEO's office signs the unwind.

The register does not tell DFW what to build. It tells DFW what remains to be decided, before which date, at what price, from which funding source, against which federal dependency, and inside which contract instrument. It also names the reader's actual customer at Terminal F. Not the Flagship passenger already routed through Terminal D. Not the Admirals Club member headed to Terminal C. The connecting passenger, forty-five to ninety minutes on the ground, walking from a widebody arrival to a domestic departure across a Skylink node at 6:15 a.m. on a bad morning. The building is being made for her. Every option on the register that fails her test should come off. Every option that serves her — quietly, at low marginal cost, at repeat-part discipline through the module template, inside a contract instrument the airport actually holds — is the argument for spending the fabrication-release window well.

The concrete is a decoy. The register is the deadline. The airport-executive layer — the named owner, the real instrument, the confirmed procurement path, the CPE ceiling, the federal-partner concurrence, and the Board's standing route — is the recommendation.
