# The Building Is Already Finished

*Data centers near flight paths, and the gap between clearing a surface and proving compatibility*

**Transform Airports AI Council — Stage 2 Draft (v1)**
**Run:** Data Centers at Airports: Airside Risks
**Prepared for:** Airport executives, capital planners, and policy readers
**Date:** 2026-07-21

---

In June 2026, at The Eastern Iowa Airport in Cedar Rapids, a data center stood finished. Steel up, roof on, cooling plant installed — a completed building on a parcel Google had developed near the airfield. There was only one problem. The airport was not sure it could let the thing turn on. The company had to ask for a *temporary* certificate of occupancy while the airport worked out whether "heat and steam plumes from the data center … could interfere with landings and takeoffs" [Operations-Analyst brief, citing KCRG, June 2026]. Read that sequence again. The concrete cured, the switchgear was energized, and only then did the question arrive: does this interfere with airplanes?

That is the wrong order. And it is not an Iowa problem. It is the order in which almost every one of these decisions is being made, because the review that governs a large building near a runway asks the wrong question. A height-and-obstruction review measures a shape. It asks whether the building pokes through an imaginary surface in the sky — the Part 77 surfaces, the geometry of protected airspace. It is a survey problem, and a data center passes it easily, because a data center is short. The hazard a data center poses to aircraft has almost nothing to do with its silhouette. It lives in the machinery: how it rejects several hundred megawatts of heat, how it makes backup power when the grid fails, how much water it pools on the ground, how much metal and radio noise it stacks beside a localizer.

**Here is the argument. A data center near an approach or departure path should have to prove compatibility with aircraft operations before it is approved — not merely demonstrate that its roofline stays below a surface. The controlling risk is not the steam wisp on a cold morning; it is the process plant running under stress, most acutely a fleet of diesel generators in prolonged islanded operation, and the permanent foreclosure of airfield capacity that no operator can ever un-build. The tools to run that test exist, are mostly cheap, and are already used for power plants. What is missing is the requirement to use them, and the discipline to use them on the right day.**

## Executive Summary

1. **The review that governs the decision cannot see the hazard.** 14 CFR Part 77 evaluates whether a *structure* penetrates airspace surfaces; thermal plumes, generator exhaust, EMI, lighting, and wildlife are not Part 77 surfaces. A data center can earn a "Determination of No Hazard" and still create every airside risk under discussion [Regulatory-Political brief]. The FAA's own guidance warns that plume turbulence "can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the top of the stack or cooling tower," worst in calm, cold, stable air, "in and around approach and departure corridors" [Chief-Engineer brief, citing FAA AIM 7-6-16]. The building clears the surface; the hazard column clears the building.

2. **The controlling scenario is prolonged emergency generation, not normal cooling.** On an ordinary day a modern data center is a quiet box moving warm air. The design-controlling event is a grid outage that forces a fleet of diesel generators — a small on-site power plant — into sustained simultaneous operation. Virginia data centers hold permits for more than 10,500 diesel generators totaling roughly 27 gigawatts of backup, and a single campus can field dozens [Operations-Analyst brief, citing Virginia Mercury]. Federal rules cap testing near 100 hours a year but place *no time limit* on run hours during a declared emergency [Airport-COO brief, citing backuppower.ai]. That is the plume, the smoke, and the fire call that matter.

3. **The emergency-response load is an industrial fire the airport was never sized to fight.** A large-hub fire department is federally indexed under Part 139 to reach a burning aircraft at the far runway midpoint within about three minutes. It carries foam and dry chemical for fuel fires. It cannot extinguish a lithium-ion battery fire, which needs sustained water or submersion. When a government data center in Daejeon, South Korea, caught fire in September 2025, it burned for roughly 22 hours and drew more than 200 firefighters and 60 engines [Director-of-Public-Safety brief; Emergency-Management brief, citing Korea Herald and NetworkWorld]. A three-minute clock and a 22-hour fire are incompatible commitments from one roster.

4. **The single codified, enforceable hook is wildlife and water — not turbulence.** FAA guidance directs managers to eliminate hazardous wildlife attractants within 10,000 feet of the air operations area for turbine airports and names stormwater impoundments explicitly; roughly 95% of reported bird strikes occur in the low-altitude approach and departure band [Operations-Analyst brief, citing FAA and ScienceDirect]. A hyperscale campus's stormwater ponds and warm basins sit squarely inside that ring. This is the risk with a number attached, and it should anchor any screen.

5. **The federal current is running away from scrutiny, which leaves airside safety as the only door open.** Section 743 of the 2024 FAA Reauthorization bars the FAA from conditioning a non-aeronautical project — even through a grant assurance — unless it "materially impacts the safe and efficient operation of aircraft," and gives the agency 45 days to assert jurisdiction or lose it [Regulatory-Political brief, citing Kaplan Kirsch]. Congress narrowed federal review precisely as the data-center land rush arrived. Airside risk is no longer a bonus argument; it is the last enforceable one.

6. **The one risk that cannot be mitigated is the one the review ignores: permanent capacity foreclosure.** A commissioned data center is a 20-to-50-year fixed asset with contractual uptime obligations; there is no phase-two removal [Chief-Engineer, Airport-CEO, Airport-COO briefs]. Whatever future runway, taxiway, approach, or lower-visibility procedure the site sterilizes is sterilized for a generation, decided by a real-estate reviewer who never priced it.

7. **The incentives all point to "yes," which is exactly why the screen has to be imposed from the top.** MWAA sold 424 acres of Dulles "Western Lands" — part of an 854-acre tract assembled in 2005–2007 for a fourth runway — to Digital Realty for $236.5 million in 2018, with proceeds dedicated to lowering Dulles's cost per enplaned passenger [Infrastructure-Economist, Airport-CEO, Regulatory-Political briefs]. The land now carries a campus master-planned for up to 1 gigawatt of IT load [Infrastructure-Economist brief, citing Data Center Frontier]. The county harvests the recurring tax annuity; the airport captured a one-time check and kept the airspace above the plume.

8. **The honest counter is real and it sharpens the thesis rather than defeating it.** The frightening plume-turbulence literature comes from high-temperature combustion stacks, not chiller yards; the world's densest data-center clusters already sit next to Dulles and O'Hare with no documented airside incident; and cooling is trending toward closed-loop designs that throw no visible plume at all [Contrarian brief]. The correct response is not a blanket gate over every building past a proximity line. It is a configuration-first, risk-tiered screen that scrutinizes evaporative cooling and prolonged generation heavily and waves through dry, closed-loop designs well outside the corridor.

## The Argument

### A geometry test for a machinery problem

Start with what the review actually does. When a developer proposes a structure near an airport, the binding federal instrument is a Form 7460-1 filing and a Part 77 aeronautical study. The FAA models whether the building penetrates the airspace surfaces that protect approaches, departures, and traffic patterns, and issues a "No Hazard" or "Hazard" determination. It is a good tool for the question it answers. That question is: is this object too tall, here?

It is not built to answer any of the questions this run is about. It does not evaluate heat. It does not evaluate exhaust chemistry. It does not evaluate radio-frequency emissions as anything other than a physical penetration. The regulatory analyst on this panel put it plainly: Part 77 "measures the building's shape, not its behavior" [Regulatory-Political brief]. And the two can diverge completely. The FAA's own Aeronautical Information Manual, section 7-6-16, tells pilots to avoid flight in the vicinity of exhaust plumes from smokestacks and cooling towers, warns that the turbulent effects "can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the top of the stack or cooling tower," and specifies that the danger concentrates in "calm and cold air, especially in and around approach and departure corridors" [Chief-Engineer brief]. A structure that clears every Part 77 surface can still launch a column of disturbed air a thousand feet into exactly the low, slow, low-margin band where an aircraft is configuring to land.

The technology scout on this panel framed the divergence as cleanly as anyone: two data centers with identical footprints and identical heights can pose completely different airside risks, and the difference is invisible to an obstruction review [Technology-Scout brief]. One runs a closed-loop dry cooling plant and emits a weak, invisible thermal plume. The other runs banks of open evaporative towers that throw a visible, buoyant, saturated column into a departure corridor. The regulatory surfaces treat them the same. The physics does not. A review triggered by *shape* is structurally blind to a hazard that lives in the *machine*.

This is not a theoretical gap. It is the gap that produced a finished building in Cedar Rapids that could not get a permanent occupancy permit. It is the gap that let roughly 200 data centers rise in Loudoun County's "Data Center Alley," some 26 to 50 million square feet of them, directly under and adjacent to Dulles operations, while the public fight was about noise, water, and power — and the airside compatibility question went essentially un-asked [Technology-Scout, Airport-COO briefs]. The buildings are already there. The question is what we make the next one prove.

### The wrong day: why "normal cooling" is measuring the wrong scenario

The single most important analytic move in this entire debate is choosing the day you model. Get the day wrong and every study downstream is theater.

The instinct — and the developer's preferred framing — is to model normal operation. On a normal day, the case for worry is weak, and the honest analysts on this panel say so. Data-center evaporative and dry coolers are low-velocity heat sources compared with a power-plant stack; the routine-cooling turbulence risk is real but modest [Airport-COO, Chief-Engineer briefs]. If the argument stopped there, the skeptics would win.

It does not stop there, because the controlling scenario is not the normal day. It is the grid outage during a heat event or an ice storm, when the campus flips from "invisible plume" to a small power plant: dozens of diesel generators starting at once, running hot exhaust for days, at peak thermal load, in the calm cold stable air that maximizes plume rise. Every brief that touched the physics converged on this. The chief engineer called it "the generator plant under prolonged islanded operation" and warned that any compatibility finding modeling only normal cooling "has tested the wrong load case" [Chief-Engineer brief]. The COO, the emergency manager, the operations analyst, and the contrarian all landed in the same place independently.

Three facts define the scenario. First, scale: a single campus can hold 40 to 138 generators producing up to roughly 345 MW, each unit 1 to 3 MW [Airport-COO brief, citing CPR]. Second, duration: testing is capped near 100 hours a year, but there is no federal cap on run hours during a declared emergency [Airport-COO brief]. Third — and this is the part that should unsettle anyone approving against today's conditions — the worst case is getting worse. Virginia's Department of Environmental Quality is being asked to *expand* permitted generator run-hours precisely because the grid cannot keep up with data-center load [Chief-Engineer brief, citing DataCenterKnowledge and Inside Climate News]. Approve a facility against today's 20-minutes-a-month test profile and you may inherit tomorrow's extended-runtime variance. The operating envelope is not a fixed engineering constant. It is a regulatory variable trending against the airport.

There is a deeper reason the outage scenario controls, and the emergency manager on this panel stated it from 25 years in the EOC: the data center's worst day is correlated with the airport's worst day. "A grid failure, an ice storm, or a hurricane does not politely hit the data center on a day the airport is calm" [Emergency-Management brief]. The same regional event that already degrades the airfield — reduced arrival rates, crews committed, mutual-aid partners stretched — is the event that puts dozens of megawatts of diesel into continuous generation a few thousand feet from the runway. Standard reviews are worst at pricing correlated risk. An obstruction review is a steady-state geometric snapshot; it has no way to represent "48 hours of continuous diesel generation and a 22-hour battery fire during the same ice storm that closed a runway" [Emergency-Management brief]. That composite is the day the screen must be built around.

### The risk with a number: wildlife and water

If the plume is the cinematic risk, wildlife is the quantifiable one — and it is the only risk in this bundle with a codified, enforceable federal standard already attached.

FAA guidance treats open and standing water as a hazardous wildlife attractant. AC 150/5200-33C directs that hazardous attractants be kept beyond 10,000 feet of the air operations area at turbine airports, applies a 5-statute-mile concept where an attractant could move wildlife across approach or departure airspace, and specifically names stormwater impoundments; detention basins are supposed to stay dry between rainfalls [Operations-Analyst, Chief-Engineer, Regulatory-Political briefs]. Against that standard sits a hard operational fact: roughly 95% of reported bird strikes occur below about 1,067 meters AGL — the approach and departure band — and peer-reviewed work finds stormwater ponds near airports are used by exactly the hazard species that matter [Operations-Analyst brief, citing ScienceDirect].

A hyperscale campus imports two attractants inside that ring: large stormwater basins that hold open water and offer thermal refuge in winter, and expansive flat roofs that make ideal loafing and nesting habitat. Unlike plume turbulence, this risk needs no borrowed foreign threshold and no bespoke CFD run. It is quantifiable with existing strike data and existing FAA distance standards. The regulatory analyst identified it as the codified hook that survives even the narrowed post-2024 federal regime: a data center's cooling and stormwater design is "a federally reviewable airside question, not a civil-site afterthought" [Regulatory-Political brief]. It should be the anchor of any screen, precisely because it is the piece hardest to argue away at a zoning board.

### The tenant the airport did not staff for

Now the risk the thesis itself undersells, and the director of public safety insists is the controlling one from his chair. A data center is not a warehouse with servers. It is a concentrated fuel farm, a lithium-ion hazard, and a cooling plant that competes with the airport's hydrants for water — dropped onto or beside an airfield whose emergency apparatus was sized for a completely different problem.

Take the pieces in turn. The fuel: NFPA 110 standby requirements and Tier III/IV designs drive 72 to 96 hours of on-site diesel, which means a single large facility can store from roughly 25,000 up to 240,000-plus gallons under NFPA 30 [Director-of-Public-Safety brief, citing PowerMag]. That is a bulk combustible-liquid inventory, with routine generator testing, sited near movement areas and vulnerable to the same events — overrun, spill, vehicle strike — that already define airfield risk. The batteries: ARFF trucks carry foam and dry chemical for hydrocarbon fires. Lithium-ion thermal runaway is self-sustaining, generates its own oxygen, vents toxic hydrogen fluoride, and reignites after knockdown. "No portable fire extinguisher stops a lithium-ion thermal runaway," the fire-service literature concludes; the only reliable tactic is massive sustained water or submersion [Director-of-Public-Safety brief, citing ACERTS]. The water: hyperscale cooling draws 1 to 5 million gallons a day, silently re-rating the airport's available fire flow unless someone re-runs the hydraulics before approval [Director-of-Public-Safety brief].

Then the controlling case, made concrete. Daejeon, September 2025: a lithium-ion pack in a government data center entered thermal runaway; extinguishment took roughly 22 hours, more than 200 firefighters, and 60 engines, and 647 government IT systems went dark [Director-of-Public-Safety, Emergency-Management briefs]. Translate that to a Part 139 field: a 22-hour commitment of regional structural-fire resources staged around the fence line, while the airport still owes a three-minute ARFF response to every departure. No U.S. airport fire force generates that response from its own roster; every unit comes from mutual aid. And "mutual aid will cover it" is an assumption, not a plan — it depends on signed agreements, pre-incident plans, SIDA access for outside companies, drafting points, and radios that survive the facility's own electrical noise [Director-of-Public-Safety brief]. None of that is in an obstruction study.

History rhymes here. On December 17, 2017, an underground switchgear fire at Atlanta cut airport power for about 11 hours, canceled more than 1,000 flights, and stranded some 30,000 travelers — because the backup feed was co-located with the primary, so one fire took both [Emergency-Management brief, citing Utility Dive]. The lesson the field absorbed was physical separation of redundancy. A data center is the built embodiment of the opposite instinct: extreme power density and redundancy concentrated in one place, either competing for the airport's own utility feeds or becoming a second critical load the region expects the airport to help protect [Emergency-Management brief]. Both are the airport's problem, and neither appears on Form 7460.

### The risk you cannot un-build

Everything above can, in principle, be mitigated. Dry coolers instead of wet. Lined, fast-draining basins instead of amenity ponds. Bunded fuel storage, a hardened separate fire-water supply, EMI shielding, generator siting informed by the FAA's own plume tool, enforceable run-hour caps. The genuinely location-locked residual — the one thing no operating restriction touches — is capacity foreclosure.

A commissioned data center is effectively permanent. It is a 20-to-50-year asset with contractual uptime obligations to tenants who, as the chief engineer put it, "will litigate before they accept an operating restriction" [Chief-Engineer brief]. There is no phase-two removal. Whatever airfield-capacity envelope it consumes — a future runway, a parallel taxiway, a displaced threshold, a lower-minimums approach not yet designed — is foreclosed for a generation. The obstruction check asks whether a 100-foot building pokes into today's surfaces. It does not ask whether it freezes the land and airspace the airport would need in 2050. As the COO said from the duty desk: the objection is not "it's too tall today"; it is "it forecloses the airfield we might need" — and "the one thing the duty desk can never do is un-build the box after it's energized" [Airport-COO brief].

This is where the CEO's chair reframes the whole run. From the top of the house, every other risk on this list is manageable, monitorable, or restrictable. Foreclosed capacity is not. "That asymmetry, not any single engineering finding, is what should govern the decision" [Airport-CEO brief]. A well-structured ground lease improves every headline metric a rating agency likes — non-aeronautical revenue diversification, lower cost per enplanement, coverage, days cash. But the same agencies penalize the *story* of an airport that mortgaged its aviation franchise for a real-estate check. The reversible mistakes are survivable. The irreversible ones define careers.

### Why the market will always argue "yes"

The reason this needs a hard screen, rather than good intentions, is that the economics are structured so that everyone with a vote is pulled toward approval, and the party holding the residual risk captures the smallest slice of the value.

Follow the money at Dulles, because MWAA is not a bystander to this thesis — it is the case study. The 2018 sale netted the Authority roughly $207 million on 424 acres [Infrastructure-Economist, Airport-CEO briefs]. The land now carries a campus master-planned for up to 11.7 million square feet, 14 buildings, and about 1 gigawatt of IT load across six substations [Infrastructure-Economist brief, citing Data Center Frontier]. Meanwhile, Loudoun County's personal-property tax on data-center equipment grew from $60 million in FY2013 to more than $800 million in FY2026, and data centers are projected to supply roughly 45% of county tax revenue — about $1.3 billion of $2.9 billion — by FY2027 [Infrastructure-Economist brief, citing NetChoice]. The county harvests an annuity. The airport got a one-time check and kept the airspace that the plume and the gensets sit under. When a party captures little upside but retains the downside, screening discipline is the only protection, because the deal economics will always argue yes.

Power scarcity locks the incentive in. Northern Virginia passed roughly 4,900 MW of active data-center capacity by early 2025, and Dominion's contracted data-center load jumped from about 21 GW in mid-2024 to about 40 GW by year-end [Infrastructure-Economist brief, citing IEEFA]. PJM capacity prices in the Dominion zone rose roughly tenfold, clearing near the FERC cap for 2026/27 [Infrastructure-Economist brief]. In that market, the cooling and backup choices that reduce airside risk are the expensive ones. Full dry cooling — which eliminates the visible plume — costs three to four times as much to install and carries a 25-to-35% power penalty [Infrastructure-Economist brief, citing DOE via Data Center Knowledge]. The plume-free option is the one the developer can least afford. The risk-reducing design is the economically penalized design. That is a market-design problem, not a physics problem, and markets over-produce unpriced externalities.

The airline strategist adds the sharpest twist: the first-order carrier reaction to a data-center lease is *approval*, because under residual and hybrid-residual rate methodologies the revenue lowers cost per enplanement, and non-aeronautical revenue is now roughly 46% of total U.S. airport revenue [Airline-Commercial brief, citing ACI-NA]. That alignment is a trap. It buys carrier silence on the one axis that is cheapest to satisfy — short-term cost — while doing nothing for the two axes a hub carrier actually optimizes: capacity and reliability. And the decision that can hurt carriers most is made in the one room where they have no vote. Majority-in-interest clauses give signatory airlines a veto over airline-funded *capital projects*; they almost never extend to *non-aeronautical land-use* decisions [Airline-Commercial brief]. So a lease that later forecloses a taxiway or a run-up pad is decided outside carrier consent — and the carriers remember that when the constrained airfield project finally comes up for the MII vote they *do* control.

### The current is running the other way

The last piece of the argument is the one economists undersell and the regulatory analyst insists on: the thesis is right not because the physics is certain, but because *no one else is required to check* — and Congress just made that more true.

Section 743 of the 2024 FAA Reauthorization rewrote the rules of federal engagement. It replaced a list-based approach with a project-based test, and barred the FAA from conditioning a non-aeronautical project — even through a grant assurance — unless the project "materially impacts the safe and efficient operation of aircraft" or "adversely affects the safety of people or property on the ground." Airports now notify rather than seek a determination, and if the FAA does not assert jurisdiction within 45 days, it forfeits it [Regulatory-Political brief, citing Kaplan Kirsch and AirTAP]. The deliberate direction of federal policy is less review, not more — arriving precisely as the land rush peaks.

That inverts the usual assumption. A plan that assumes the FAA will catch a bad data center is planning against current law. But the same section leaves one door open, and it is exactly the door this thesis walks through: the carve-out for projects that "materially impact the safe and efficient operation of aircraft." Frame a plume, wildlife, or EMI risk credibly and the FAA stays in the room; fail to frame it and the 45-day clock runs out and federal leverage evaporates. The thesis's evidentiary standard is not a best-practice nicety. After 2024 it is the mechanism that keeps the only remaining federal door from closing.

## The Counter-Case, Honestly Presented

The strongest case against this thesis is not "data centers are harmless." It is narrower, better-evidenced, and harder to dismiss, and it deserves to be stated at full strength.

Begin with the physics, because the thesis leans on numbers that were not measured on a data center. Every serious turbulence finding — plumes reaching 1,000 feet above a source, hazardous vertical velocities — comes from power-plant smokestacks and combustion exhaust: high-temperature, high-velocity vertical jets [Contrarian brief]. A data center rejects *low-grade* heat through chillers, dry coolers, and cooling towers at modest exit temperatures and low exit velocities. Except for the rare hours its generators run, it is not a combustion source at all. Borrowing the power-plant literature and applying it to a chiller yard, the contrarian argues, overstates the hazard by an order of magnitude. And the widely cited 4.3 m/s vertical-velocity threshold is not even an FAA number — it is Australia's, from CASA, written around power-station cooling towers with far greater single-point heat flux than a distributed data-center dry-cooler field [Contrarian, Technology-Scout, Operations-Analyst briefs]. There is no domestic bright line, because for a decade the FAA declined to convert plume analysis into a Part 77 standard, and its own 2006 Safety Risk Analysis judged overflight risk "insignificant" [Regulatory-Political brief].

Then the revealed-preference evidence, which is the contrarian's best card. The two densest data-center clusters in the country already sit next to major hubs. Loudoun's Data Center Alley abuts Dulles. Elk Grove Village, the second-largest U.S. market, abuts O'Hare, the busiest complex airfield in the country [Contrarian brief]. Decades of coexistence, hundreds of facilities, dozens of generators — and no documented pattern of turbulence upsets, nav interference, or bird strikes traceable to these buildings. That is a large natural experiment, and it points the wrong way for a precautionary standard. If proximity produced airside incidents, the densest clusters next to the busiest fields would have surfaced them.

On the specific risks, the contrarian presses hard and is often right. On wildlife, the FAA's own guidance points the *other* way: AC 150/5200-33 targets landfills, wastewater plants, wetlands, and open water. A dry data center is none of those. It typically replaces farmland, retail, or vacant parcels — frequently *lowering* the wildlife attractant profile near the runway [Contrarian brief]. On the single risk the FAA regulates most aggressively, the data center is usually the compatible use. On EMI, the contrarian calls it a solution in search of a problem: data centers are RF-quiet by design and by FCC Part 15; the documented aviation-EMI problems come from *intentional* transmitters, and no chief engineer on this panel could produce a single documented case of a data center degrading an ILS, VOR, or GPS approach [Contrarian, Chief-Engineer briefs]. On emergency response, a low-occupancy, highly automated, clean-agent-suppressed building with its own fire suppression may be a *lighter* burden than the hotel, arena, or hospital that zoning would otherwise allow on the same parcel [Contrarian brief].

And the technology arc is bending away from the exact mechanism the thesis fears. The industry is moving to closed-loop liquid and "zero-water" cooling for water-scarcity reasons — which means the marginal new campus is *less* plume-generating than the last one [Contrarian, Technology-Scout briefs]. Industry siting practice already discounts the runway-end parcel as unsuitable for mission-critical facilities, so the most sensitive locations are self-selecting out of the developer's own shortlist [Contrarian brief].

The regulatory reality reinforces all of this. The mechanism the thesis wants — an evidence demand triggered by proximity — arguably already exists. The FAA maintains a Thermal Exhaust Plume assessment tool and the MITRE Exhaust Plume Analyzer; AIM 7-6-16 already tells pilots to avoid plumes; the 7460 process already lets the FAA demand a plume study [Contrarian brief]. So the honest challenge is this: what does a new "prove compatibility" standard add beyond better use of tools the FAA already has — and if the answer is "a generic precautionary gate applied to every building past a proximity line," then the thesis has manufactured a pseudo-technical obstruction that raises no aircraft's margin of safety while treating a chiller yard like a smokestack. That is the argument that will be used to defeat a badly built screen at the zoning board. It is a good argument.

## Why the Counter-Case Is Insufficient

Concede what must be conceded, by name, without flinching.

Concede that the 1,000-foot turbulence figure and the 4.3 m/s threshold are combustion numbers, imported from power plants, and that applying them to routine data-center cooling overstates the hazard. The panel's own engineers say so: opponents "who hang the argument on a morning steam wisp will lose the technical fight" [Chief-Engineer brief]. Concede that EMI is unproven — no brief on this panel, including the ones building the case for the thesis, could produce a documented instance of a data center degrading a navigation aid [Chief-Engineer, Contrarian briefs]. Concede that a dry data center usually *reduces* the wildlife profile relative to farmland or a landfill, and that the wildlife argument holds only at the stormwater-pond vector. Concede that cooling technology is genuinely de-risking, that closed-loop designs throw no visible plume, and that the runway-end parcel is already self-selecting out. Concede, finally, that a blanket precautionary gate over every building past a proximity line would be exactly the obstruction the contrarian warns against, and would deserve to lose.

None of that touches the thesis, because the thesis does not rest on the routine day, the visible steam, or a monolithic hazard class. It rests on three things the concessions leave standing.

**First: the controlling scenario the contrarian's own physics validates.** The strongest point on the thesis's side is the one the contrarian *conceded* — in writing. "The prolonged-outage generator scenario is real and is the controlling case," the contrarian wrote. "My 'tens of minutes per month' argument collapses during a multi-day grid failure, when a large campus can run its entire diesel fleet continuously. At full site load, an AI campus of 150–250 MW could operate dozens of large generators for days. *That* is a combustion source — hot, sustained exhaust — and it is exactly the condition (and the atmospheric window: calm, cold, stable air) the plume-turbulence literature flags" [Contrarian brief]. The combustion-versus-cooling distinction does not rescue the data center; it *identifies the scenario in which the data center becomes the power plant the literature was written about.* The whole point of modeling the islanded-generation case is that it converts the borrowed combustion numbers from an overstatement into the correct reference. And the trend cuts the wrong way: DEQ is being asked to raise the run-hour ceiling because the grid cannot keep up [Chief-Engineer brief]. The scenario that validates the hazard is becoming more frequent, not less.

**Second: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — and the reviewer never looked.** The natural-experiment argument leans entirely on the absence of a documented incident, and the contrarian, to its credit, conceded the weakness: "Turbulence upsets on approach are often attributed to wake, weather, or 'unknown,' and thermal-plume causation is rarely investigated or logged. The Elk Grove/Loudoun record may be quieter than reality" [Contrarian brief]. There is a structural reason the record is quiet at Dulles specifically: the prevailing approach and departure paths run mostly north–south, while the Digital Dulles site sits to the west, so aircraft do not routinely fly through its plumes [Deep-Research brief]. The quiet is partly geometry — the campus happens to sit off the centerline — not proof that a campus *on* the centerline would be benign. And more fundamentally, the compatibility question at Dulles was never asked: the environmental review focused on wetlands; analysis of thermal or airspace effects was, in the public record, simply absent [Deep-Research brief]. You cannot cite the silence of a study that was never run as evidence of safety. The absence of a smoking gun is not evidence of safety; it is evidence that the question is being answered after the concrete is poured — which is the thesis.

**Third — and decisively: irreversibility is not a risk you can mitigate, so it is the one you must screen.** Every one of the contrarian's engineering rebuttals shares a hidden premise: that the risk is *controllable by design*. Dry cooling kills the plume; NFPA 855 separation contains the battery; dry basins deny the birds; shielding handles the EMI. Grant every one. The capacity-foreclosure risk has no design fix, because it is not a property of the machinery — it is a property of the *land and the permanence*. Here the contrarian concedes the whole point: "Even if today's plume is benign, a permanent 1.2-million-square-foot building forecloses a future runway extension, taxiway, or approach redesign. 'It doesn't hurt current operations' is not the same as 'it preserves optionality.' Land-use permanence is a real cost the height-only review does miss — and here the thesis is simply correct" [Contrarian brief]. That concession is fatal to the counter-case, because it is the one risk that cannot be engineered away and the one the current review is structurally incapable of catching.

So the thesis holds — but the concessions reshape it, and this is where the contrarian actually makes the argument *better*. The correct output is not a blanket gate. It is a **configuration-first, risk-tiered screen**, and every honest brief on this panel, including the skeptical ones, converged on it. Classify the outdoor heat-rejection system first, because that single answer sets the risk tier: heavy scrutiny on open evaporative cooling inside a departure corridor, light-touch on closed-loop or dry systems well outside it [Technology-Scout brief]. Trigger the studies by the variables that actually carry risk — evaporative cooling, generator fleet size and run-hour exposure, detention-pond siting, and capacity-foreclosure geometry — not by a proximity line drawn around every building [Contrarian, Technology-Scout briefs]. Reward the closed-loop design that de-risks itself. A screen built this way does exactly what the contrarian demands: it does not treat a chiller yard like a smokestack. It reserves the smokestack treatment for the day the chiller yard *becomes* a smokestack — the islanded-generation case — and for the pond, the roof, and the permanence that no cooling choice can fix.

And the tools to run it are neither exotic nor expensive. The MITRE Exhaust Plume Analyzer is free and models aircraft upset for four aircraft classes. The CASA method supplies a defensible screening threshold the U.S. framework lacks. Airport EMI suites model ILS reflection. FAA AC 150/5200-33C supplies deterministic wildlife-separation distances [Technology-Scout brief]. The demonstration the thesis demands is technically routine. The gap is procedural, not technological — which means it is the kind of gap an operator can close by choosing to.

## Implications for MWAA

MWAA is not weighing an abstraction. It has already run this play once, on the exact land this thesis is about, and it is about to be asked to run it again.

Start with the record. Between 2005 and 2007 the Authority assembled an 854-acre tract at Dulles explicitly to hold open a fourth runway and its support facilities. In September 2018 it sold 424 of those acres — the "Western Lands" — to Digital Realty for $236.5 million, netting roughly $207 million, with proceeds dedicated to a fund that lowers Dulles's cost per enplaned passenger [Infrastructure-Economist, Airport-CEO, Regulatory-Political, Procurement briefs]. The compatibility review that approved it modeled wetlands, not plumes or generators [Deep-Research brief]. This is the outcome the thesis is trying to prevent from happening blind, and it happened here. To be fair to that decision: the parcel was runway-adjacent, not runway-critical; the planned fifth runway sits on the south side, not the western parcel; and the proceeds were legally confined to reducing Dulles's cost base, a genuine benefit to carriers and passengers [Infrastructure-Economist, Regulatory-Political briefs]. The point is not that MWAA paved a runway site. It is that the *category* of decision — monetizing airfield-reserve land under a "no future airport use envisioned" assumption — was made without the airside test the thesis demands, and the assumption is aging.

Because the premise is now under stress. Dulles runs more than 560,000 operations a year, posted the highest growth rate of the nation's 50 largest airports in 2025, and a fifth runway approved in a 2005 FAA Record of Decision but never scheduled is back under fresh FAA review in 2025 [Infrastructure-Economist, Airline-Commercial briefs]. United carried roughly 49.9% of IAD enplanements in 2025, about 62% including United Express [Airline-Commercial brief]. That single-carrier concentration is the reason foreclosing capacity looks free today — IAD has spare airfield because United thinned the hub after the Continental merger — and the reason it is not free. Carrier demand is cyclical, and optioned airfield land is worth most exactly when spare capacity looks abundant and cheap to sell. Selling the option at the cyclical low is the classic airport error, and it is invisible to a fair-market appraisal [Airline-Commercial brief].

Three moves follow, each grounded in a lever MWAA actually holds.

**Choose the lease, not the sale.** The Western Lands were conveyed fee-simple, which is the weakest possible instrument for enforcing operating restrictions over a 30-year asset. Once title transfers, covenants — generator run-hour caps, plume monitoring, dry-basin stormwater, lighting spec, EMI obligations — survive only as recorded deed restrictions, hard to monitor and harder to claw back [Procurement brief]. A long-term ground lease with reopeners and a capacity-reservation clause keeps enforcement, reversion, and future-runway optionality in the Authority's hands. For a use whose airside risk is still being characterized, the lease is the correct vehicle; it lets MWAA tighten restrictions at renewal and reclaim land if a future compatibility finding or the fifth runway demands it. Name the cost honestly: the covenant-heavy lease is the slower, lower-upfront-cash instrument. That is the price of keeping the pen.

**Build the record the narrowed federal regime now requires of you.** Section 743 means MWAA cannot outsource this judgment to the FAA. When the Authority notifies the FAA of a project, framing a credible "materially impacts the safe and efficient operation of aircraft" case within 45 days is what keeps FAA jurisdiction alive; silence forfeits it [Regulatory-Political brief]. The binding windows are the Authority's own instruments: the ALP amendment and land-release decision, where Grant Assurance 21 compatibility is formally judged, and the ground-lease covenant, where a plume ceiling and generator limits become enforceable rather than aspirational [Regulatory-Political, Procurement briefs]. And the honest schedule from board direction to a defensible, FAA-consented instrument is 18 to 30 months — not the 6-to-12 a real-estate pro forma assumes — because independent compatibility studies alone run 9 to 15 months, and no developer notice-to-proceed should precede the FAA airspace determination [Procurement brief]. A faster timeline is borrowing certainty from a study that has not been done.

**Publish the screen, and require these studies on any data center within the 5-statute-mile approach/departure zone or penetrating Part 77 notice surfaces.** Not as a veto dressed as a study, but as a configuration-first, risk-tiered gate the developer must clear:

- **A future-capacity finding**, ALP-referenced, demonstrating the site forecloses no reserved runway, taxiway, approach, or procedure. This is the non-mitigable residual — treat it as pass/fail [Airport-COO, Chief-Engineer briefs].
- **A thermal-plume assessment** using the FAA/MITRE tool run on the *full islanded-generation load case* under calm, cold, stable conditions, mapped against actual approach and departure geometry and reported against an adopted vertical-velocity reference (CASA's 4.3 m/s, flagged as a borrowed standard) — not a normal-cooling steam analysis [Chief-Engineer, Technology-Scout, Operations-Analyst briefs].
- **A wildlife and stormwater compliance review** to AC 150/5200-33C: lined, fast-draining basins, no amenity ponds inside the separation distances, no open water [Operations-Analyst, Airport-COO briefs].
- **An emergency-response package**: an aggregate fuel-inventory and SPCC analysis treating the campus as a single hazmat site under 139.321; an NFPA 855 battery-suppression plan; a re-run of the airport's fire-flow hydraulics after the cooling draw; and a rehearsed, signed mutual-aid concept of operations — with a cost-share for the ARFF and EM burden the pro forma never funds [Director-of-Public-Safety, Emergency-Management briefs].
- **A NAVAID reflection-and-RFI study** and a lighting/glare review to downward-shielded, non-specular standards [Technology-Scout, Chief-Engineer briefs].

MWAA is uniquely positioned to make this the reference standard rather than a local defensive crouch. It sits at the center of the largest data-center market on earth; it is an independent operating authority with a 17-member board and plenary power to hold its own land and write its own covenants [Airport-CEO brief]; and it has the credibility, precisely because it made the 2018 trade, to define "airside-compatible" before the FAA does. There is even an upside the risk framing misses: the scarce asset in a data-center deal is not the land or the chips but grid interconnection, jammed for five to seven years, and an airport with banked land, a utility relationship, and room for a substation controls the one siding everyone needs [Virtual-Chris brief]. The controlling variable — evaporative versus closed-loop cooling — is a lease clause, not a law of physics, and anything that is a lease clause is negotiable. The move is to price the risk into the deal, not to pretend the deal will not come.

---

The building in Cedar Rapids is finished. It will almost certainly operate, and it will almost certainly be fine, because most of these buildings on most days are fine. That is exactly why the sequence should trouble an executive: the airport was reduced to negotiating occupancy for a structure it could no longer refuse, because the only review that had teeth measured a shape and the shape was legal. The question is not whether data centers belong near airports. Some do, engineered right and sited off the centerline; some do not. The question is whether the airport asks *before* the concrete cures or *after* — and whether, when it finally reserves land for a runway it may need in 2050, it still owns the ground to put it on.
