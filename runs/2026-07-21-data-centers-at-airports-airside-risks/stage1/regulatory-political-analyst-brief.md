# Regulatory & Political Analyst Brief
## Data Centers at Airports: What the Rules Bind, What the Politics Won't Move

**Thesis under test:** height and land-use review can pass a data center that airside-risk review would flag. My job is to say which of those reviews the operator is actually *required* to run, which are advisory, and — the part economists miss — which direction the political current is running. It is running *away* from federal scrutiny, and the one lever that survives is airside safety. That makes the thesis's demand for independent compatibility evidence not a best-practice nicety but the last enforceable hook MWAA has.

---

## 1. Key Findings

- **Part 77 is a geometry test, not a physics test.** 14 CFR Part 77 evaluates whether a *structure* penetrates imaginary airspace surfaces. Thermal plumes, generator exhaust, EMI, lighting, and wildlife are not Part 77 surfaces. A data center can earn a "Determination of No Hazard" and still create every airside risk in the thesis. **This is regulation — and it is regulation built for the wrong hazard.** [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-77]

- **A Part 77 "No Hazard" determination cannot stop construction.** It is advisory. The FAA issues an opinion on airspace; it does not have veto power over a private building. The binding leverage sits in *land use* (grant assurances, the ALP, the ground lease), not in the airspace study. Confusing the two is the single most common regulatory error operators make here.

- **The strongest codified hook is wildlife, not turbulence.** AC 150/5200-33C bars new water impoundments of ≥¼ acre within a runway approach and within 5,000 ft of a runway end, and treats attractants within 10,000 ft (turbine airports) or 5 statute miles of approach/departure airspace as presumptively incompatible. Data-center **stormwater retention ponds** land squarely inside this rule. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf]

- **The FAA has no codified plume-obstruction standard.** It calls thermal plumes "incompatible" in advisory language (AIM 7-6-16) and assesses them case-by-case with the MITRE Exhaust Plume Analyzer, but there is no Part 77 surface for heat. The 2006 FAA Safety Risk Analysis concluded overflight risk was "insignificant." **That contradiction is the regulatory gap the thesis exploits** — guidance says avoid, analysis says relax, and neither is a binding land-use standard. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html]

- **Congress just narrowed the FAA's ability to police this.** The FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (Pub. L. 118–63), §743 — rewriting §163 — bars the FAA from conditioning or regulating non-aeronautical projects, *including through grant assurances*, unless the project "materially impacts the safe and efficient operation of aircraft" or "adversely affects the safety of people or property on the ground." The FAA gets **45 days** to assert jurisdiction or loses it. **This is politics: the deliberate direction of federal policy is less review, not more.** [Source: https://www.kaplankirsch.com/resources-and-news/faa-reauthorization-act-makes-key-changes-to-airport-law/]

- **Airside risk is now the *only* door left open.** §743's carve-out for projects that "materially impact the safe and efficient operation of aircraft" is exactly the category the thesis argues data centers occupy. Frame a plume/wildlife/EMI risk credibly and the FAA stays in the room; fail to frame it and the 45-day clock runs out and federal leverage evaporates. The thesis's evidentiary standard is the mechanism that keeps the door open.

- **The money runs one way.** FAA revenue-use rules require spare land to be leased at fair market value with proceeds spent on the airport — which turns idle acreage into a standing incentive to monetize. Data centers are premium, investment-grade, long-lease tenants. The economic gravity is toward "yes," and the political economy around most airports reinforces it. **The operator's own incentives are the adversary here, not some outside developer.**

- **The controlling scenario for regulators is generator operation, not normal cooling.** Grant Assurance 21 compatibility, wildlife rules, and plume guidance all bite hardest during prolonged emergency generation — the low-frequency, high-consequence state. Any screening that certifies on normal-cooling assumptions is measuring the wrong day.

## 2. Evidence: Regulation vs. Politics

### What the FAA *requires* (regulation)

**Part 77 / airspace notice.** A developer must file FAA Form 7460-1 for structures exceeding notice thresholds; the FAA runs an aeronautical study and issues a "No Hazard" or "Hazard" determination. The study evaluates physical penetration of Part 77 surfaces and effect on procedures and navigation aids. It does **not** evaluate heat, exhaust chemistry, or radio-frequency emissions as obstructions. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/obstruction_evaluation] [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-77]

**Wildlife attractants (AC 150/5200-33C).** The separation criteria are explicit and enforceable through grant assurances and the ALP: no ≥¼-acre impoundment within an approach corridor and 5,000 ft of a runway end; hazardous attractants within 10,000 ft (turbine) / 5 statute miles of approach-departure airspace are presumptively incompatible. Detention basins must be engineered dry between rainfalls to deny nesting habitat. A data center's cooling-water and stormwater design is therefore a *federally reviewable airside* question, not a civil-site afterthought. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf]

**Grant assurances (the real leverage).** Grant Assurance 21 (Compatible Land Use) implements 49 U.S.C. §47107(a)(10); Assurance 20 requires the sponsor to mitigate hazards; Assurance 25 requires airport revenue — including land-sale/lease proceeds — to be used for the airport. FAA Order 5190.6C (Chs. 20 and 22), effective February 20, 2026, tells FAA field staff how to apply these when a sponsor changes land to non-aeronautical use, which requires FAA approval and, for federally acquired land, a formal release. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/publications/orders/compliance_5190_6/order-5190-6c-compliance-chapter-20]

**Thermal plumes (guidance, not rule).** AIM 7-6-16 warns pilots that high-temperature exhaust plumes cause "turbulence and vertical shear" with effects that can extend more than 1,000 ft above the source, and advises avoidance. The FAA/MITRE Exhaust Plume Analyzer models the vertical gust needed to cause severe turbulence or upset for light aircraft at low altitude — but this is applied inside a discretionary aeronautical study, and the FAA's own 2006 Safety Risk Analysis judged overflight risk "insignificant." There is no numeric plume threshold codified in Part 77. [Source: https://www.mitre.org/our-impact/intellectual-property/exhaust-plume-analyzer] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf]

**Takeaway on the regulatory stack:** the codified, enforceable hooks are (1) wildlife/stormwater separation and (2) the compatible-land-use grant assurance applied at ALP change or land release. Everything about plumes, EMI, and lighting is *advisory* and only becomes binding if the airport writes it into the ground lease or the FAA folds it into a §743 "materially impacts safe operation" finding.

### What Congress has done (politics — changeable in principle, not on project timelines)

**§743 of the 2024 Act narrows the FAA on purpose.** It replaced §163's list-based approach with a project-based test, barred the FAA from extending authority to non-aeronautical portions of mixed-use projects, and — critically — prohibited the FAA from imposing conditions "through any grant assurance" on projects it lacks approval authority over. Airports now *notify* rather than seek a jurisdictional determination, and silence past 45 days forfeits FAA jurisdiction. Land acquired with federal assistance stays under FAA authority "without exception." [Source: https://www.kaplankirsch.com/resources-and-news/faa-reauthorization-act-makes-key-changes-to-airport-law/] [Source: https://airtap.umn.edu/publications/briefings/2024/december/landuse]

Read that against the thesis: Congress has *raised* the bar for federal intervention precisely as the data-center land rush arrives. The compatibility burden the thesis wants is now the sponsor's own to impose — through lease covenants and its own ALP discipline — because the federal backstop has been trimmed. **A plan that assumes the FAA will catch a bad data center is planning against the current law.**

**The revenue reauthorization cycle is the calendar that matters.** Pub. L. 118–63 authorizes FAA programs through September 2028; the next reauthorization is the realistic window to change §743's balance. That is a multi-year, must-pass-vehicle fight. On MWAA's project timeline, §743 is a fixed constraint, not a variable.

## 3. Case Studies: Where the Force Decided the Outcome

**MWAA Western Lands → "Digital Dulles" (2018).** MWAA assembled an 854-acre tract between 2005 and 2007 explicitly to build a **fourth runway** and supporting facilities. After the runway and support area were built, 424 acres of "Western Lands" sat idle. In September 2018 the MWAA Board approved selling those 424 acres to Digital Realty for **$236.5 million**, with proceeds dedicated to a fund that lowers Dulles's cost per enplaned passenger. The land is now the Digital Dulles data-center campus, complete with substations. [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/news/airports-authority-announces-sale-424-acres-western-lands-dulles-international] [Source: https://www.loudounnow.com/2018/09/14/424-acres-at-dulles-airport-could-be-sold-for-data-centers/]

The lesson is not that MWAA erred — the land was runway-adjacent, not runway-critical, and covenants barred residential use. The lesson is *what won*: airline CPE pressure plus Loudoun's data-center tax base beat long-horizon airfield optionality, and the deciding review was land-use compatibility and zoning, **not** an airside-risk study. The thesis's exact fear — that airfield-capacity preservation gets traded for near-term revenue under a compatibility review that never modeled plumes or generator load — is already the documented MWAA precedent. That is why this run matters at MWAA specifically.

**Loudoun County — "Data Center Alley."** The county hosts the largest data-center market on earth, and data centers now anchor a large share of its commercial tax base. The local political economy is overwhelmingly pro-data-center: jobs, tax revenue, and economic-development awards flow one way. An airport commission weighing airside risk against a data center is not arguing with a developer — it is arguing with the county's fiscal model. **Expect the local political gradient to run against the operator's caution.** [Source: https://biz.loudoun.gov/2025/01/22/loudoun-county-announces-economic-development-award-supporting-sky-harbours-business-aviation-campus-at-dulles-international-airport/]

**Power-plant plume precedents.** The reason there is no bright-line FAA plume rule is that the agency litigated the question for a decade around power plants (e.g., contested siting near airports that produced the 2011–2012 MITRE study). The FAA repeatedly declined to convert plume analysis into a Part 77 obstruction standard. For the operator, that means: **do not expect the FAA to hand you a number.** If MWAA wants a plume limit, MWAA writes it into the lease. [Source: https://www.aviationairportdevelopmentlaw.com/2012/11/articles/federal-aviation-administration-faa/faa-finally-issues-guidance-on-plume-hazards-to-aircraft-from-power-plants-or-does-it/]

## 4. Quotes & Data Points a Strategist Can Lift Verbatim

1. **On the current-law direction:** "Under §743 of the 2024 FAA Reauthorization, the FAA may not condition a non-aeronautical project — even through a grant assurance — unless it 'materially impacts the safe and efficient operation of aircraft,' and it has just 45 days to assert that jurisdiction or lose it. Airside risk is not a bonus argument; after 2024 it is the only federal argument left."

2. **On the geometry-vs-physics gap:** "A data center can hold a Part 77 'Determination of No Hazard' and still throw a thermal plume, attract gulls to its stormwater ponds, and run diesels beside a departure path. Part 77 measures the building's shape, not its behavior."

3. **On the wildlife hook:** "AC 150/5200-33C prohibits new water impoundments of a quarter-acre or larger within a runway approach and 5,000 feet of the runway end. A data center's cooling and stormwater design is therefore a federally reviewable airfield question — the one plume-adjacent risk that already has a codified number."

4. **On the MWAA precedent:** "MWAA bought 854 acres to hold open a fourth runway, then sold 424 of them to Digital Realty for $236.5 million to lower Dulles's cost per enplanement. The compatibility review that approved it never modeled a plume or a generator. That is the outcome this thesis is trying to prevent from happening blind."

5. **On advisory vs. binding:** "The FAA's own 2006 Safety Risk Analysis called plume overflight risk 'insignificant,' while AIM 7-6-16 tells pilots to avoid plumes entirely. When federal guidance points both ways, the airport's lease covenant — not the FAA — is the enforceable standard."

## 5. What Can and Cannot Move — and On What Clock

**Cannot move on MWAA's timeline:**
- **§743's narrowed FAA jurisdiction** — fixed until at least the next reauthorization (post-2028). Plan as if the FAA will *not* be the safety net.
- **Absence of a codified plume/EMI obstruction standard** — the FAA has declined this for a decade. Do not wait for a number that isn't coming.
- **The revenue-diversification incentive** — federal revenue-use rules and airline CPE pressure structurally push toward monetizing idle land. This is durable.

**Can move — because they are the operator's own instruments — and the decisions they attach to:**
- **The ground lease / use-and-lease covenant window.** This is where a plume ceiling, generator-runtime limits, dry-basin stormwater design, lighting spec, and an EMI-monitoring obligation become *binding*. Miss this window and no later review recovers it.
- **The ALP amendment / land-release decision.** The moment non-aeronautical use is approved is the moment Grant Assurance 21 compatibility is formally judged. Require independent compatibility evidence *as a condition of ALP concurrence*, before the lease is signed.
- **The §743 45-day notification.** When MWAA notifies the FAA of the project, framing a credible "materially impacts safe operation of aircraft" airside case is what keeps FAA jurisdiction alive. Silence forfeits it in 45 days.
- **The future-airfield-capacity decision.** Whether these acres are ever needed for a runway, taxiway, or de-ice pad is a board judgment the compatibility review must force to the surface — MWAA has already traded this optionality once.

**The strategist's takeaway:** the regulation here is thinner than the operator assumes, and the politics run against caution. That combination means the airport cannot outsource this judgment to the FAA. The thesis is right for a reason economists undersell — not because the physics is uncertain, but because *no one else is required to check.*

---
*Distinguishing note: "This is regulation" — Part 77 notice, AC 150/5200-33C wildlife separations, Grant Assurance 21 land-use approval at ALP change, NEPA review triggered by FAA action. "This is politics" — §743's deliberate narrowing of FAA authority, the revenue-use incentive to monetize land, and the local tax-base gravity that makes "yes" the path of least resistance. The first set you comply with. The second set you plan around, because it will not change on any timeline this project runs on.*
