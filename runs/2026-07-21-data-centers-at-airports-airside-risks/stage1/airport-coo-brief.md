# Stage 1 Brief — Airport COO

**Run:** Data Centers at Airports: Airside Risks
**Lens:** Chief Operating Officer, large US hub. Written from the duty-desk chair, not the planning studio. This is the operator's read on whether a data center near the airfield actually costs us something on a Monday morning — and where the thesis is right, where it overreaches, and where it is dangerously incomplete.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **The thesis is directionally correct and the operator already lives it.** At MWAA this is not hypothetical: Dulles sits inside the largest data-center concentration on earth. Loudoun County alone runs roughly 26 million square feet of data center today, with a proposed Dulles Cloud South adding as much as 56 million more before Prince William supervisors rejected it in July 2026 [Source: https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/loudoun-county-is-home-to-26-million-sq-ft-of-data-centers-dulles-cloud-south-could-add-another-56-million-sq-ft-if-it-passes/]. The buildings are already there. The question is not whether to allow them; it is what we make the next one prove.

2. **The controlling scenario is prolonged emergency generation, not normal cooling.** On a normal day a modern data center is a quiet box that moves warm air. The operator's worst case is a regional grid outage where a fleet of diesel generators runs for days. A single campus can hold 40 to 138 generators producing up to ~345 MW, each unit 1–3 MW [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/08/qts-aurora-data-center-diesel-generators/]. Non-emergency testing is capped near 100 hours/year, but federal rules put **no time limit** on run hours during a declared emergency [Source: https://backuppower.ai/data-centers/epa-100-hour-rule/]. That is the plume that matters, the smoke that matters, and the ARFF call that matters.

3. **The plume risk is real but narrow, and it is not the operator's biggest problem.** FAA guidance is explicit that thermal exhaust plumes can produce turbulence extending **over 1,000 feet above the stack**, worst in calm, cold, stable air, and can cause airframe damage, aircraft upset, and engine damage — the phases of flight that matter are exactly approach and departure [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html]. But data-center evaporative and dry coolers are low-velocity heat sources compared with a power-plant stack. The plume is a real check-item, not the headline. The operator should not let a vivid, low-probability turbulence story crowd out the boring, high-probability problems.

4. **Wildlife and stormwater are the higher-frequency operational hit.** Data-center campuses bring acres of impervious roof and large stormwater ponds. FAA's hazardous-wildlife guidance is unambiguous: detention basins must go dry within 48 hours and be steep-sided and lined to avoid becoming bird habitat, and land uses that attract hazardous wildlife are discouraged within 5,000 ft (piston) / 10,000 ft (turbine) of the air operations area and 5 statute miles of approach corridors [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf]. A poorly designed pond three miles off the end of the runway is a bird strike waiting to be written up. This is where design decisions made by people who never work the airfield come back as our problem.

5. **The real strategic loss is capacity sterilization, and current reviews do not test for it.** The obstruction-surface check asks whether a building pokes into today's Part 77 or approach surfaces. It does not ask whether a 100-foot building freezes the land and airspace we would need for a future runway, a displaced threshold, a new approach, or a lower-visibility procedure. Once a nine-figure data hall is energized, it is effectively permanent. The operator's objection is not "it's too tall today"; it is "it forecloses the airfield we might need in 2050." The thesis is strongest precisely where it says the test should preserve **future** airfield capacity, not just clear current surfaces.

6. **The operator controls far less of this than the strategy deck assumes.** The airport controls its own property and its input to the FAA obstruction process; it does not control off-airport zoning. Loudoun, Fairfax, and Prince William approve the land use; FAA makes the airspace determination on Form 7460; the developer controls cooling and generator design. On the parcels that matter most — the ones off the airport fence but under the approach — the operator is a commenter, not a decider, unless it forces the issue up front [Source: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/loudoun-county-virginia-eliminates-by-right-data-center-development].

7. **Emergency response is a standing tax the pro-forma never shows.** A large generator farm with substantial on-site diesel storage is a fixed hazmat and fire load inside our mutual-aid footprint. ARFF and the local fire service inherit it. Nothing in the data center's revenue model funds our incident response, and the emergency-generation scenario that stresses air quality also stresses our people.

## 2. Evidence

**Scale, in our own backyard.** Loudoun County hosts on the order of 26 million square feet of operating data center, concentrated in Ashburn/Sterling directly under and adjacent to Dulles operations; the rejected Dulles Cloud South proposal would have roughly tripled a single corridor's footprint [Source: https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/loudoun-county-is-home-to-26-million-sq-ft-of-data-centers-dulles-cloud-south-could-add-another-56-million-sq-ft-if-it-passes/]. City Journal's reporting describes buildings "nearly 100 feet tall with massive electrical substations beside them" lining the county [Source: https://www.city-journal.org/article/loudoun-county-virginia-data-centers-construction]. New Dominion transmission monopoles up to 185 feet are going in near the Dulles-area substations to feed them [Source: https://www.wusa9.com/article/news/local/virginia/data-centers-ashburn-loudoun-dominion-energy-virginia-state-corportation-commission/65-f080cdfe-fb78-4040-a964-cf155c07a0b7]. The linear infrastructure — lines, poles, substations — is itself an obstruction and a maintenance-access question, not just the halls.

**Plume physics and the FAA's own framework.** FAA has treated thermal exhaust plumes as a distinct hazard the standard obstruction review misses: the Airport Obstruction Standards Committee found that no FAA regulation protects against plume effects (only physical stack height is regulated), and that plumes near airports "may pose a unique hazard to aircraft in critical phases of flight" [Source: https://www.aopa.org/-/media/Files/AOPA/Home/News/All-News/2015/FINAL2--AOSC-Position-Paper-Exhaust-Plumes.pdf]. FAA contracted MITRE to build an Exhaust-Plume-Analyzer model and publishes a Technical Guidance Assessment Tool for thermal exhaust plume impact through its airport environmental/land-use office [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf]. The AIM tells pilots turbulence can extend over 1,000 ft above a stack or cooling tower, is worst in calm stable hot conditions, and can cause upset and engine damage [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html]. Translation for the operator: a tool exists, so "we couldn't have known" is not a defense. If we don't require the plume analysis, that's a choice.

**Generators — the scenario that controls.** Individual data-center gensets run 1–3 MW; campuses field dozens to well over a hundred, with one Colorado site seeking 98 additional units on top of 40 for ~345 MW total [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/08/qts-aurora-data-center-diesel-generators/]. Non-emergency operation is generally capped near 100 hours/year, but there is no federal time cap during an actual emergency, and PSD air permitting triggers only above 250 tons/year of NOx or CO [Source: https://backuppower.ai/data-centers/epa-100-hour-rule/]. The operator reads that as: normal-day emissions are permitted-small and low-velocity; the tail-risk day — extended grid failure, all units running — is when exhaust volume, thermal output, visible haze, and fire/hazmat load all peak simultaneously. That is the scenario to design against and the one the developer's "typical operation" narrative will understate.

**Wildlife and stormwater.** AC 150/5200-33C requires detention basins engineered to drain within 48 hours and to stay dry between storms, favors steep-sided, rip-rap or concrete-lined, narrow linear basins, and — where ponds can't be moved — bird balls, wire grids, or netting; it sets separation guidance of 5,000 ft / 10,000 ft / 5 statute miles depending on aircraft and approach geometry [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf]. Large campuses with amenity ponds, wet basins, or slow-draining detention are exactly the attractant profile the AC warns against, and they sit within those distances of Dulles.

**Governance reality.** Loudoun eliminated by-right data-center development in 2025, meaning each project now runs a discretionary land-use approval — a real intervention point, but one held by the county, not the airport [Source: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/loudoun-county-virginia-eliminates-by-right-data-center-development]. Federally, anything over 200 ft AGL, or within the notice surfaces near a runway, triggers FAA Form 7460 and the OE/AAA airspace analysis [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Form/FAA_Form_7460-1_042023.pdf]. The obstruction process is about penetration of surfaces; it does not natively evaluate plume, glare, wildlife, or future-capacity sterilization unless someone forces those studies into scope.

## 3. The operational case — for and against the thesis

**Where the COO backs the thesis, hard.**

The thesis says the test should be affirmative compatibility evidence, not mere absence of obstruction. From the operator's chair that is simply correct, for one reason the strategist may underweight: **the obstruction check is a snapshot and the airfield is a fifty-year asset.** A building that clears today's surfaces can still foreclose the runway extension, the parallel taxiway, or the lower-minimums approach we haven't designed yet. Nobody at the duty desk can un-build an energized data hall. Requiring the developer to prove compatibility — plume, wildlife, stormwater, lighting, and airspace-preservation — shifts the burden and the cost to the party that profits, which is where it belongs. It also gives the operator documented standing to say "no" or "not there," which today we largely lack off-airport.

Second, the thesis's instinct to separate the visible from the aerodynamic is exactly the operator's discipline. Visible vapor from an evaporative cooler is a nuisance and a glare/ceiling-obscuration question. Aerodynamic turbulence is a flight-safety question. They are not the same, they don't peak in the same weather, and conflating them gets you laughed out of the room by both the pilots and the developer's engineers. The run's guardrail — don't conflate vapor, exhaust, heat haze, and turbulence — is the right one.

**Where the COO pushes back.**

The thesis risks over-indexing on the plume — the cinematic risk — when the operator's recurring pain is duller and more frequent: birds off a stormwater pond, a de-icing-season ceiling obscured by cooler vapor, a generator-farm hazmat load, and above all the quiet permanent loss of airfield optionality. If this becomes "a report about thermal turbulence," it will be technically interesting and operationally beside the point. The controlling scenario is prolonged emergency generation and the controlling **strategic** loss is capacity sterilization. Lead with those.

The thesis also assumes a lever the operator mostly doesn't hold. On the parcels that matter — off-fence, under the approach — we are a commenter to a county and an input to an FAA determination. If the recommendation is "the airport should require studies," it has to be honest that the airport can *require* that only on its own land and can only *advocate* for it elsewhere, through the 7460 process, ALP protections, and intergovernmental agreements. A recommendation that pretends the COO can veto a Loudoun parcel will fail in execution and everyone in the ops center knows it.

Finally, honesty cuts both ways: most of these risks are controllable by design. Dry coolers instead of wet, lined fast-draining basins instead of amenity ponds, non-specular roofing, shielded downward lighting, generator siting and stack design informed by the FAA plume tool, and enforceable operating limits. The genuinely *location-dependent*, un-mitigable residual is the airspace/future-capacity foreclosure. That is the hill to plant the flag on.

## 4. Concrete examples that frame the thesis

1. **Dulles Cloud South (Prince William, rejected July 2026).** A proposal to add up to ~56 million square feet adjacent to an airport that already sits in the world's densest data-center cluster [Source: https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/loudoun-county-is-home-to-26-million-sq-ft-of-data-centers-dulles-cloud-south-could-add-another-56-million-sq-ft-if-it-passes/]. It died on local land-use politics, not on an aviation-compatibility finding. That is the tell: the airport's airside interest was not the deciding instrument. If the next one passes the county, the operator needs its compatibility test already on the record.

2. **The 185-foot transmission monopoles feeding the Dulles-area substations.** Approved despite backyard opposition, up to 185 ft, threading toward substations near the airport [Source: https://www.wusa9.com/article/news/local/virginia/data-centers-ashburn-loudoun-dominion-energy-virginia-state-corportation-commission/65-f080cdfe-fb78-4040-a964-cf155c07a0b7]. This is the capital-handoff pattern the operator knows cold: the halls get the scrutiny, and the linear infrastructure that actually penetrates surfaces and complicates approach protection slides through on a separate track.

3. **The Colorado 138-generator campus (~345 MW).** The clean illustration of the controlling scenario — dozens of megawatts of diesel that sit idle on a normal day and become the operative hazard during an extended grid event, with no federal cap on emergency run hours [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/08/qts-aurora-data-center-diesel-generators/][Source: https://backuppower.ai/data-centers/epa-100-hour-rule/]. Design and permit reviews that model "normal cooling" miss the day that stresses the airport.

4. **The stormwater pond three miles off the threshold.** Not a single named site but the recurring operator's nightmare: an amenity or slow-draining basin inside the 5-statute-mile approach zone, drawing gulls and waterfowl, in direct tension with AC 150/5200-33C's 48-hour-dry, lined-basin guidance [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf]. Cheap to prevent at design; expensive and permanent once graded and planted.

5. **The FAA thermal-plume tool that exists and often goes unused.** FAA's MITRE-derived Exhaust-Plume-Analyzer and the land-use assessment tool are available at no cost [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf][Source: https://www.aopa.org/-/media/Files/AOPA/Home/News/All-News/2015/FINAL2--AOSC-Position-Paper-Exhaust-Plumes.pdf]. The operator's screening ask is simple and defensible: if you propose a significant heat source under our approach, you run the FAA's own model and show us the result. That converts a vague worry into a pass/fail the airport can put in the record.

## 5. What the operator would put in a screening framework (MWAA-usable)

Require, on any data center within the 5-statute-mile approach/departure zone or penetrating Part 77 notice surfaces:

- **Airspace + future-capacity test:** 7460/OE-AAA plus an ALP-referenced showing that the site does not foreclose any reserved future runway, approach, or procedure. This is the non-mitigable residual — treat it as pass/fail.
- **Thermal plume analysis** using FAA's own tool, evaluated under worst-case calm/cold/stable conditions, for the peak generation scenario, not normal cooling.
- **Wildlife/stormwater compliance** to AC 150/5200-33C: lined, fast-draining basins; no amenity ponds inside the separation distances; documented drain-down.
- **Emergency-generation scenario** modeled explicitly: full-fleet, multi-day run — exhaust, visible haze, and hazmat/fire load — with enforceable operating and reporting commitments and a cost-share for airport ARFF/EM burden.
- **Lighting and glare** to downward-shielded, non-specular standards to protect approach visual environment.

The through-line for the CEO and the strategist: the operator's objection is not to data centers. It is to a review that ends at the obstruction surface. Make the developer prove compatibility and preserve the airfield we might need in 2050 — because the one thing the duty desk can never do is un-build the box after it's energized.
