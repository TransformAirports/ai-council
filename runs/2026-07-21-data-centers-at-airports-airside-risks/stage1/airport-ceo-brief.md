# Airport CEO Brief — Data Centers Near the Airfield: The Financial-and-Political Case

**Run:** Data Centers at Airports: Airside Risks
**Lens:** The chief executive's chair — bond covenants, airline agreements, board votes, 30-year amortization
**Prepared for:** Stage 2 Strategist

---

## Framing: why this thesis is a CEO problem, not a planning problem

The technical panel on this run will litigate thermal plumes, generator exhaust, and Part 77 surfaces. That is the right work, and it is not my work. From this chair, a data center near the airfield is a **land-monetization decision with a 30-to-50-year tail**, and the airside-risk question is really a question about **option value**: if I lease this parcel to a hyperscaler, what future aeronautical capacity do I foreclose, what do I owe my signatory airlines, what do I promise the rating agencies, and can the decision survive a chair transition and an FAA compliance review?

The trap is that the revenue is real, immediate, and politically intoxicating, while the cost — foreclosed runway or taxiway geometry, an operational hazard credited to my approval, a grant-assurance finding — arrives years later, on someone else's watch, and is nearly impossible to unwind. A CEO who books a premium ground lease this quarter and loses 200 acres of future airfield expansion, or a Grant Assurance 19/22 finding, has made a bad trade in the only currency that matters at this level. That is the argument the thesis is really making, and it is correct.

---

## 1. Key Findings (for a board chair to absorb in one read)

1. **The airside screen the thesis proposes is not anti-revenue — it is the discipline that protects the revenue.** A data center that clears current obstruction surfaces but degrades a future runway or triggers a wildlife/EMI/emergency-response problem is a lease we cannot exit and a hazard credited to *our* approval. The screen is cheap insurance against an un-hedgeable liability.

2. **Land monetization is the single most rating-agency-friendly move an airport can make — until it isn't.** Moody's, S&P, Fitch, and KBRA reward non-aeronautical revenue diversification, liquidity, and lower cost-per-enplanement. A well-structured data-center ground lease improves every headline metric. But the same agencies penalize concentration, contingent liabilities, and any signal that the airport traded its core franchise — aviation capacity — for a real-estate check.

3. **Under a residual agreement, the airlines effectively own the upside — and the veto.** At MWAA, the Digital Dulles proceeds were directed to *reduce airline rental costs and fees* [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers]. That tells you who the counterparty really is on any land deal at a residual hub: the signatory carriers. They will support revenue that lowers their cost per enplanement and resist anything that consumes capital or airfield they consider theirs by agreement.

4. **Grant Assurance 24 forces fair-market rent; the Revenue Use Policy makes a sweetheart data-center deal a federal offense.** Non-aeronautical tenants must pay fair market value; a below-market lease is one of four recognized forms of revenue diversion [Source: https://transportation.org/audit/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2024/05/Revenue-Use-Presentation_August-23-2023.ppt.pdf]. The political pressure to land a marquee hyperscaler at a discount is exactly the pressure that produces an FAA finding.

5. **The federal calendar does not move on the developer's schedule.** FAA Reauthorization runs only through FY2028, PFC is still capped at $4.50 (frozen since 2000), AIP sits at $4.0B/year, and the IIJA supplemental airport grants expire September 30, 2026 [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791]. Any thesis that assumes federal cover for the airside side of a data-center approval is betting on authorizations that are not secured.

6. **Governance shapes what is survivable.** A 17-member, politically appointed board with six-year staggered terms and plenary authority (MWAA: 7 Virginia, 4 D.C., 3 Presidential, plus Maryland) means a land decision must survive a chair transition and a change in the appointing governor [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/about-authority/board-directors]. A deal that needs one champion is a deal that dies when the champion rotates off.

7. **The controlling risk from this chair is not the plume — it is irreversibility.** Every other operating risk on this run can be mitigated, monitored, or restricted. Foreclosed airfield capacity cannot. That asymmetry, not any single engineering finding, is what should govern the decision.

## 2. Evidence

### The bond-rating lens

Rating agencies evaluate airport revenue credits on a consistent spine: enplanement trend, **airline concentration risk**, cost per enplanement (CPE), **debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)**, **days cash on hand**, and the legal strength of the revenue pledge and rate covenant [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/info/data/credit-ratings]. Most large hubs sit A-to-AA on the senior lien; recent upgrades (SFO to Aa3 by Moody's in November 2025; San Diego to Aa3 senior in December 2024) were driven by liquidity and enplanement recovery [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/orlando-international-airport-upgraded-by-moodys-fitch-and-kbra].

A data-center ground lease touches this profile in three ways:

- **Positive:** durable, investment-grade-tenant, inflation-escalated non-aeronautical revenue lowers CPE and lifts coverage and days-cash — the exact metrics that produced 13 airport upgrades since 2024 [Source: https://www.stlouis-mo.gov/government/departments/airport/news/fitch-ratings-issues-positive-outlook-for-airport-revenue-bonds.cfm]. This is the strongest financial argument *for* the deal.
- **Neutral-to-watch:** if proceeds are one-time (a land sale, as at Dulles) rather than a recurring lease, agencies treat them as non-recurring and give limited credit to the operating profile.
- **Negative:** a contingent liability — a lease that constrains a future runway the airlines and FAA expect, or a hazard that invites litigation — is precisely the kind of qualitative overhang that caps a rating even when the numbers look good. Agencies penalize the *story* of an airport that mortgaged its aviation franchise.

**Net effect on the next bond issuance:** a clean, fair-market, recurring data-center lease is accretive to the all-in cost of capital. A contested one, or one that airlines oppose under a Majority-in-Interest fight, introduces headline and litigation risk that widens spreads regardless of the coverage math.

### Airline use-and-lease structure

The counterparty on any land decision is defined by the rate methodology. Under **residual** agreements, non-aeronautical profits offset aeronautical costs — the airlines get the upside as lower rates — and these agreements run longer and are structured to give bondholders added security [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-rate-methodologies]. Under **compensatory**, the airport keeps non-aeronautical profit and bills airlines full aeronautical cost.

This distinction determines the politics of a data-center deal:

- At a **residual** hub (MWAA's Dulles history fits the pattern — proceeds went to reduce airline costs), the carriers are *aligned* with revenue that lowers CPE but hold a **Majority-in-Interest (MII)** veto over capital projects that raise rates [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/14482/chapter/2]. MII clauses let signatory airlines controlling a majority of landed weight review, accept, or reject capital projects [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-agreements]. A data center itself is usually not an airline-rate-base capital project — but any airfield mitigation, relocated infrastructure, or future capacity the deal disturbs *is*, and that is where MII bites.
- Hub carriers at concentrated airports command 70%+ shares and translate that into gate control, rate preferences, and capital-project approval power [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/aip/airport-business-practices-and-their-impact-on-airline-competition.pdf]. **The carrier that dominates your hub holds the leverage on any land decision that touches the airfield.**

The strategic point: the next use-and-lease renegotiation window is where a land decision is actually adjudicated. A CEO who books a data-center lease but hands the dominant carrier a grievance to weaponize in the next agreement has lost more than the lease is worth.

### The federal stack

Four federal levers sit above this decision, each on its own calendar:

- **Grant assurances.** Assurance 24 (self-sustaining rates) requires fair market value from non-aeronautical tenants; a below-market lease is revenue diversion [Source: https://transportation.org/audit/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2024/05/Revenue-Use-Presentation_August-23-2023.ppt.pdf]. Assurances 19 (operation and maintenance), 21 (compatible land use), and 22 (economic nondiscrimination) all bear on whether a data center is a permissible use of obligated land.
- **Land-use change approval.** Even where the FAA lacks direct approval authority over a land-use change, all statutory and grant-assurance obligations remain in force, and proceeds from federally acquired or conveyed land are restricted [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airports/article/53082175/navigating-airport-land-use-regulations-to-grow-nonaeronautical-revenue]. The FAA has blocked airport land dispositions it judged inconsistent with these obligations (e.g., the Sarasota/New College sale) [Source: https://www.wusf.org/politics-issues/2024-04-24/new-college-sarasota-airport-land-sale-seven-reasons-faa-block].
- **Airport Layout Plan (ALP).** Any parcel on the ALP requires FAA concurrence to change use; the Manassas parcel was flagged as potentially carrying "FAA restrictions on proceeds allocation" precisely because of ALP status [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].
- **Funding calendar.** FAA Reauthorization (P.L. 118-63) runs through FY2028; PFC frozen at $4.50; AIP at $4.0B/year FY25–28; IIJA supplemental airport grants expire September 30, 2026 [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791]. If a deal's airfield mitigation depends on federal grant eligibility, the window is narrow and the money is oversubscribed.

**Where the thesis depends on unsecured federal cover:** any assumption that the FAA will bless a data center inside an ALP-designated aeronautical reserve, or that grant-funded mitigation is available, is not a settled fact — it is an application with a multi-year, uncertain outcome.

### Board governance

MWAA is governed by a 17-member board: 7 appointed by the Governor of Virginia, 4 by the D.C. Mayor, 3 by the President, plus Maryland representation; terms run six years with one reappointment; the board elects its chair annually and holds plenary authority [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/about-authority/board-directors]. This is an **independent operating authority**, not a city department — it can issue its own revenue debt, hold its own land, and set its own policy, which is exactly why a land decision here is durable and hard to reverse once made.

The governance implication for this thesis: with six-year staggered terms and appointments split across two governors, a mayor, and the President, **no single appointing authority controls the board, and no chair serves indefinitely.** A land decision that depends on the current chair's relationships will not survive the rotation. The screen the thesis proposes is, among other things, a way to make the decision *institutionally* rather than *personally* — to give the next chair a defensible record rather than an inherited fight.

The distinction matters more broadly: an **operating authority** (MWAA, PANYNJ) can move on land and debt with autonomy; an **airport district** answers to an elected board with its own politics; a **city department** (e.g., a mayor's aviation department) is subject to council appropriation and procurement, and cannot commit the way an authority can. What is survivable at one is impossible at another.

### The host region

The political economy here is unusually charged because MWAA sits in **the largest data-center market on earth** — Northern Virginia's "Data Center Alley." The demand is local, the developers are sophisticated, and the precedents are already set on adjacent public land:

- **Digital Dulles:** Digital Realty bought 433 acres from MWAA for $236.5M in 2018, netting ~$207M, with proceeds directed to reduce airline costs [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].
- **Manassas Regional Airport:** Amazon Data Services bought 20 acres of city land for $27.72M in December 2023, projected at $4–6M annual revenue [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].

The alliances write themselves: the county tax base, the utility, the developer, and — under a residual structure — the airlines who see lower CPE. The organized opposition is quieter but real: the future-capacity constituency (the FAA, the master-plan engineers, and any carrier planning growth), community groups on power/water/noise, and the ratepayer/environmental interests that have made data-center power demand a live political issue in Virginia. **This thesis creates a coalition for the check and a coalition for the caution, and the CEO's job is to make sure the caution coalition is heard *before* the ink dries, not in litigation after.**

### The CEO's calendar as evidence

The large-hub CEO role is dominated by capital, government affairs, and airline relations, not operations — that is delegated to the COO and the AOC. ACI-NA's Large Hub Committee frames the CEO agenda as funding, regulatory flexibility, passenger facilitation, and commercial issues [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/committees-programs/large-airports/], against a stated $173.9B five-year infrastructure need [Source: https://airportscouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2025-Infrastructure-Needs-Study.pdf]. A land deal that generates non-aeronautical revenue to close that gap is *exactly* what peer CEOs are spending time on — which is precisely why the airside discipline has to be imposed from the top, because everyone's incentive points toward closing the deal.

### Long-tenure strategic patterns (2000–2025)

- **Got right:** aggressive non-aeronautical land monetization where it did not compromise the airfield. Reason Foundation valued long-term leases of 31 U.S. airports at ~$131B, evidence of how much latent value sits in airport real estate [Source: https://reason.org/policy-study/study-leasing-31-us-airports-would-generate-131-billion-to-fund-other-infrastructure-and-pay-debt/]. CEOs who diversified revenue and protected liquidity earned the upgrades of the 2020s.
- **Got wrong:** decisions that looked obvious at the time and foreclosed capacity — land committed to non-aviation use inside future airfield footprints, or deals struck below fair market value that produced FAA revenue-diversion findings. The pattern is consistent: the reversible mistakes were survivable; the irreversible ones defined careers.

## 3. The financial-and-political case, as the CEO sees it

**For approving (and for imposing the screen):**
- Recurring, investment-grade, escalated non-aeronautical revenue lowers CPE, lifts coverage and days-cash, and reduces the all-in cost of the next bond issuance — the metrics that drive ratings [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/info/data/credit-ratings].
- Under a residual structure, the airlines are aligned because the revenue lowers their rates — the Dulles precedent proves the mechanism [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].
- The regional demand is unmatched; refusing outright cedes value to Manassas, Loudoun, and Prince William with no aviation benefit.
- **The screen is what makes the "for" case defensible:** it converts a personal bet into an institutional record that survives a chair transition and an FAA audit.

**Against (or for hard conditions):**
- **Irreversibility.** A 30-to-50-year lease inside or adjacent to future airfield geometry cannot be unwound; no quarter of NAR growth offsets a foreclosed runway.
- **Grant-assurance exposure.** Below-market terms or incompatible land use invites a revenue-diversion or Assurance 21/22 finding — a political and financial event, not a paperwork one [Source: https://transportation.org/audit/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2024/05/Revenue-Use-Presentation_August-23-2023.ppt.pdf].
- **MII and the next agreement.** A deal that gives the dominant carrier a grievance becomes leverage in the next use-and-lease renegotiation, where the real money is decided [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/14482/chapter/2].
- **Federal calendar risk.** Any reliance on FAA land-use concurrence or grant-funded mitigation is an unsecured bet on a slow, oversubscribed process [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791].

**The CEO's verdict:** approve *conditionally, or not at all* — never approve on the strength of "it clears current obstruction surfaces." The controlling question is whether independent evidence demonstrates compatibility *and* preserves future capacity. That is the thesis, and from this chair it is not a planning nicety; it is fiduciary.

## 4. Data points and precedents a strategist can use

1. **Digital Dulles:** 433 acres, $236.5M sale (2018), ~$207M net to MWAA, proceeds applied to reduce airline costs — the on-property precedent that proves both the revenue upside and the residual-agreement dynamic at the exact authority making this decision [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].
2. **Manassas / Amazon:** 20 acres, $27.72M (Dec 2023), $4–6M projected annual revenue — the neighboring-airport comp that quantifies per-acre value and regional demand [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].
3. **Grant Assurance 24 + Revenue Use Policy:** non-aeronautical tenants must pay fair market value; a below-market lease is one of four recognized forms of revenue diversion — the bright line a marquee deal is most likely to cross [Source: https://transportation.org/audit/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2024/05/Revenue-Use-Presentation_August-23-2023.ppt.pdf].
4. **Federal calendar:** FAA Reauthorization through FY2028, PFC frozen at $4.50 since 2000, AIP $4.0B/year, IIJA airport supplemental expires 9/30/2026 — the authorization horizon any federally-dependent element of the deal must clear [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791].
5. **MWAA governance:** 17 members (7 VA / 4 DC / 3 Presidential / + MD), six-year terms with one reappointment, plenary authority — the reason a land decision must be built to survive a chair rotation, not a single champion [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/about-authority/board-directors].

## Documented vs. widely-known-but-unofficial

- **Documented:** the Digital Dulles and Manassas transactions and figures; grant-assurance and revenue-use rules; MWAA board composition; the federal funding calendar; rating-agency methodology spine and recent upgrades.
- **Widely known but unofficial:** that residual hubs credit land proceeds back to airlines is documented at Dulles specifically but is treated here as the general pattern; MWAA's precise current rate methodology and MII thresholds are governed by the confidential signatory agreement and are not fully public. The claim that CEOs' incentives structurally bias toward closing land deals is an industry judgment, not a cited fact. Treat the "controlling risk is irreversibility" framing as the CEO's argument, not an FAA finding.
