# Key Findings

- **Thermal plumes can reach aircraft altitudes:** Hot exhaust from cooling towers or generators can create turbulence and vertical wind shear extending over *1,000 feet* above a source ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=Whether%20plumes%20are%20visible%20or,or%20size%20of%20the%20plume)). FAA guidance warns that low-altitude encounters with such plumes (especially in calm, cold air) have caused airframe damage, upsets, and even engine failures ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=facilities%2C%20or%20other%20industrial%20systems,corridors%20or%20airport%20traffic%20areas)). Smaller aircraft are affected at higher altitudes than heavy jets ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=more%20pronounced%20in%20calm%20stable,plumes%20by%20flying%20on%20the)).

- **Plume hazards are critical on approach and departure:** Exhaust plumes pose the greatest danger during takeoff and landing when aircraft are near the ground ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=to%20gaseous%20oxides%2C%20and%2For%20icing,is%20very%20hot%20and%20the)). FAA’s _Aeronautical Information Manual_ explicitly cautions pilots to avoid visible or invisible plumes around airports because of turbulence, reduced visibility, oxygen depletion, icing, and particulate ingestion risks ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=visible%20or%20invisible%20emissions%20from,and%20cold%20air%2C%20especially%20in)). Studies cited by FAA show significant turbulence from an exhaust stack can persist out to *3–5 km* (2–3 miles) from the source in calm conditions ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)).

- **Data centers emit enormous heat and moisture:** A large “hyperscale” data center (30–50 MW IT load) rejects on the order of *30–100 MW* of waste heat continuously. Many use evaporative cooling towers, each of which can release *hundreds of gallons of water per minute* as warm vapor ([www.potomacriver.org](https://www.potomacriver.org/focus-areas/water-resources-and-drinking-water/water-resources/planning/data-centers-and-water-use-in-the-potomac-river-basin/#:~:text=range%20between%20100,use)). At peak demand, data center cooling water use can reach *8,500 gallons per day per megawatt* ([www.potomacriver.org](https://www.potomacriver.org/focus-areas/water-resources-and-drinking-water/water-resources/planning/data-centers-and-water-use-in-the-potomac-river-basin/#:~:text=match%20at%20L165%20range%20between,use)) – meaning a 30 MW facility might evaporate ~255,000 gallons per day into the air. This can produce persistent **visible plumes** in cool or humid weather, resembling power-plant cooling tower clouds.

- **Visible vapor plumes can impair flight operations:** The FAA notes that plume moisture can condense into clouds or icing conditions ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=directed%20unstable%20gases%20%28effluent%29,total%20extent%20of%20their%20turbulent)). A concentrated cooling-tower plume can create a localized fog bank or ice crystals aloft, reducing pilot visibility and potentially icing aircraft surfaces or engines ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=facilities%2C%20or%20other%20industrial%20systems,corridors%20or%20airport%20traffic%20areas)). For example, power-plant cooling towers have generated clouds that drift into approach paths in cold weather, prompting pilot advisories ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=significant%20air%20disturbances%20such%20as,heights%20of%20over%201%2C000%20feet)). Data center plumes, while smaller, could similarly produce short-range visibility hazards if located under an approach corridor.

- **Diesel generators present intermittent but intense emissions:** Unlike commercial power plants, data centers run backup **diesel generators** primarily during tests or outages. A single large data center campus may deploy dozens of high-capacity gen-sets (e.g. 20–50 units of 2–3 MW each) to cover its load. During a grid failure, hundreds of thousands of horsepower of diesel engines could start simultaneously, emitting hot exhaust and particulates equivalent to a medium power station. FAA warns that engine exhaust plumes can contain gaseous oxides and particulates that contaminate turbine intakes ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=directed%20unstable%20gases%20%28effluent%29,total%20extent%20of%20their%20turbulent)). While brief, a mass generator startup could momentarily reduce local air quality and visibility near runways (diesel smoke opacity can spike on start-up ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most))), and the hot exhaust could contribute to thermal turbulence.

- **Light and electromagnetic interference are manageable but real:** Data center campuses are often brightly lit and packed with electrical equipment. Bright security lighting or LED facades can cause glare or pilot distraction if not shielded. FAA regulations do not allow any light source that could be mistaken for aeronautical beacons or that impairs pilot vision on approach ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=altitudes,altered%2C%20or%20removed%2C%20has%20an)) ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=proposed%20structures,does%20not%20apply%20to%20VFR)). Similarly, large electrical systems must comply with FCC/FAA rules to prevent **electromagnetic interference**: while server equipment is well-shielded, the sheer scale of power conversion (high-voltage transformers, switchgear, miles of cable) raises the potential for radio-frequency noise or even disturbance to instrument landing systems if poorly sited. For instance, large metal structures or power lines can distort Instrument Landing System signals or radar – FAA’s obstruction evaluation process explicitly checks for line-of-sight or **electromagnetic interference** with nav aids ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=proposed%20visual%20flight%20rule%20,to%20have%20physical%20or%20electromagnetic)). Any on-airport data center would require technical analysis to ensure no degradation of radar, ILS, or communications.

- **Wildlife attraction and stormwater hazards:** Data centers can inadvertently attract wildlife hazardous to aircraft. The big culprit is water: sites often include stormwater ponds or use water-intensive cooling. Open retention basins or discharge streams can draw birds (waterfowl, wading birds) and other wildlife. FAA guidance recommends against new wildlife attractants within *10,000 feet* of airports serving turbine aircraft (double the 5,000 ft limit for piston-only fields) ([www.jetwhine.com](https://www.jetwhine.com/blog/airport-circular-is-wildlife-nimby-guide/#:~:text=distances,that%20at%20a%20turbine%20airport)). Even *temporary standing water* on-site after rain can increase bird strike risk. Moreover, warm water outputs or heat from equipment could concentrate insects (a food source for birds and bats) or provide cozy roosting spots. Airports receiving federal grants must assure that land uses will not increase wildlife hazards (Grant Assurance 19, relating to safe airport operations). A data center project near runways would likely need a wildlife hazard assessment and a stormwater management plan designed to **eliminate standing water**, via underground tanks or rapid drainage, to comply with FAA’s 5,000–10,000 ft rule ([www.jetwhine.com](https://www.jetwhine.com/blog/airport-circular-is-wildlife-nimby-guide/#:~:text=distances,that%20at%20a%20turbine%20airport)).

- **Emergency scenarios drive worst-case risks:** Under **normal operations** (grid power on, cooling systems active), a data center continuously emits heat and some water vapor, but no combustion exhaust. In this state, the dominant risk to aircraft is thermal turbulence and any visible moisture plume. **Peak cooling days** (e.g. hottest summer afternoons) push maximum heat rejection, yet paradoxically may produce smaller plumes because hot ambient air can hold more moisture (less visible fog) but still create rising heat thermals. The **most critical scenario** is a cold, still day with full IT load: the large temperature delta causes strong updrafts and dense condensation plumes that rise high into approach paths ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=turbulent%20effects%20of%20an%20exhaust,a%20higher%20altitude%20than%20heavier)). By contrast, **emergency power** operations (during a blackout or monthly generator tests) add intense combustion plumes. A full-load emergency run could elevate the combined exhaust temperature and velocity beyond normal HVAC output. Notably, any **crosswind** will dilute and bend plumes – even a light 5–10 knot wind can significantly dissipate thermal effects ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=turbulent%20effects%20of%20an%20exhaust,a%20higher%20altitude%20than%20heavier)). Thus, the absolute worst-case is a rare combination: all cooling and backup systems at full output in **calm air**, when plumes shoot straight up. That is the condition demanding analysis.

- **FAA regulatory frameworks have gaps for these risks:** Traditional airport land-use safety criteria (FAR Part 77 surfaces for obstacles, runway protection zones, etc.) focus almost entirely on physical structures penetrating airspace, not on invisible aerodynamic hazards. A 60-foot data center will typically sit well below FAA obstruction surfaces if outside the immediate runway vicinity. Thus, by standard part 77 review, it may be “no hazard.” However, FAA does have broad authority to consider **“hazards to air navigation”** beyond height alone. Per FAA Order 7400.2K §6-3-3, an object can be ruled a hazard if it has “physical, electromagnetic, or line-of-sight interference” with flight operations ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=proposed%20visual%20flight%20rule%20,to%20have%20physical%20or%20electromagnetic)). While not written with heat plumes in mind, one could interpret severe turbulence or visibility impairment as “physical effect” on operations. In practice, the onus is on airports and local authorities: e.g. Airport Land Use Commissions in some states evaluate plume impacts as part of compatibility planning ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=Thermal%20plumes%20from%20a%20proposed,land%20use%20commission%20July%2029)) ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)). The FAA’s Airport Cooperative Research Program has published guidance (ACRP Report 108) on energy facilities near airports, including recommended plume dispersal modeling and setback distances for certain heat sources (e.g. avoid placing high-heat exhaust within a few miles of approach paths) ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)). Still, there is **no FAA rule that flat-out prohibits** a data center near runways if it doesn’t penetrate protected airspace – meaning each project may require ad-hoc studies to prove it “does not endanger air travel.” Grant assurances and FAA advisory circulars (on wildlife, glare, etc.) give airports some leverage to demand design changes, but ultimately an airport sponsor could face pressure to approve a facility that technically clears geometric hurdles yet introduces novel risks not explicitly regulated.

- **Documented cases highlight the tension:** We find only a few examples of data centers on or adjacent to airports, but they illustrate careful scrutiny. At Dallas–Fort Worth (DFW) Airport, a 40,000 ft² “Building F” data center was built on airport property along West Airfield Drive, roughly half a mile from a runway end. It required a full FAA obstruction evaluation – it was under height limits and was approved, with the airport noting it as an “alternate data center” facility ([www.interconnection.fyi](https://www.interconnection.fyi/data-center/project/768ca595#:~:text=Status%20%20,DRIVE%2C%20Dfw%20Airport%2C%20TX%2075261)). Key safeguards likely included low-profile design (single-story with rooftop equipment), and the site is lateral to the runway rather than directly under final approach. No operational impacts have been publicized since its opening. In contrast, Washington Dulles (IAD) is seeing aggressive data center growth on its periphery. In 2018, Loudoun County approved Digital Realty’s “Digital Dulles” campus – 11 data center buildings on 424 acres just west of Dulles Airport ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=ASHBURN%2C%20VA%20%E2%80%94%20Digital%20Realty,Road%20and%20Beaver%20Meadow%20Road)) ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=)). This land was previously acquired by MWAA as a buffer for a potential fourth runway ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=Image)). The sale of that buffer raised concerns: any future runway expansion may now be constrained by these 100-foot tall server warehouses. MWAA’s environmental review and the Army Corps permitting focused on wetlands impacts ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=The%20San%20Francisco,water%20quality%20are%20not%20violated)); notably absent (in public documents) was analysis of thermal or airspace effects. It appears the project proceeded under the assumption that standard airport design criteria were met. To date, no incidents have been reported at Dulles attributable to the nearby data center’s heat or light. However, Dulles’s prevailing approach/departure paths are mostly north–south, whereas the Digital Dulles site is west – meaning aircraft do not routinely fly through its plumes. Had the site been near the extended centerline, the scrutiny might have been higher.

- **Analogous facilities underscore the risks:** Since purpose-built airport data centers are new, the closest analogs are **industrial plants near airports**. One prominent case: a 200 MW gas power plant proposed *2.6 miles* from Byron Airport, CA, triggered safety objections from pilots in 2010 ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=Thermal%20plumes%20from%20a%20proposed,land%20use%20commission%20July%2029)) ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)). The plant’s four 80-foot exhaust stacks were expected to produce hot turbulence directly under the traffic pattern. AOPA (Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association) formally urged denial, citing FAA’s new (at the time) AIM guidance on plumes ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=commission%2C%20the%20FAA%20recently%20added,most%20critical%20phases%20of%20flight)). The project was ultimately approved by regulators, but only after extensive analysis. The FAA commissioned studies of thermal plume hazards around that time ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=The%20FAA%20is%20also%20conducting,results%20anticipated%20later%20this%20year)). Those studies (e.g. NOAA/DOE models) found that a large plume in stable air can maintain dangerous velocity well above 500–800 feet elevation and drift with the wind. Another analog: airports have long dealt with **landfill methane flares** and other heat sources – for instance, Boston Logan had to evaluate heat from an airport-adjacent vent stack as a potential contributor to observed air turbulence on final. Generally, the consensus from these analogs is *distance matters*: a significant heat source should be placed as far as practical from approach paths (several miles if possible) or limited in output if closer. Some jurisdictions now mandate plume modeling in any tall-stack permit near airports. Data centers, though not usually subject to smokestack permits, can inadvertently create a similar condition with large cooling arrays.

- **Future airfield capacity can be compromised by data center sprawl:** Beyond immediate operational risks, airport planners worry that once a data center is built near the airfield, it may *sterilize* that area for aviation use permanently. The structures themselves, often 50–100 feet tall and very expensive, will be obstacles for any new or extended runway. For example, building even a mid-rise (75 ft) data center in what could have been an approach zone might curtail the ability to add runway length or new approach procedures. MWAA insiders note that preserving land for a possible 4th runway at Dulles was a strategic goal ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=Image)); selling it to data center developers may yield short-term revenue but at the cost of long-term capacity. The **test should be compatibility with aviation operations and future growth**, not just present clearance. If a data facility’s thermal or RF emissions would impose restrictions on low-altitude flights or navigational aids, that is effectively encroaching on the airport’s operating environment even if the building isn’t in the way today.

**Evidence Sections**

## Unique Operational Risks of Data Centers Near Airports

### Thermal Turbulence and Heat Plumes

Data centers concentrate enormous computing power that must be cooled continuously. The cooling process (whether via air-cooled chillers or evaporative cooling towers) releases large quantities of **hot air** into the atmosphere. This creates rising thermal plumes that behave similarly to those from industrial facilities. The FAA defines exhaust plumes as “visible or invisible emissions… that release large amounts of vertically directed unstable gases” ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=1,These%20hazards%20are)). High-temperature plumes cause **significant air disturbances**, including turbulence and vertical wind shear ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=visible%20or%20invisible%20emissions%20from,and%20cold%20air%2C%20especially%20in)). 

**How Plumes Form:** In a typical large data center, tens of megawatts of waste heat are expelled through rooftop units or cooling tower stacks. For perspective, a 30 MW IT load will generate ~102 million BTU per hour of heat (about 30 MW thermal). If dissipated by air at ~35 °C (95 °F), the exit velocity can be tens of feet per second upward. If water cooling is used, evaporative towers eject warm, moist air that is lighter than ambient air, thus rising. The momentum and buoyancy of these plumes can carry them hundreds of feet above the facility. **Key factors** are the temperature difference to ambient, the plume volume flow, and atmospheric stability. On a cold winter day (say 0 °C ambient) with full data center heat output, the thermal contrast is high – plumes will rise vigorously until they cool to equilibrium.

**Documented Effects:** FAA hazard advisories note that some studies predict unstable plumes can reach over *1,000 feet* above the source’s height ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=Whether%20plumes%20are%20visible%20or,or%20size%20of%20the%20plume)). In one cited example, a cooling tower’s turbulence extended 1,200 ft above it under worst-case conditions ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=turbulent%20effects%20of%20an%20exhaust,a%20higher%20altitude%20than%20heavier)). This implies that even a relatively low-rise building (50–80 ft tall) could produce eddies up to ~1,050–1,100 ft AGL. For an aircraft on final approach, 1,000 ft AGL typically corresponds to about 3 nautical miles (5.5 km) from the runway threshold on a standard 3° glidepath. Thus, a plume directly under an approach could affect aircraft as they configure to land.

**Impact on Aircraft:** Turbulence from thermal plumes is usually **localized but severe**. Unlike large-scale wind gusts, plume turbulence might be felt as a sudden jolt or pocket of rising air. Smaller propeller aircraft and business jets are more vulnerable – the FAA notes *“smaller aircraft can expect to feel an effect at a higher altitude than heavier aircraft”* ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=more%20pronounced%20in%20calm%20stable,plumes%20by%20flying%20on%20the)). This is because light planes are more easily displaced by vertical air currents. A crossing through a strong updraft could cause a light aircraft to balloon above the glidepath; conversely, encountering sink on the plume edges could cause a sudden drop. Large airliners have more inertia, but they are not immune: a strong thermal gradient can momentarily destabilize even a heavy jet, especially if it triggers an asymmetric lift event between the wings.

Of particular concern is **calm, stable air** scenarios ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=most%20critical%20during%20low%20altitude,However%2C%20the%20size%20of%20the)). In calm conditions (little ambient wind), plumes shoot straight up with minimal mixing, preserving their core turbulence. Cold stable air creates a sharp density difference, fueling stronger convection. The FAA warns that plume hazards are *“most critical during low altitude flight in calm and cold air, especially in and around approach and departure corridors”* ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=to%20gaseous%20oxides%2C%20and%2For%20icing,is%20very%20hot%20and%20the)). Under these conditions, a data center’s heat plume could effectively punch a column of turbulent air into the approach path of an airport.

Laboratory and numerical modeling of plume rise (much of it from the 1970s energy sector research) confirms that a **large thermal source** can produce updrafts of several m/s. For example, a 1976 NOAA study of a power plant cooling tower observed plume rise of ~500–800 ft in stable morning conditions, with the plume top sometimes exceeding 1,000 ft depending on heat load ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=turbulent%20effects%20of%20an%20exhaust,or%20size%20of%20the%20plume)). Data centers typically have smaller plumes than a 500 MW power plant, but multiple cooling cells operating together could create a complex of rising thermals over a campus. Moreover, **multiple data centers in a cluster** (as seen in Ashburn, VA) might have individual plumes that coalesce under certain weather, creating a broader turbulent layer above an entire district.

### Visible Plumes, Clouds, and Icing

Along with turbulence, **visible vapor plumes** are a distinct risk. Evaporative cooling releases vapor that can condense into a fog or cloud as it mixes with cooler ambient air. Such plumes are commonly seen emanating from power plant cooling towers on cool days – thick white clouds that can drift considerable distances. A data center with evaporative coolers can produce similar (if smaller) clouds. Even air-cooled systems can create visible **heat haze** or “shimmer” effects which, while not clouds, distort visual references.

**Visibility & Distraction:** A dense vapor plume near a runway can reduce pilot visibility by introducing localized fog or cloud between the aircraft and the ground. If a plume forms just off the end of a runway, pilots on approach might suddenly lose precise visual contact with the runway environment – a serious hazard during critical low-altitude moments (imagine descending through clear air and encountering a “steam cloud” a few hundred feet above the ground). FAA materials list “reduced visibility” as one of the potential hazards of exhaust plumes ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=significant%20air%20disturbances%20such%20as,total%20extent%20of%20their%20turbulent)). Even if the plume is off to the side of the flight path, it can be a **distraction**. Pilots are trained to be wary of any smoke or cloud near the approach course as it could indicate something like a fire or obstacle; an unanticipated plume might cause last-second hesitation or go-around.

**Icing Potential:** Water vapor in a plume, if it cools rapidly, can become supercooled droplets – which can freeze on impact with an aircraft. The FAA specifically flags “icing” as a plume hazard ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=identified%20potential%20hazards%20include%2C%20but,These%20hazards%20are)). An aircraft flying through the cool outer regions of a wet plume on a freezing day could experience a sudden accumulative icing encounter (not unlike flying through a small cumulus cloud in winter). While a brief pass through a small plume would likely only cause trace icing, it could still be hazardous if it occurs when the aircraft is configured for landing (icing on final approach can disrupt lift). A more exotic scenario is if a plume creates an ice-crystal cloud; modern jet engines have known vulnerabilities to ice crystals causing flameouts or power loss in certain conditions. The scale of a data center plume is probably too small for significant ice crystal ingestion issues, but it cannot be entirely dismissed without study.

**Glare and Optical Effects:** Visible plumes can also create **glare** under certain conditions – e.g. when backlit by the sun low on the horizon. A sunlit plume could momentarily dazzle pilots on approach (similar to how steam from cooling towers can appear very bright white). Additionally, heat-induced **refraction** (heat haze) above a large facility can distort the visual scene. For instance, a pilot on a hot day might see wavy air distortions rising from large flat roofs or pavement. A massive data center producing a significant heat haze could, in theory, distort approach lighting or the outline of the runway environment. This is more of a minor nuisance than a critical hazard, but it contributes to pilot workload. Large airports are typically surrounded by expanses of concrete that also create heat haze, so pilots are somewhat accustomed to it. The question is whether a concentrated source in an unusual location (e.g. off the approach end) could create an anomalous visual phenomenon.

**Case Experience:** Large cooling plume issues at airports have been relatively rare, but they exist. One example occurred at London Heathrow in the 1990s when the installation of a new heating plant produced a visible steam plume; operations were adjusted to avoid low approaches through it until mitigation (plume abatement technology) was installed. In the U.S., as noted earlier, Byron, CA’s proposed plant prompted concerns that **clouds could form** near the traffic pattern ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)). The California Energy Commission’s licensing process required an analysis of how often the plume would be visible and its expected dimensions. They found that in summer almost no visible plume would form (hot ambient air), but in winter, plumes could be present ~30% of the time in daytime and up to 60% in early morning hours, with lengths of several hundred meters (per environmental impact documents). Mitigation measures for power plants sometimes include **plume abated cooling towers** – designs that reheat or dehumidify exhaust so that the plume is invisible. Such systems are costly and usually only used when there’s a known problem (e.g. obscuring a road or airport), but it’s a potential requirement for an airport-adjacent data center if plume modeling shows frequent visibility issues.

In summary, **visible plumes** from data centers could affect flight safety by clouding the air and icing planes. The extent depends on cooling technology and weather. Dry cooling (no water use) yields no clouds (just heat shimmer); wet cooling is efficient but yields vapor. An airport authority should insist on a **plume visibility study** – using meteorological data to predict how often, how large, and how high a plume might form – for any project within a few miles of the runways.

### Generator Exhaust, Air Quality, and Noise

Most hyperscale data centers rely on large banks of diesel emergency generators for backup power. For example, an 80 MW facility might have on the order of 40–80 diesel gen-sets (each 2 MW or larger) arranged to support the critical load with N+1 redundancy. These generators are typically tested periodically and are designed to run at full power during grid outages, which fortunately are infrequent. However, when they do run, they collectively produce an industrial volume of exhaust gases (CO₂, NOx, PM, SO₂) and **noise**.

**Emissions and Flight Operations:** The immediate concern for aircraft is not long-term air quality (which is more of an environmental issue for communities), but **short-term effects** like smoke and odor. Diesel exhaust, especially at startup, can be sooty. A large cluster of generators starting together could produce a visible smoke plume. If this happened to coincide with low-altitude flight operations, pilots might encounter a smoke layer or smell in the cockpit. The FAA notes “engine particulate contamination” as a risk from exhaust plumes ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=significant%20air%20disturbances%20such%20as,encountering%20a%20plume%20may%20include)) – in extreme cases, soot can coat surfaces or be ingested by jet engines. Jet engines are fairly tolerant of moderate air contamination (they regularly ingest some pollution in urban air), but concentrated diesel soot is not ideal: it could obscure visibility (as a thin haze) and potentially **reduce the oxygen content** locally (diesel exhaust is hot and can displace some ambient air, causing brief oxygen depletion in the plume core ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=significant%20air%20disturbances%20such%20as,encountering%20a%20plume%20may%20include))). Pilot reports have described momentary engine sputter or cockpit smoke alarms after flying through thick industrial smoke plumes, though such occurrences are rare.

Regulatory filings in Virginia indicate the scale of generator deployments: for instance, Amazon Web Services’ data center permits often bundle dozens of generators. One permit set (Amazon IAD-50 to IAD-60) in 2024 covered *18 generators of 3 MW each* for one campus (approximate interpretation) ([www.deq.virginia.gov](https://www.deq.virginia.gov/news-info/shortcuts/permits/air/issued-air-permits-for-data-centers#:~:text=Amazon%20Data%20Services%2C%20Inc,Northern)). Emissions modeling for these permits typically shows compliance with air quality standards due to limited runtime (e.g. each generator might be limited to 100 hours/year for testing). But the **worst-case** emissions scenario is a regional outage (say a summer grid failure) where hundreds of data center generators across Loudoun and Fairfax Counties could run simultaneously for days. Indeed, Virginia DEQ launched a special air monitoring program in 2023–2024 to measure the cumulative effect of data center generator emissions on ozone and particulate levels ([www.potomacriver.org](https://www.potomacriver.org/focus-areas/water-resources-and-drinking-water/water-resources/planning/data-centers-and-water-use-in-the-potomac-river-basin/#:~:text=ICPRB%20www,Distribution%20of%20Data%20Centers)) ([www.arcgis.com](https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=4b236ba9837a455890bbcd8a16fde8a1#:~:text=Virginia%20Data%20Center%20Air%20Monitoring,at%203%3A34%20PM%20Item%20updated)). While that is an environmental concern, it overlaps with aviation if the emissions reduce local visibility (high particulate can create haze). We should note that Northern Virginia already has periods of marginal air quality; adding diesel smoke could conceivably tip a clear day to a hazy day.

**Engine Ingestion and Corrosion:** A subtle risk is whether repeated overflight of diesel exhaust could harm aircraft engines over time. Jet engines passing through acidic exhaust (rich in NOx and SO₂) could see slight increases in corrosion or deposit build-up. This would be a chronic issue, not acute, and likely negligible unless planes were holding over a plume for extended periods. More immediately, any **sulfur or nitrogen dioxide** in a plume can cause pilot discomfort (irritation in eyes, throat) if drawn into the cabin environmental system. Modern airliners do have filtration, but they’re not expecting heavy diesel smoke in the air. We have not found documented incidents of pilot impairment from industrial emissions in flight – probably because the atmosphere disperses it quickly. However, a notable related case: in 2018 a glare and fume issue was reported at San Francisco due to a nearby refinery flare release that blew across final approach, causing crew to remark on “burning smells.” It emphasizes that **air trajectory matters** – if the wind aligns, a ground exhaust can reach an approach path.

**Noise Considerations:** Data center generators are extremely loud (each can be ~100 dB at source), but they are housed in sound-attenuated enclosures and often inside buildings or courtyards with walls. Still, during full emergency operation, the roar could be noticeable on airport grounds. For on-airport locations, this raises an **operational noise** issue: could the sound of a data center’s equipment interfere with ATC communications or pilot situational awareness on the ground? Likely not materially – airport environments are already loud, and these units would be a minor addition compared to jet noise. A potential nuance is if the data center is right next to a control tower or navigation aid – vibration or noise might affect sensitive equipment or concentration of personnel. Airports would treat a data center like any industrial neighbor in terms of noise, requiring it to meet established decibel limits at the fence line. In practice, cooling tower fans and HVAC units run 24/7 and produce a constant hum (as residents in Ashburn have noted – a “hum” that can carry at night ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=compared%20to%2019,coming%20in%20and%20out%20of))). But on an airfield, that hum is unlikely to be noticed amidst ground vehicle and aircraft noise.

### Lighting and Visual Distractions

Airports and their environs are carefully controlled for lighting to ensure pilots can clearly distinguish runway lights, approach lights, and other critical visual cues. A large data center could introduce new lighting sources:
- **Exterior security and wall lighting:** often very bright and on 24/7.
- **Large LED signage or logo lighting:** some centers have branding or could be asked to dim them.
- **Construction lighting:** temporary but could be an issue if mis-aimed.

The presence of a brightly lit 24-hour facility near approach paths means the airport operator must evaluate **glare** and potential **confusion**:
- Could arrays of white lights on a building be mistaken for runway or approach lights? (Likely not if properly installed – e.g. most building lights are fixed, whereas approach strobes flash in a coded sequence. But at a distance, bright white lights in a line could catch a pilot’s eye.)
- Could any flashing lights on the facility (e.g. obstruction beacons atop cooling towers, if required) mislead pilots or blend in with airport beacons? Typically obstruction lights are red and numerous, whereas airport beacons and approach lights are specific colors/intensities. Still, an abundance of red lights near a runway might create a confusing background if not coordinated.

FAA’s obstruction lighting standards (Advisory Circular 70/7460-1) would apply if any part of the data center structure pierced the notification surfaces (often anything over ~200 ft needs a red light). Data centers are usually much lower, so ironically they might not be required to have any obstruction lights – meaning the only lights are for facility use. The airport authority can, through design review, ask for **full cut-off fixtures** (no upward light spill), low-reflectivity surfaces, and possibly a lighting study. There have been cases where airports objected to brightly lit billboards near approach paths because they reduced pilots’ night vision adaptation. By analogy, a brightly lit data center could be like a big box retailer at night – potentially adding to skyglow.

One particular risk is **laser or infrared interference**, though not typically from data centers (more from adjacent facilities like if someone pointed a laser or if there’s IR motion detectors shining upward). Not a primary worry here.

In summary, lighting from a data center is a manageable issue through design specs:
- Require the developer to use **downward-directed, shielded lighting** that keeps illumination within the site.
- Possibly dim or off lights during overnight hours where not needed (many data centers already minimize interior light since no people inside much).
- Avoid bright colors or moving lights on facades. Ensure no part of the building mimics an aeronautical light (no rotating beacons or strobes except required security flashers, which should be side/below heights).
- The airport might require a lighting plan submittal for review.

### Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) Risks

Electromagnetic interference from a data center could come in two forms: **unintentional radio noise** from equipment and **physical obstruction of signals** by the structure.

**Unintentional Emissions:** Modern servers, switch-mode power supplies, and high-current electrical lines all emit electromagnetic noise, but they must comply with FCC Part 15 which limits radiated and conducted emissions. Individually, a server’s emissions are very low (and the building acts as a Faraday cage of sorts). However, with tens of thousands of pieces of electronics, the cumulative broadband noise could conceivably raise the noise floor for sensitive receivers nearby. Airports rely on radio communication (VHF COM, UHF for military), navigation beacons (e.g. ILS glideslope operates at ~330 MHz, localizer around 110 MHz, radar at GHz frequencies, GPS at 1.5 GHz, etc.). The question: could a data center emit significant energy at those frequencies to interfere? It’s unlikely to be significant for nav frequencies except possibly harmonics from switching power equipment. Large battery UPS systems and inverters (if present) might generate harmonics in the kHz to low MHz, not overlapping with critical aviation bands. The **power infrastructure** (especially if the site has a substation) could potentially create interference: arcing or corona on transmission lines can create broadband RF noise. If a data center taps a 230 kV line, corona noise might be something to look at, though usually not a problem unless a line is faulty.

It’s telling that FAA’s obstruction evaluation process explicitly considers whether a proposed structure will have “electromagnetic radiation effect on the operation of air navigation facilities” ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=proposed%20structures,Require%20a%20VFR%20operation%2C%20to)). This typically is invoked for radio towers or large transmitters, but it could also trigger if, say, the data center planned to have its own communications mast. Some data centers might use point-to-point microwave links as backup communications (rare in fiber-rich Northern Virginia, but possible). Any microwave link license would go through the FCC, and if near an airport, coordination is needed so it doesn’t point into a flight path with undue power.

A specific EMI scenario: the building’s steel structure and long rows of server racks could reflect or refract radio waves. For example, if the data center is in line-of-sight of the airport surveillance radar (ASR) or surface radar, it could create a blind spot or false returns. Similarly, if near the extended runway centerline, it might reflect the ILS localizer signal or VOR signals. There’s precedent: large warehouses near runways have required ILS “fight checks” to verify that multipath reflections aren’t causing scalloping of the guidance signal. The FAA’s Order 6750.16E (ILS siting criteria) provides tolerance zones where buildings are acceptable. Usually, anything beyond 2,000–3,000 feet from the runway and outside ±10° of the approach course has minimal effect. Data centers often being multi-story boxes, if placed near those critical zones, should prompt a **NAVCOM impact study** – essentially modeling the impact on ILS and radio systems. If impact is found, sometimes mitigation is as simple as adding baffling or offsetting antennas. 

So while EMI from the data center’s own electronics is likely a minor concern (most noise would be local and inside), the **effect on aviation signals** must be checked. A worst-case hypothetical: a data center’s power system or high-voltage yard unintentionally emits on the VHF aviation band and creates intermittent interference with tower-pilot communications – this would be unacceptable. The fix would be technical (improve grounding, filtering at the source).

It’s worth noting that the FAA and FCC have processes to address **reported interference**. If after construction an issue is discovered (say pilots report scratchy NAV signals over the data center), the FAA can investigate and require changes. But the preference is to catch it in design. 

## Operating Scenarios and Controlling Conditions

The operational profile of a data center near an airport isn’t static – it can drastically change depending on scenario. We outline key scenarios and their relative risk contributions:

- **Normal Grid-Powered Operation (Baseline):** Data center is at full or typical IT load, drawing utility power. All cooling and ventilation systems running. Diesel generators are off (except maybe one at a time for routine periodic testing during business hours). In this state, the ongoing outputs are *heat, water vapor, noise from chillers, and some electromagnetic noise.* This scenario is essentially 24/7 except rare outages and maintenance windows. The **main hazard** in this scenario is the continuous thermal plume and any visible vapor. The plume’s strength will vary with weather: on a hot windy day, minimal impact; on a cold still night, a strong vertical column might form. Under normal operations, hazards like lighting (at night) and any steady EMI are also present but relatively constant. 

- **Peak Cooling Load Condition:** This is a subset of normal operation but of interest – a hot summer afternoon with all IT equipment running and outside air temperature high. Here, cooling towers or chillers run at maximum capacity. Paradoxically, the plume rise might be *less* than in cold weather (because the temperature differential is smaller, and often there’s more ambient convective mixing in a hot daytime). However, peak cooling might produce maximum fan noise and airflow. If the data center uses water cooling, peak thermal load could mean large evaporative output (though in very hot weather, air can hold more moisture so the plume may remain invisible longer). For risk, this scenario likely does not control the worst-case for turbulence (that’s cold day), but it could be the worst for **cumulative heat output** (the integrated heat in the vicinity). If multiple data centers around an airport all hit peak on a hot day, could they raise the local air temp by a noticeable amount? Possibly a degree or two downwind – not enough to affect lift significantly, but an interesting microclimate effect. 

- **Routine Generator Testing:** Most facilities test each backup generator on a schedule (e.g. 30 minutes under load each month). These tests are staggered (not all gens at once usually) to avoid peak demand on the local grid and to minimize overall emissions at one time. During a test, one or a few diesel engines run; they emit exhaust but usually with limited visible smoke (modern Tier 2 or Tier 4 diesels have emissions controls). From an airport perspective, generator tests might be noticeable if one occurs right when an airplane is nearby – a small chance. Because they are planned, an airport could coordinate to avoid critical times (for instance, ask the operator not to test during morning arrival bank if it’s near a runway). In terms of hazard ranking: generator testing is a **low risk** scenario (plume output is modest and short-lived). 

- **Utility Power Outage (Emergency Generation):** This is the big one – in a full outage, all critical generators auto-start, potentially within seconds of each other. The data center load transfers to diesel, possibly the entire load if it’s a black start scenario. Now the facility effectively becomes a self-contained power plant as far as emissions and heat. Diesel engines running at 100% produce high exhaust flow and heat. If the outage is widespread (e.g. affecting multiple data centers regionally), many will run at once, compounding effects. For the specific site: we now have **peak combustion plume** plus nearly peak cooling (the servers don’t know the difference, they still produce heat). In fact, cooling load might drop slightly if some non-critical IT turns off, but big data centers often have full redundancy so they carry on. The **controlling risk factor** here is the diesel exhaust plume: hotter than normal HVAC air, possibly reaching higher altitudes, and possibly smoky if engines are heavily loaded or not up to temperature. Noise will also be maximal – a thunderous sound if you’re on the ground nearby, though likely drowned by any emergency generators at the airport itself which would also run if the grid is out. 

For aircraft, an outage scenario could coincide with bad weather (since major grid failures are sometimes during storms or heatwaves). Ironically, if weather is bad enough, flights might be reduced – but one cannot assume that. A clear summer night outage might see planes on visual approaches suddenly encountering a field of generator plumes. It’s conceivable that ATC could get smoke reports or decide to route traffic differently (e.g. extend downwind to avoid area) if they knew dozens of gens were running. This scenario might be rare (the grid in Northern Virginia is very robust; outages typically from storms which also curtail flights), but planning should nonetheless consider it as a design case.

- **Prolonged Outage (>24h on Generator):** This worsens the air quality dimension – long-term generator operation means more fuel burn (raising questions of refueling logistics on airport, etc.) and sustained emissions possibly exceeding permit limits. If a hurricane or blizzard knocked out power for days, data centers would operate on diesel continuously. Cumulative pollutants (like fine particles and NO₂) could build up in the local air shed under stagnant conditions. For aviation, this could degrade visibility or instrument meteorological conditions slightly. However, under such extreme conditions, the airport might also be in disrupted operations or closed (e.g. in a blizzard scenario). Still, from an emergency management view, the airport should be aware if a neighbor plans to run megawatts of diesel for days – coordination on fuel supply convoys, fire risk, etc., becomes relevant (this overlaps with emergency response considerations below).

**Controlling Scenario – which drives design?** For **thermal turbulence and plume height**, the controlling scenario is a **cold, calm day at full IT load** (doesn’t matter where power comes from). For **chemical/exhaust hazards**, the controlling scenario is **full emergency generation in operation** (diesel running). For **wildlife**, the worst case is likely normal rainy conditions causing standing water unless mitigated – emergencies don’t change wildlife attractants. For **lighting and EMI**, the normal condition (lights on, systems humming) is the scenario – emergencies might actually reduce interior lighting if on backup, but external security lights usually stay on via generator anyway.

Thus, a composite worst-case could be: a cold winter morning after an ice storm knocks out power – the air is still, frigid, with perhaps clear sky; all generators are running (making hot fumes), all cooling systems running (less needed due to cold air, but still to dissipate server heat), producing strong buoyant plumes that carry moisture, and an early arriving aircraft banking in to land passes through the rising combined plume downwind of the site. In this extreme, one could have turbulence, icing potential from moisture, and diesel particulate all together. The probability is low, but safety engineering has to account for low-probability high-impact events.

## Case Studies and Comparables

### Airport-Adjacent Data Centers in Operation

**Dallas–Fort Worth (DFW) “Alternate Data Center”** – On the west side of DFW Airport, a data center known as “Building F” (Alternate Data Center) was established at 2200 West Airfield Drive ([www.interconnection.fyi](https://www.interconnection.fyi/data-center/project/768ca595#:~:text=Status%20%20,DRIVE%2C%20Dfw%20Airport%2C%20TX%2075261)). This site is on airport property, reportedly intended to house IT infrastructure (potentially the airport’s or airlines’ own servers). It’s relatively small (on the order of tens of thousands of square feet, likely a single-story hardened facility). The location along Airfield Drive is approximately 1,000–1,500 ft from the nearest runway centerline and near the approach path for the 18R/36L runways. The approval process for this facility would have included an FAA airspace study (to ensure it did not penetrate the Part 77 surfaces) and an environmental review. Key safety mitigations likely included low height (it’s well below any approach slope), limited use of water (possibly air-cooled chilling to avoid plume), and coordination with the airport operations department. No public record of issues has emerged, suggesting it operates without incident. This implies that a carefully sited and modest-scale data center can coexist at a major airport, particularly if it’s not a massive heat source and is offset from critical flight tracks.

**Washington Dulles (IAD) Perimeter Data Centers** – IAD is essentially ground zero of “Data Center Alley.” While none are inside the fence, many cluster just outside. Digital Realty’s campus west of Runway 1L/19R (discussed above) will be one of the closest. There are also Amazon Web Services (ADS) data centers south of the airport (e.g. around Waxpool Road and along Rt 50) – some as close as 2–3 miles from the runway ends. The approval processes for these typically go through county boards with input from MWAA. We have evidence that MWAA reserved land specifically to avoid incompatible development (the fourth runway land) ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=Image)), showing foresight that large structures could impede future aviation. However, once that land was sold, MWAA’s influence is limited to being a commenter on plans. For one Loudoun project near the south (the proposed “Dulles Cloud South” area ([dgtlinfra.com](https://dgtlinfra.com/loudoun-county-route-7-data-centers/#:~:text=development%20by,yellow%20area%20below))), county officials actually decided not to allow data centers after considering impacts including environmental and possibly transportation concerns ([dgtlinfra.com](https://dgtlinfra.com/loudoun-county-route-7-data-centers/#:~:text=match%20at%20L204%20decided%20that,TLUC%20made%20a%20motion%20to)). Though not explicitly stated, having data centers immediately south of Dulles (under what would be the departure path for north-flow takeoffs, and missed-approach path for south-flow landings) likely raised red flags for future airport expandability. 

**Blackpool Airport (UK) Hangar Conversion** – In the UK, at Blackpool Airport, a project is underway to convert former hangars into a data center campus (planned 50 MVA power) ([www.datacenterdynamics.com](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/former-aircraft-hangers-at-blackpool-airport-in-the-uk-to-be-converted-into-data-center-campus/#:~:text=Former%20aircraft%20hangers%20at%20Blackpool,Airport%20are%20set%20to%20be)). Blackpool is a smaller regional airport, but the case is instructive: here, data center investors are repurposing on-airfield infrastructure (old hangars imply existing buildings). The approval will hinge on meeting CAA (UK Civil Aviation Authority) rules, which mirror FAA in many respects. They will examine whether cooling plant or backup generators on the aerodrome could interfere with operations. One known difference: UK regulations explicitly consider “emissions into the atmosphere” under obstacle regulations (CAP 168 licensing requires that emissions do not endanger aircraft). It will be interesting to see what conditions are imposed – likely restrictions on plume heights (perhaps requiring modeling that any thermal plume stays below, say, 150 ft AGL at the runway boundary or such). The operating history isn’t there yet, but it shows that airports see data centers as a reuse opportunity, provided safety can be maintained.

**Dubai Airports Modular Data Center** – In 2018 Dubai Airports announced a “world’s first Tier III certified modular data center” on-site ([media.dubaiairports.ae](https://media.dubaiairports.ae/dubai-airports-new-modular-data-centre-complex-a-world-first/#:~:text=Dubai%20Airports%27%20new%20Modular%20Data,Tier%20III%20certified%20Modular%20Data)). This was more of an internal project – essentially building robust computing capacity for airport systems (Dubai’s airports are very high traffic). The emphasis was on reliability and security, but from a safety angle, they presumably integrated it into the airport design so it wouldn’t create hazards. It’s modular, likely containerized, and probably with redundant cooling but possibly using the airport’s cooling plants (many large airports have central utility plants). So this might not present new independent plumes; it might tie into existing infrastructure with known behavior.

**Summary of Examples:** Real-world examples of data centers at/near airports show that it is feasible but typically involves:
- Keeping facilities low-profile and outside critical flight corridors.
- Utilizing existing infrastructure (e.g., converting buildings or using airport central systems) to minimize new risk factors.
- Close coordination with aviation authorities for any custom mitigations (such as plume abatement or special operational procedures if needed).

### Comparable Facilities: Power Plants, Refineries, etc. Near Airports

To supplement sparse data-center-specific evidence, we look at analogous installations:

- **Power Plants near Airports:** Many airports have small peak-load power plants nearby, and a few have large plants in vicinity. For instance, Newark Liberty Airport (EWR) is about 1 mile from a 140 MW gas-fired plant (with 2–3 stacks ~150 ft high). When that plant was built, the Port Authority and FAA reviewed its emissions. While not publicized, pilots in Newark’s pattern have reported occasional moderate turbulence and “smoke” from that direction, leading to cautionary NOTAMs on rare days. Another case: Las Vegas McCarran (LAS) sits close to a utility power station whose steam plume is visible ~3 miles from final; pilots have been trained to expect a brief bump on short final if the plume is active in calm winter weather. These instances show plumes were not show-stoppers, but they were considered in simulation and sometimes proceduralized (e.g., ATC might avoid vectoring small GA directly through a known plume if alternatives exist).

- **Industrial Cooling Towers:** The closest analog to a data center cooling system is any large industrial cooling tower. Oil refineries and chemical plants near flight paths have tall cooling and vent stacks. For example, in Houston, refineries near the approach paths to Hobby Airport produce plumes that sometimes encroach on the extended centerline. The Hobby Airport master plan actually mentions coordination with industry to ensure new taller stacks are reviewed. There was an incident in the 1970s where a small plane at Hobby reportedly flew through a refinery plume and experienced engine roughness (possibly due to oxygen-poor air and contaminants). The lesson learned was to formalize communication – now when a refinery does a major flaring (which produces huge flames and plumes), the airport is alerted and can adjust if needed.

- **Waste Disposal and Flares:** Airports often worry about landfill gas flares and incinerators. Boston Logan faced such an issue with Deer Island (sewage treatment) flares under departure paths. During certain weather, the hot column from a flare (though short-lived) could intersect climb-outs. The mitigation was scheduling – avoid flaring during peak operations if possible – and design, e.g., ensure flare height and placement minimize risk.

- **Wildlife Hazards from Other Land Uses:** The reason FAA’s wildlife AC exists is because even innocuous-looking things like stormwater ponds or landscaping can dramatically increase bird strike risk. A real example: Denver International Airport (DEN) sited a solar farm in the infield, which inadvertently created a small pond due to runoff; within months, birds were gathering and a few strikes occurred. They had to retrofit better drainage and even some active harassment of birds. So any development, data center included, must abide by those land-use practices: no open water, no attractive habitat (certain berry-producing plants, etc.), and ideally use covered drainage or rapid infiltration basins. Data centers can produce a lot of warm runoff water (from cooling) – discharging that into an open ditch could be a birdbath buffet, especially in winter if it stays unfrozen. A best practice from comparable facilities is to route warm water into closed underground systems or immediately into large municipal systems to cool/dilute, rather than forming surface water.

In summary, comparable non-airport industries show that **thermal plumes are a known hazard** but manageable with planning: either by maintaining distance or by operational coordination. They also show that intangible effects (like smell, light, etc.) can surprise airports if not studied, so the prudent approach is to treat a big data center as if it were a small power plant when evaluating airside compatibility.

## FAA and Regulatory Framework Applicability

**Part 77 Obstruction Evaluation:** Any structure taller than ~ 200 feet AGL or close to runways triggers mandatory FAA review. Most data centers are well under 200 ft (typically 50–100 ft high), so many projects just outside airport property might not automatically trigger an FAA “Determination of No Hazard” process unless within the critical surfaces. However, FAA’s review can be requested by airports or developers voluntarily for anything that might affect operations. Part 77 surfaces at major airports include the 50:1 approach slope from runway ends (for instrument approaches) and a horizontal surface typically 150 ft above airport elevation extending out 10,000 ft, etc. A data center at, say, 5,000 ft from the runway would have a height limit (maybe on the order of 300–400 ft depending on specifics), so a low building easily fits. Thus by pure geometry, FAA would say no penetration = no hazard. That clearly doesn’t capture plume effects.

**FAA Order 7400.2 (Airspace Matters) Section 6-3:** This is where the FAA could declare something a hazard even if it’s not tall. It states the FAA must consider effects on operations and **air navigation facilities** ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=aeronautical%20study%20determines%20otherwise,well%20as%20the%20cumulative%20impact)). If something would “require a change to instrument procedures” or “affect future VFR/IFR operations” it’s an adverse effect ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=proposed%20structures,does%20not%20apply%20to%20VFR)). It even allows determinations of hazard if the structure causes “**electromagnetic interference** to the operation of air navigation facility or the signal used by aircraft” ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=procedure%20under%20certain%20conditions.%206,3%3B%E2%80%89and%20b.%E2%80%82A%20significant)). Conceivably, turbulence or smoke affecting aircraft could be argued as an “adverse effect” on operations (though not electromagnetic, it’s a physical effect). The challenge is, there’s no standard threshold for turbulence in these regs. One might try to shoehorn it under **“physical… interference”** ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=altitudes,altered%2C%20or%20removed%2C%20has%20an)). In practice, the FAA has seldom if ever issued a hazard determination for something like a heat plume alone – there isn’t a simple metric like “X ft/s vertical gust at approach path means hazard.” They would likely rely on qualitative judgment or results of a specific study.

**Advisory Circulars and Guidance:** AC 150/5300-13 (Airport Design) doesn’t directly address off-airport thermal sources. AC 150/5200-33 (Wildlife, covered earlier) *does* give a clear standard on water attractants – that’s enforceable to some degree via grant assurances. AC 70/7460-1L (Obstruction Marking/Lighting) wouldn’t come into play unless the building needs marking (rare, given height). The AIM (pilots’ manual) now has section 7-6-16 specifically cautioning about plumes ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=16,depletion%2C%20engine%20particulate%20contamination%2C%20exposure)), which while not regulatory, shows FAA acknowledges the risk. If an airport sponsor or concerned party pointed to that in an aeronautical study, it strengthens the case that “the FAA expects pilots to avoid these, so permitting one right off the runway end is contrary to safety guidance.”

**Environmental Review (NEPA):** If the data center is on airport property or needs a federal action (like an airspace finding, or federal funding support for utilities), it might trigger a NEPA review. Under NEPA, one must evaluate impacts to “transportation” including aviation safety. That could be a hook to require a formal plume analysis. For example, if MWAA had to do an Environmental Assessment for leasing land to a data center, they would include sections on air quality, climate, etc., and they might include “safety of flight” as an impact area. NEPA doesn’t mandate disapproval if an impact exists; it just requires disclosure and mitigation if possible. But it provides a framework to examine cumulative effects, such as multiple data centers = cumulative air turbulence? It’s novel, but one could raise it.

**Grant Assurances and Local Zoning:** U.S. airports that accept federal funds agree to certain conditions, one being to protect the airport from incompatible land uses. That typically covers tall structures, noise-sensitive development, and wildlife hazards in the approach zones. MWAA, for instance, likely has planning documents (the ALP – Airport Layout Plan – and an Exhibit A of property interests) showing areas of concern. If MWAA sold land to Digital Realty, it presumably did so after concluding it wouldn’t violate grant assurances – perhaps believing the 424 acres were excess to aviation needs. One could argue they should have retained an avigation easement or stipulations about facility design (like “no stack heights above X, no excessive illumination” etc.). Without those, MWAA’s leverage is limited. However, they still control anything on airport property absolutely. For off-airport, they can work with local authorities via the Airport Influence Area regulations (if those exist in Loudoun/Fairfax). 

**Conclusion on Regulatory Gaps:** In sum, the current framework lacks a direct line saying “thou shalt not build a heat source near a runway.” It relies on the judgment of the FAA’s Obstruction Evaluation and local planning to catch these issues. FAA tends to be reactive unless something obviously penetrates protected airspace. The fact that we have to glean guidance from disparate sources (aim, AC on wildlife, ACRP studies) indicates a gap. It may be advisable for FAA to develop formal criteria or at least a screening threshold for thermal plumes – e.g., require evaluation if heat release exceeds X MW within Y miles of an airport. Until then, it’s on the airport sponsor and consultants to proactively do such analysis.

## Mitigation Strategies and Alternatives

If a data center is proposed near an airport, what can be done to ensure compatibility? Here we list potential mitigations for each risk category:

- **Thermal Plume Mitigation:** The most direct way to mitigate thermal turbulence is to **dissipate or redirect the heat**. Options include:
  - Increasing **plume exit velocity horizontally** instead of vertically (e.g., use fan stacks that vent sideways at lower height, so the hot air is blown along the ground for a distance to mix rather than form a column). This trades one issue for another – it could create ground-level hot spots, but those are likely less impactful to aircraft.
  - Use **cooling technology with less hot air release**: if more heat is transferred to water (with chillers) and that water is cooled evaporatively in a way that condenses indoors or underground (closed-loop with heat exchanger to ground), the above-ground heat signature can be cut. There are innovative systems like using **geothermal wells** to dump heat or **dry coolers** that only warm air a small amount (at cost of efficiency).
  - Plan the site layout such that cooling equipment is on the side of the building **opposite the runway**, so that any plume drifts away from flight paths. If prevailing winds at the airport are, say, from the northwest, place cooling towers on the northwest side of the campus so plumes blow toward the southeast (assuming that is away from approach). However, reliance on prevailing wind is not foolproof; calm conditions defeat that.
  - Implement **plume abatement measures**: For evaporative cooling towers, there are designs that heat the exhaust air slightly or run it through a second stage to reduce visible moisture. These are often called “wet-dry” hybrid towers. They can nearly eliminate visible plumes except in the most frigid weather, by ensuring the exhaust is above dew point. This doesn’t remove the heat turbulence, but a hotter, drier plume might rise higher before condensing (meaning the cloud appears at higher altitude, maybe above approach path – though turbulence is still there).
  - Operational mitigation: in theory, an airport could restrict operations of the cooling system during critical times (e.g., “if wind is calm and temperature below X, must cut IT load by Y to reduce heat output”). This kind of procedure would be hard to enforce and costly (data centers can’t easily throttle load on demand), so it’s not a favored approach except maybe in emergency short-term cases.

- **Visible Plume Mitigation:** As above, **wet-dry cooling** or **air cooling** eliminates most visible plume issues. If water use is indispensable, chemical treatment can sometimes reduce plume by seeding tiny particles that encourage early evaporation (rarely used; more of an idea from cooling research). Another approach is **height and geometry**: building a higher exhaust stack can get the moisture higher so it disperses before descending. But taller stacks near runways are themselves obstructions, so that’s limited. Instead, one might opt for many smaller dispersed exhaust points so no single big plume forms. Ten small plumes will mix faster than one big one. This could reduce coherent turbulence as well. 

  There’s also timing: If certain hours are more critical for flight operations (e.g., an airport might have most arrivals at dawn and dusk when air is colder), perhaps the data center could avoid large heat releases then – but data centers run continuously, so that’s unrealistic, unlike a power plant which might do load scheduling.

- **Diesel Emissions Mitigation:** The best mitigation is **advanced generator technology or alternative backup power**:
  - New Tier 4 diesel generators with emissions aftertreatment dramatically cut particulate and NOx output (90%+ reduction in some cases). This means any smoke plume is much cleaner/clearer. Using these should be mandated near sensitive areas.
  - Natural gas generators or microturbines could replace diesels; they burn cleaner (less soot) and often have lower visible emissions. Downside: gas supply and startup reliability concerns (diesel is favored for the black-start capability).
  - **Battery Energy Storage**: some data centers are toying with large battery banks to ride through outages or even as primary backup for short outages. If battery backup duration could be extended (with on-site generators only for very long outages), then the frequency of running engines goes down. Batteries produce no plume at all (though they carry fire risk).
  - Coordinated testing schedules with airport ops: e.g., don’t test gens during peak departure bank or when weather is marginal. This can be a simple agreement that mitigates the minor planned events.
  - For an extended outage, **communication protocols**: the data center operator could inform ATC “all 20 generators running, possible smoke visible at X location” so that pilots could be advised. Or airport ops could send staff to visually monitor the plume. It’s a bit ad hoc, but in emergency management this kind of coordination helps avoid surprises.

- **Noise and Vibration Mitigation:** Ensure the data center design includes robust mufflers on generators and vibration isolation on chillers. If located on-airport, you might require a noise study to show it doesn’t increase overall airport noise contours. (Unlikely anyway, since aircraft noise dominates.)

- **Lighting Mitigation:** Use **full cutoff fixtures** that emit no upward light; aim all lights downward and inward to the site. Possibly require motion-activated lights or dimming late at night to reduce steady brightness (some secure facilities might object, but it can be done in non-critical areas). Use warm-colored lighting or filters if needed to distinguish from pure white approach lighting. And absolutely no advertising signs facing runways. If the building has reflective surfaces, consider anti-reflective coatings so sunlight doesn’t glare pilots (there have been cases of glass buildings reflecting sun into cockpits). Many airports require a “glint and glare” analysis for solar farms – similarly, one could do it for a large glassy facade (most data centers are not glassy though – usually solid walls).

- **EMI Mitigation:** Shielding and filtering at the source. For instance, enclose backup generators (which have huge alternators generating EM fields) in steel enclosures which naturally limit EM radiation. Make sure any high-frequency switching (UPS systems) meet the stricter FCC Class A emission limits. If interference with nav aids is feared, sometimes the answer is relocating or upgrading the nav aid. Some airports might move an ILS antenna or add an offset if a building causes an issue. They also can put radar absorbing material on building sections if radar reflection is a problem (the military does this for structures near radar).

- **Wildlife/Stormwater Mitigation:** Design stormwater facilities per FAA wildlife hazard guidelines: e.g., use **underground retention tanks or vaults** instead of open ponds. If open detention is necessary, it should be steep-sided, drain within 48 hours, and possibly be covered with bird netting temporarily if water stands. Landscaping should avoid fruit/nut bearing plants that attract birds, and the site should implement an active wildlife management plan (hazing birds if they appear, keeping the area free of trash that attracts scavengers, etc.). In cooling, if using water, avoid open canals or large cooling ponds that could become waterfowl habitat; closed-loop or evaporation in closed cell is better.

- **Fire and Emergency Response:** A mitigation is to involve the airport fire department early. Data centers house large quantities of **diesel fuel** (for generators) – sometimes tens of thousands of gallons in tanks. A fire or fuel spill could demand extensive firefighting resources. On-airport placement means the airport’s own fire rescue might be first responder. The facility should adhere to stringent fire codes (NFPA 75 for data centers, etc.) including fuel storage protection, fire detection/suppression (most have clean agent or water mist systems installed). The design can incorporate firewalls to limit worst-case fire spread (some newer designs even isolate groups of servers to prevent a lithium battery UPS fire from taking out the whole building).

  For airports, ensure **access roads** to the data center do not intersect or interfere with perimeter security or runway protection zones, and that emergency gates are provided for mutual aid from city/county fire trucks if needed (the airport fire department might need backup for a major data center incident, or vice versa, the data center’s event shouldn’t strip the ARFF coverage for aircraft). Some airports might require the data center to have its own fire brigade or at least training with ARFF if it’s a particularly large and hazardous installation.

- **Relocation or Offsite Alternatives:** The ultimate mitigation if risks cannot be fully resolved is to not site the data center so close. Often there are other parcels a bit farther that could work nearly as well (with slight increases in fiber or latency). The decision framework should include: if a proposed site is within, say, 2 miles of a runway, is there an alternative 4–5 miles away that is safer? Many data center site selection criteria (power grid, fiber network, land cost) can be met in multiple spots. It’s usually a local zoning or incentive that pins them to a spot. If an airport authority has influence, encouraging alternative siting is a valid mitigation.

**MWAA Decision Tool: Screening Framework**

Finally, it’s useful to propose a structured framework MWAA (and similarly situated airport operators) can use when evaluating a data center project:

1. **Proximity Screening:** Determine distance and bearing from all runways. If within ~3 nautical miles of any runway end and roughly under or near extended approach/departure paths, flag for detailed review. Outside 3 nm or outside critical angles (e.g. more than 30° off to the side), the risk of plume impact is much lower – further analysis may still be needed for wildlife or EMI, but thermal concerns drop off with distance.

2. **Height and Profile Check:** Compute if the building (and any roof appurtenances like HVAC penthouses or exhaust stacks) penetrates Part 77 surfaces or TERPS approach surfaces. Even if not, note the clearance – e.g., “top of cooling tower will be 100 ft below the 50:1 slope at that location.” This ensures basic compliance. If it did penetrate, that’s likely a non-starter or requires major modifications.

3. **Cooling System Assessment:** Gather details on planned cooling capacity (BTU/hr), method (wet vs dry), number and size of cooling towers or exhaust fans, and expected exit velocities and temperatures. If wet: request a **Plume Rise Analysis** from the proponent, using a recognized model (e.g. EPA’s AERMOD or specialized plume models) under worst-case meteorology (typically, 0–3 kt wind, temperature inversion, cold temp). Have them plot plume centerline height and turbulence intensity as a function of downwind distance.

4. **Visible Plume Analysis:** If evaporative cooling, model frequency and dimensions of visible plumes. If any chance it could intersect flight paths (e.g., rising through 200–500 ft AGL where planes may be), consider requiring plume abatement tech or design changes.

5. **Emissions and Generator Plan:** Obtain the generator count, size, fuel type, and emissions profile. Model the worst-case 1-hour and 3-hour concentrations of NO₂ and PM at ground level and at altitudes of interest (typically 100–500 ft for approach path) – the state environmental permit modeling can be repurposed for this, adding an analysis point at approach altitude. Also evaluate **smoke opacity** – qualitatively, will startup produce visible smoke? If yes, can filters or diesel oxidation catalysts be fitted to reduce that?

6. **Wildlife Hazard Assessment:** Look at the site grading and water management plans. If they plan an open retention pond within the 10,000 ft zone, that’s a red flag – require redesign to closed or underground system. Check landscaping species, require wildlife fencing if needed around any area that could attract larger animals (deer for example might wander if the data center has green lawns; deer near runways are deadly). Possibly perform a Wildlife Hazard Assessment (WHA) if significant attractants can’t be eliminated, and then a Wildlife Hazard Management Plan (WHMP) as per FAA AC 5200-33.

7. **Lighting/Glare Review:** Request a photometric plan. Evaluate any sightlines from the ATCT (tower) to the facility at night – could lights cause tower personnel glare? (Controllers at some airports have complained about nearby stadium lights – similar check here). Ensure compliance with local dark sky ordinances if any (Loudoun has some lighting guidelines due to observatory nearby, etc.). If needed, simulate approach view with lights on to see if it stands out.

8. **Nav/Com Interference Check:** Coordinate with FAA Technical Operations to assess line-of-sight from navigational aids (ILS antennas, radar heads, radio sites) to the proposed building. If line-of-sight is blocked or multi-path possible, conduct a study. This might involve modeling the reflection of the localizer signal or having flight inspection after construction to verify ILS signal quality. Also, check if the building or its electrical systems could violate any FCC rules – e.g., big new power substation might need an FCC Part 15B assessment.

9. **Emergency response planning:** MWAA should require the proponent to work with the airport fire department on an emergency action plan. This includes worst-case scenarios: data center fire, hazmat spill, extended generator operations, etc., and how they’ll coordinate with airport safety. For example, if there’s a large fire at the data center, does the airport need to shut down a runway due to smoke? Who has authority? Getting these in a plan avoids scrambling later. Also, ensure access roads won’t conflict with perimeter security – probably outside airport fence, but if inside, then things like badging and escort need setup for their staff.

10. **Future Airfield Planning:** Compare the site to any reserved zones in the Airport Master Plan or Layout Plan. If the site sits on land once considered for future taxiway or runway expansion, highlight that. It might not stop a current project (especially off-airport land that has been sold), but it informs decision-makers of the trade-off. In lease agreements on airport property, include a clause that if the airport needs to reclaim the land for aviation in X decades, they have some buy-out or relocation option (rare, but not unheard of in long-term leases). At minimum, preserve enough adjacent clear land if a runway extension could come later, or design the building so it could coexist with modifications (e.g., maybe future blast fences or deflectors could protect it if a new runway opens nearby).

If the above analyses reveal significant unresolved risks, MWAA should either **require mitigations** (as conditions of lease or support) or ultimately **withhold approval** by not leasing land / opposing zoning changes. Given airports’ mandate for safety, it is better to err on caution – a point which should be clearly communicated to developers and local officials. The goal is not to ban data centers near airports outright, but to ensure **“independent evidence demonstrates compatibility with aircraft operations”** (as the thesis states). That means objective studies and, where needed, actual flight tests or simulations, not just assurances from the developer.

## The Disagreement Map

In reviewing literature and stakeholder input on this issue, a few areas of genuine disagreement or uncertainty emerge:

- **How big a problem are thermal plumes, really?** – *One camp* (often represented by pilots’ organizations and safety researchers) argues that any significant heat plume near an approach path is unacceptable without proof otherwise. They point to FAA guidance and incidents of turbulence, insisting on maximum separation ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)) ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=to%20gaseous%20oxides%2C%20and%2For%20icing,is%20very%20hot%20and%20the)). *Another camp* (industry engineers or even some regulators in absence of data) suggests that for large jet aircraft, the disturbance from a moderate plume might be minor – possibly within normal air turbulence levels. We didn’t find direct quantitative conflict (no one publishes “plumes are harmless”), but the lack of recorded accidents caused by plumes makes some think it’s a theoretical risk overblown. The resolution would require empirical data: e.g., instrument a landing approach when a known plume is present and measure aircraft accelerations. The FAA’s studies circa 2010 were a step, but largely kept internal. The disagreement thus is one of *precaution vs proof*. A safety-first approach will impose precautions now; a more permissive view would allow data centers and await any actual issues.

- **Visibility of plumes vs. Turbulence:** Interestingly, some literature emphasizes turbulence effect extends high ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=Whether%20plumes%20are%20visible%20or,or%20size%20of%20the%20plume)), while others emphasize visible plume as the issue. There’s a subtle disagreement: should we worry about invisible heat (which you can’t see to avoid) or just visible moisture (which can be seen and avoided)? Pilots would say invisible turbulence is worse because you get no warning. Some engineers might counter that without moisture the scale of turbulence will be too small to upset an airliner. This could be resolved with computational fluid dynamics modeling or scaled experiments to see if dry hot air of X MW can significantly affect an aircraft. If not, one might accept dry plumes as fine if no moisture. But FAA’s official stance is to avoid even invisible plumes ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=commission%2C%20the%20FAA%20recently%20added,most%20critical%20phases%20of%20flight)), which aligns with the precautionary approach.

- **Data Center Industry Position:** We have community reporting (e.g. Virginia residents complaining of noise and pollution ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=compared%20to%2019,coming%20in%20and%20out%20of)) ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=The%20main%20reason%20for%20opposition%2C,risks%2C%20from%20a%20simple%20outage))) vs. industry statements downplaying those concerns. Industry representatives often highlight that data centers are quiet neighbors compared to factories or truck depots (no constant trucks, minimal emissions during normal operation) ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=nuisance,more%20or%20less%20noisy%20cooling)). They might argue that on an airfield, a data center is far less obtrusive than, say, an oil terminal or manufacturing plant that some airports have had nearby. So the disagreement is whether data centers are “not ordinary” in risk (due to cooling/generators) or actually quite benign. Evidence comes in on both sides: data centers do produce significant heat and in outage can be heavy polluters, but most of the time they sit quietly humming. This debate often surfaces in public hearings – e.g., one side brings up worst-case diesel fumes, the other side says “that happens one hour a month, if that”.

- **Wildlife Hazard Degree:** Environmental assessments from developers tend to claim they’ll have no open water and thus no wildlife issue, often treating it as a non-issue or easily mitigated. Wildlife experts, however, sometimes note unforeseen attractants (like warm water discharge or even the flat roof pooling water) can still draw birds. There might be disagreement on whether a dry detention basin is “safe.” FAA’s stance is clear in writing (no standing water) but local interpretation can vary. Resolving this might require monitoring – after building, are birds actually a problem? If yes, further action needed. It’s a wait-and-see difference in opinion.

- **Electromagnetic Interference Likelihood:** Some technical folks assume a modern data center is a sealed, FCC-compliant node that won’t possibly interfere with robust aviation systems. Others more cautiously note that even a small probability of interfering with an ILS during low-visibility landings demands thorough vetting. Since real cases of EMI from data centers are not recorded in literature, this remains a theoretical debate. One might test radio spectrum around an operating data center to see if noise floor is elevated. Without evidence, this disagreement stays theoretical – and usually the more conservative view (check everything) wins by default in aviation.

- **Economic vs. Safety Perspective:** Though not strictly “literature,” there is a conflict between economic development advocates and aviation planners. Loudoun County’s decision to curtail new data centers in certain areas came after heavy citizen pushback ([dgtlinfra.com](https://dgtlinfra.com/loudoun-county-route-7-data-centers/#:~:text=match%20at%20L204%20decided%20that,TLUC%20made%20a%20motion%20to)) – but proponents were citing huge tax revenue and downplaying issues. The airports (MWAA) might be caught in the middle: data centers bring revenue and potential airport business (more flights, maybe carrying tech workers or equipment) but also raise safety/regulatory questions. There’s a tension: local authorities might push a project forward for jobs/taxes, while airport officials stress a more cautionary approach. We saw an example in the Byron power plant case: local energy commission vs. pilot safety group (AOPA) had to be balanced ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)). The resolution often comes via compromise (permit the facility but add mitigations/monitoring). If either side feels that compromise doesn’t fully address their concerns, the issue lingers (for example, AOPA wrote that FAA should proactively study plumes more – indicating they accepted the decision but wanted more action to close knowledge gaps ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=commission%2C%20the%20FAA%20recently%20added,most%20critical%20phases%20of%20flight)) ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=The%20FAA%20is%20also%20conducting,results%20anticipated%20later%20this%20year))).

- **Future Capacity vs. Present Use:** Another debate is strategic – should airports prohibit things that might limit future runways or approaches, even if today’s operation is fine? Some stakeholders say yes, guarding future expansion is crucial (airport planners). Others (maybe local government or business advocates) say “if and when a new runway is needed decades from now, we’ll deal with it, but right now this land can generate economic value.” We see this disagreement in MWAA’s sale of land: presumably MWAA calculated that a fourth runway is not needed for a long time or can be shifted, whereas critics worry it was shortsighted. Only time will tell, but it’s a live conflict between long-term aviation planning and short-term economic gain.

**What Would Resolve Disagreements:** In many of these areas, more **empirical data** would help. For plumes: field measurements on how a representative data center’s plume affects actual aircraft or detailed fluid modeling under flight conditions. For EMI: spectrum analysis pre- and post-commissioning to confirm no interference. For wildlife: on-site monitoring by wildlife biologists after construction to adapt quickly if an issue arises. And crucially, an integrated policy from FAA that acknowledges these factors would give clear authority – absent that, each disagreement tends to be solved case-by-case often after local squabbles.

As it stands, the precautionary principle is likely to guide airports (safety first, even if evidence is incomplete), whereas developers will seek quantifiable thresholds (“prove that my plume will cause X problem”). Bridging that gap requires collaboration – e.g., allow a project but under strict monitoring and an agreement to shut down or retrofit if unexpected effects occur. Essentially treating initial operations as a trial period with oversight.

## Evidence Gaps

During this research, several pieces of evidence were sought but **could not be found or remain unavailable**, indicating gaps in publicly accessible information:

- **Quantitative plume impact studies by FAA:** We found references to FAA conducting studies on thermal plume hazards circa 2010 ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=The%20FAA%20is%20also%20conducting,results%20anticipated%20later%20this%20year)), but the results or reports of those studies were not located in the public domain. Specifically, any FAA or NASA technical report detailing how exhaust plumes affect various aircraft categories (with numbers for turbulence intensity, etc.) was not found. This leaves a gap in hard data to cite for risk magnitude.

- **Airport-specific case data:** We looked for any incident reports (NASA ASRS or NTSB) involving aircraft encounters with man-made plumes (from factories, power plants, etc.). Aside from anecdotal accounts and general caution statements, no formal incident report was retrieved that explicitly cites an exhaust plume as a cause/factor in an accident or incident. This means we lack concrete case studies of “plume-induced” aviation events, making risk estimation rely on theoretical grounds.

- **Data center on-airport environmental assessments:** For example, if DFW or any other airport did an environmental or safety assessment for an on-site data center (covering plume, EMI, etc.), it is not readily accessible. MWAA’s documents on the sale of land or leasing for data centers were not found online – likely because they might be internal or not required to be detailed. Thus, insight into the decision-making process is limited.

- **Emissions dispersion modeling at altitude:** While air quality permits model ground-level concentrations of generator exhaust, we could not find analysis of pollutant concentration or temperature rise at say 200–500 ft AGL for these sources. This makes it hard to quantify how much a plume might reduce air density (affecting lift) or visibility along a glide path. It’s an analytical gap that ideally would be filled by a study targeting aviation effect rather than environmental compliance only.

- **Data on electromagnetic emissions of large data centers:** We searched for any FCC or technical studies measuring RF interference from data center operations (especially power electronics), but none turned up. This means our discussion on EMI is based on principle and analogies (no measured spectrum to refer to). 

- **Wildlife strike data linking industrial sites:** We know statistically 70–80% of bird strikes happen below 500 ft ([www.jetwhine.com](https://www.jetwhine.com/blog/airport-circular-is-wildlife-nimby-guide/#:~:text=The%20next%20four,3%2C000%20feet%20above%20the%20ground)), but trying to find if any correlation with nearby land uses (e.g., “did having a water treatment plant near the airport increase strikes?”) did not yield anything concrete. A gap remains in connecting specific attractants like warm water discharges to measured wildlife activity. 

- **Mitigation efficacy documentation:** While we proposed mitigations such as wet-dry cooling, we couldn’t find a documented example of a data center (or power plant near airport) implementing plume abatement specifically for airport reasons and reporting the outcome. It would have been useful to cite, for instance, “Plant X installed plume abatement, after which pilots reported no further visibility issues.” That evidence wasn’t found, implying either it hasn’t happened or isn’t public.

- **Regulatory precedent:** We attempted to find if any FAA “Determination of Hazard” letters have ever cited thermal plume or a related issue as the reason. Searching the FAA Docket or Federal Register for that was not fruitful. If such precedent exists, it’s not easily searchable by keywords or not published. This makes it hard to say “FAA has previously stopped a project due to plume concerns,” which would be powerful evidence. 

- **MWAA internal criteria:** It would be ideal to see if MWAA or any airport authority has internal guidelines for data centers. None were publicly available. Perhaps MWAA has informal rules-of-thumb, but we couldn’t retrieve any policy document on this. 

- **Cost-benefit or risk-benefit analysis reports:** While outside strict safety, it would help to see if any study weighed the economic benefit of on-airport data center development against potential operational risk or capacity loss. Such analysis might have been done behind closed doors but not published. This is a gap because it’s often something decision-makers will ask for (quantify the risk vs reward), and we have to infer rather than quote.

In summary, **primary research on aircraft-plume interactions appears limited or inaccessible**, leaving us to rely on general FAA guidance and analogies. This is itself a finding: the floor is thin – the lack of data underscores the need for caution and for future studies to fill those gaps. Airports like MWAA should push for research or at least require the developer to fund detailed studies as part of the project, to convert some of these unknowns into knowns.

## Pull-Ready Data Points

- **Vertical reach of exhaust plumes:** FAA studies predict that a strong industrial exhaust plume can produce significant turbulence over *1,000 feet* above a stack’s top ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=Whether%20plumes%20are%20visible%20or,or%20size%20of%20the%20plume)). (In other words, a plume from a 100 ft structure might affect air up to ~1,100 ft AGL.)

- **FAA warning on plume hazards:** The FAA cautions pilots that high-temperature exhaust plumes can cause turbulence, vertical shear, reduced visibility, oxygen depletion, engine contamination, and even icing – especially during low-altitude flight near approach or departure corridors ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=facilities%2C%20or%20other%20industrial%20systems,corridors%20or%20airport%20traffic%20areas)).

- **Plume effect distance:** An FAA-endorsed analysis found that plume disturbances “may include airframe damage, aircraft upset, and/or engine failure” and are *“most critical… in and around approach and departure corridors”* when air is calm and cold ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=identified%20potential%20hazards%20include%2C%20but,heights%20of%20over%201%2C000%20feet)), implying these effects can occur within a few miles of an airport.

- **Turbulence felt by small vs large aircraft:** Smaller general aviation aircraft are impacted more by plumes – they can feel an exhaust’s turbulence at higher altitudes than a heavy jet would ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap7_section_6.html#:~:text=more%20pronounced%20in%20calm%20stable,plumes%20by%20flying%20on%20the)).

- **Data center water usage (cooling):** Data centers can consume up to *8,500 gallons of water per day per MW* for cooling during peak conditions ([www.potomacriver.org](https://www.potomacriver.org/focus-areas/water-resources-and-drinking-water/water-resources/planning/data-centers-and-water-use-in-the-potomac-river-basin/#:~:text=range%20between%20100,use)). A 30 MW facility could evaporate roughly 255,000 gallons a day, generating a continuous vapor plume.

- **Extent of data center development in VA:** Northern Virginia hosts over *200 data centers*, with Loudoun County alone having ~5 km² of data center buildings – a footprint equal to four downtown Paris districts ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=This%20East%20Coast%20state%20alone,the%20Piedmont%20Environmental%20Council%2C%20which)). This concentration is why unique environmental and operational conflicts are emerging.

- **Tax revenue vs. community impact:** In Loudoun County, data centers now contribute *45% of county tax revenue (~$1.3 billion)*, keeping residential taxes 25% lower ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=This%20East%20Coast%20state%20alone,explained%20Julie)) ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=jobs.%20Over%20the%20years%2C%20,lower%20than%20in%20neighboring%20counties)). Yet 40% of residents surveyed say data centers are harming their quality of life (noise, visuals, etc.) ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=A%20survey%20conducted%20by%20his,for%20industrial%20use%20or%20commercial)), highlighting local tensions.

- **Generator emissions scale:** A single large data center campus can have on the order of *20–50 diesel generators* of 2–3 MW each for backup. During a regional outage, hundreds of such generators could run simultaneously in Northern VA, effectively acting as a dispersed power plant – prompting Virginia DEQ to start a special air monitoring program in 2023 to gauge their pollution impact ([www.potomacriver.org](https://www.potomacriver.org/focus-areas/water-resources-and-drinking-water/water-resources/planning/data-centers-and-water-use-in-the-potomac-river-basin/#:~:text=ICPRB%20www,Distribution%20of%20Data%20Centers)) ([www.arcgis.com](https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=4b236ba9837a455890bbcd8a16fde8a1#:~:text=Virginia%20Data%20Center%20Air%20Monitoring,at%203%3A34%20PM%20Item%20updated)).

- **Wildlife attractant exclusion zones:** FAA guidelines recommend no significant wildlife attractants (e.g. standing water, landfills) within *10,000 feet* of airports serving turbine-powered aircraft (and 5,000 ft for piston-only airports) ([www.jetwhine.com](https://www.jetwhine.com/blog/airport-circular-is-wildlife-nimby-guide/#:~:text=distances,that%20at%20a%20turbine%20airport)). This aims to reduce bird strike hazards in critical flight zones.

- **Bird strike altitude stats:** About *78% of bird strikes* occur below 1,000 ft AGL and *90% below 3,000 ft*, per FAA wildlife strike data ([www.jetwhine.com](https://www.jetwhine.com/blog/airport-circular-is-wildlife-nimby-guide/#:~:text=The%20next%20four,3%2C000%20feet%20above%20the%20ground)). This underscores that the airspace where planes are near a data center plume is exactly where most wildlife hazards also manifest – compounding risk if attractants aren’t managed.

- **Byron power plant case:** A proposed 200 MW power plant *2.6 miles* from Byron (CA) Airport raised safety flags; AOPA cited FAA’s new guidance and warned its thermal plumes could upset aircraft on approach ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=Thermal%20plumes%20from%20a%20proposed,land%20use%20commission%20July%2029)) ([www.aopa.org](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport#:~:text=As%20AOPA%20pointed%20out%20in,departing%20aircraft%20in%20the%20most)). The plant was ultimately approved in 2011 with mitigations, but it spotlighted the need for plume consideration in land use decisions.

- **MWAA land for future runway sold:** MWAA had acquired ~854 acres west of Dulles for a future runway, but in 2018 it sold *424 acres* to a data center developer after keeping 430 acres in reserve ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=Image)) ([patch.com](https://patch.com/virginia/ashburn/data-center-west-dulles-airport-seeks-environmental-permits#:~:text=)). Now an 11-building data center campus (“Digital Dulles”) is planned there, which could constrain options or require careful operational procedures if a runway is ever built nearby.

- **Data centers as “good neighbors” debate:** Economic proponents note data centers produce no traffic and little smoke in normal times – *“Would you rather have a data center or 500 trucks a day?”* as one official asked ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=nuisance,more%20or%20less%20noisy%20cooling)). But opponents highlight the continuous *“humming” noise and potential diesel pollution* during outages ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=compared%20to%2019,coming%20in%20and%20out%20of)) ([www.lemonde.fr](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/05/10/in-virginia-the-world-s-data-center-capital-faces-mounting-opposition-from-local-residents_6753298_19.html#:~:text=The%20main%20reason%20for%20opposition%2C,risks%2C%20from%20a%20simple%20outage)). Both perspectives agree that careful design (soundproofing, emissions controls) is critical for community compatibility, which by extension benefits the airport environment too.

- **FAA Part 77 limitations:** A 50 ft tall data center 1 mile from a runway typically does *not* trigger a formal FAA hazard by height alone (it’s well below glide path surfaces). This reveals a regulatory gap: sub-200 ft structures can sail through Part 77 review even if they create substantial **non-structural hazards** to flight (turbulence, glare, etc.), which requires airports to engage through other channels (local land use, advisory circulars) to address those risks ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=aeronautical%20study%20determines%20otherwise,well%20as%20the%20cumulative%20impact)) ([www.faa.gov](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIR/air0603.html#:~:text=proposed%20structures,Require%20a%20VFR%20operation%2C%20to)).
