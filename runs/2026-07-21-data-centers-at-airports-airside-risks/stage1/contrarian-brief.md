# Contrarian Brief — Data Centers at Airports: Airside Risks

**Role in this run:** I argue *against* the run thesis. The thesis holds that data centers near approach/departure paths carry airside risks (thermal plumes, generators, lighting, EMI, wildlife, stormwater, emergency-response load) that standard height and land-use review misses, and that approval should require independent, affirmative evidence of compatibility. My job is to build the strongest honest case that this thesis is overstated — that the risks are already governed, largely manageable, and in several instances physically smaller than the thesis implies — and that a demand for "independent proof of compatibility" risks becoming pseudo-technical obstruction that raises no aircraft's margin of safety.

This is not advocacy for data centers. It is a stress test. If the run thesis survives this brief, it deserves to be published. Where the thesis is genuinely right, I concede it explicitly in Section 3.

---

## 1. The strongest case against the thesis (7 bullets)

1. **The risk the thesis fears already has a federal home.** Thermal exhaust plumes are not an unregulated gap. FAA addresses them in the Aeronautical Information Manual (AIM 7-6-16, "Avoid Flight in the Vicinity of Exhaust Plumes"), in Part 77 obstruction evaluation, and in a dedicated FAA technical assessment tool for thermal exhaust plume impact. The 7460 filing process already lets FAA demand a plume study. The thesis describes a hole that the regulation has already filled.

2. **The plume-hazard science is about combustion, not data-center cooling.** Every serious turbulence finding — plumes reaching 1,000+ ft above a stack, hazardous vertical velocities — comes from power-plant smokestacks and combustion exhaust: high-temperature, high-velocity vertical jets. A data center rejects *low-grade* heat through chillers, dry coolers, and cooling towers at modest exit temperatures and low exit velocities. Except for the rare hours its backup generators run, a data center is not a combustion source. Borrowing the power-plant turbulence literature and applying it to a chiller yard overstates the physical hazard by an order of magnitude.

3. **The world's two densest data-center clusters already sit next to major hubs — with no reported aviation impacts.** Loudoun County's "Data Center Alley" is adjacent to Washington Dulles (an MWAA airport). Elk Grove Village — the second-largest US data-center market — abuts O'Hare, the busiest complex airfield in the country. Decades of coexistence, hundreds of facilities, dozens of generators, and no documented pattern of turbulence upsets, nav interference, or bird strikes traceable to these buildings. That is a large natural experiment, and it points the wrong way for the thesis.

4. **Visible vapor is not aerodynamic turbulence, and conflating them is the thesis's biggest vulnerability.** A cooling-tower plume you can see is condensed water vapor — an aesthetic and psychological object, not necessarily a turbulence hazard. The run brief itself warns against conflating visible vapor, exhaust, heat haze, and turbulence. A compatibility standard that reacts to what a pilot *sees* rather than to measured vertical velocity would fail its own logic.

5. **A dry, automated building is a *better* airfield neighbor than most land uses it replaces.** FAA's wildlife guidance (AC 150/5200-33) targets putrescible-waste landfills, wastewater plants, wetlands, and open water. A data center attracts none of those. It replaces farmland, retail, or vacant parcels — frequently *lowering* the wildlife attractant profile near the runway. On the single risk FAA regulates most aggressively near airports, data centers usually improve the picture.

6. **EMI from data centers is a solution in search of a problem.** Data centers are RF-quiet by design and by law: FCC Part 15 caps unintentional emissions, and shielding is a commercial necessity. The documented aviation-EMI problems come from *intentional* transmitters (wind-farm radar clutter, broadcast towers) — data centers don't transmit. There is no documented case of a data center degrading an ILS, VOR, or GPS approach.

7. **Generators and emergency load are routine, bounded, and already everywhere near airports.** Backup generators run during grid outages and brief monthly tests — on the order of tens of minutes per month — under EPA emissions tiers and air-permit hour caps. Airports themselves operate large backup-generator fleets on the field. A low-occupancy, highly automated, clean-agent-suppressed building is not a mass-casualty occupancy; it is a lighter emergency-response burden than the hotel, arena, or hospital that zoning would otherwise permit on the same parcel.

## 2. Evidence

**FAA already regulates exhaust plumes as an aviation hazard.** FAA maintains a "Technical Guidance Assessment Tool — Thermal Exhaust Plume Impact" for land-use review, and AIM §7-6-16 formally instructs pilots to avoid flight in the vicinity of exhaust plumes from smokestacks and cooling towers [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf] [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html]. FAA's Airport Obstruction Standards Committee (AOSC) supplemented 2006 guidance on aircraft overflight of industrial exhaust plumes, and the Part 77 / OE-AAA process (roughly 200,000 aeronautical studies per year) can already require a plume study before approval [Source: https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/flight_info/aeronav/obstruction_evaluation/]. The mechanism the thesis wants — an evidence demand triggered by proximity — exists.

**The turbulence findings are combustion findings.** The frequently cited result that "the significant turbulent effects of an exhaust plume can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the height of the top of the stack or cooling tower," with the worst cases in "calm winds, low temperatures, and neutral or unstable stratification," comes from the exhaust-plume literature built around power-plant stacks and combustion jets [Source: https://www.aviationairportdevelopmentlaw.com/2012/11/articles/federal-aviation-administration-faa/faa-finally-issues-guidance-on-plume-hazards-to-aircraft-from-power-plants-or-does-it/]. Cooling-tower physics is different in kind: the exhaust "is initially a forced jet which transitions to a buoyant plume higher up," with turbulent mixing that dilutes the thermal signal as it rises [Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10652-021-09790-w]. Data-center heat rejection is lower-temperature and lower-velocity still. The hazard exists on a spectrum, and data-center cooling sits near the bottom of it, not the top.

**Cooling is trending toward *less* plume, not more.** The industry is moving to closed-loop liquid cooling and "zero-water" designs precisely because evaporative cooling is water- and plume-intensive [Source: https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/blog/closed-loop-cooling-in-oracle-ai-data-centers-2026-02-09/] [Source: https://sustainabilitymag.com/news/how-are-companies-pioneering-data-centre-zero-water-cooling]. A closed-loop or dry-cooled facility produces little or no visible plume at all. The technology arc is bending away from the exact mechanism the thesis worries about.

**The revealed-preference evidence is a running natural experiment.** Elk Grove Village — abutting O'Hare — is described as the second-leading data-center hub in the United States after suburban Washington, D.C., with a dozen-plus facilities and campuses up to 250 MW and 1.18 million square feet on a single site [Source: https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/t5-expands-again-chicagos-elk-grove-village/] [Source: https://www.costar.com/article/675681587/another-data-center-is-taking-off-near-chicagos-ohare-airport]. The leading US hub, suburban Washington's Loudoun County, sits next to MWAA's own Dulles. New multi-billion-dollar campuses are being sited immediately adjacent to airports (e.g., a ~380-acre, six-building program north of Kansas City International) [Source: https://www.kctv5.com/2025/08/26/100-billion-data-center-planned-north-kansas-city-airport-pave-way-newcomers/]. If proximity produced airside incidents, the densest clusters next to the busiest fields would have surfaced them by now.

**Wildlife guidance points the other way.** AC 150/5200-33 recommends siting *wastewater plants, landfills, wetlands, and open water* 5,000–10,000 ft from runway ends because they attract hazardous wildlife [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5200-33B.pdf]. A dry data center is none of those categories. On the risk FAA treats most seriously, the data center is usually the compatible use — and often an *improvement* over the parcel's prior or alternative use.

**Even industry siting practice already discounts the runway-end parcel.** Data-center site-selection guidance treats land at the immediate end of an active runway as unsuitable for mission-critical facilities [Source: https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/how-to-assess-a-propertys-data-center-potential]. The most sensitive locations are self-selecting out of the developer's own shortlist, which shrinks the population of genuinely worrying sites the thesis has to worry about.

## 3. Where the thesis is right — concessions I cannot argue away

Intellectual honesty requires naming the scenarios where my position fails and the run thesis holds.

1. **The prolonged-outage generator scenario is real and is the controlling case.** My "tens of minutes per month" argument collapses during a multi-day grid failure, when a large campus can run its entire diesel fleet continuously. At full site load, an AI campus of 150–250 MW could operate dozens of large generators for days. *That* is a combustion source — hot, sustained exhaust — and it is exactly the condition (and the atmospheric window: calm, cold, stable air) the plume-turbulence literature flags. If a campus sits under a low approach segment, the extended-generation scenario deserves the affirmative study the thesis demands. This is the single strongest point on the thesis's side, and I concede it.

2. **"No reported impacts" is not "no impacts."** My natural-experiment argument leans on absence of evidence. Turbulence upsets on approach are often attributed to wake, weather, or "unknown," and thermal-plume causation is rarely investigated or logged. The Elk Grove/Loudoun record may be quieter than reality. Absence of a documented incident is weaker proof than the thesis's demand for affirmative, pre-approval evidence.

3. **Cooling is not standardized — and my "low-grade heat" claim doesn't cover every design.** A large evaporative-cooling installation on a hot campus can throw substantial visible plume and latent heat. Where a developer chooses water-intensive evaporative cooling over closed-loop, the plume signature is materially larger, and the thesis's caution is warranted for that specific design. Compatibility is design-dependent, which is precisely the thesis's point.

4. **Future airfield capacity is a legitimate, non-physics concern.** Even if today's plume is benign, a permanent 1.2-million-square-foot building forecloses a future runway extension, taxiway, or approach redesign. "It doesn't hurt current operations" is not the same as "it preserves optionality." Land-use permanence is a real cost the height-only review does miss — and here the thesis is simply correct.

5. **Concentrated new electrical load and stormwater are genuine second-order risks.** Large campuses reshape the local grid and can add substantial impervious surface and detention ponds — open water near a runway is an FAA wildlife attractant. Poorly designed stormwater is one way a data center *does* import the exact hazard AC 150/5200-33 warns against. This is controllable by design, but it is not automatic.

Net: the thesis is strongest on the *prolonged-generation plume scenario*, *stormwater/open-water wildlife risk*, and *capacity foreclosure*. It is weakest on *EMI*, *routine cooling plume*, and *emergency-response burden*, where the fear outruns the physics.

## 4. Data points and quotes the Strategist must address or concede

1. **The regulatory gap may not exist.** FAA already directs pilots to "avoid flight in the vicinity of exhaust plumes (smoke stacks and cooling towers)" (AIM §7-6-16) and maintains a thermal-plume assessment tool for land-use review [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html]. The Strategist must explain what "independent evidence of compatibility" adds beyond a plume study FAA can already require — or concede the thesis is arguing for *better use* of existing tools rather than a new standard.

2. **The 1,000-foot number is a combustion number.** "The significant turbulent effects of an exhaust plume can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the height of the top of the stack or cooling tower" [Source: https://www.aviationairportdevelopmentlaw.com/2012/11/articles/federal-aviation-administration-faa/faa-finally-issues-guidance-on-plume-hazards-to-aircraft-from-power-plants-or-does-it/]. If the Strategist cites plume turbulence, they must specify the *operating scenario* (normal cooling vs. full-fleet generation) that produces it, or the figure is imported under false pretenses.

3. **The revealed-preference record.** Elk Grove Village is "the second leading hub for data centers in the United States, outside of suburban Washington, D.C." — both clusters adjacent to major hubs, both without a documented pattern of airside impact [Source: https://www.makerswanted.org/success-stories/elk-grove-village-approves-major-deal-to-solidify-its-position-as-midwest-data-center-hub/]. The Strategist must reconcile a strong precautionary standard with two decades of quiet coexistence next to the busiest fields — including MWAA's own Dulles.

4. **The wildlife inversion.** FAA recommends siting landfills, wastewater plants, and open water 5,000–10,000 ft from runway ends [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5200-33B.pdf]. A dry data center is none of these. The Strategist should concede that data centers usually *reduce* wildlife attractant risk — and confine the wildlife argument to the stormwater/detention-pond vector, which is the only place it holds.

5. **The technology is de-risking itself.** The move to closed-loop and zero-water cooling [Source: https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/blog/closed-loop-cooling-in-oracle-ai-data-centers-2026-02-09/] means the marginal new campus is *less* plume-generating than the last one. A screening framework should reward closed-loop/dry designs and reserve scrutiny for evaporative cooling and prolonged-generation exposure — not treat "data center" as a monolithic hazard class.

---

## Bottom line for the Council

The honest contrarian position is not "data centers are harmless." It is narrower and harder to dismiss: **the real airside risk collapses to two or three specific, controllable scenarios — prolonged full-fleet backup generation under stable cold air, evaporative-cooling plume, and stormwater/open-water wildlife attraction — plus the non-physics problem of permanent capacity foreclosure.** Everything else in the thesis (routine cooling plume, EMI, emergency response, lighting) is either already governed, physically minor, or better than the parcel's alternative use.

The strongest challenge to the run thesis is therefore this: **name the controlling scenario and regulate *that*, rather than demanding a blanket "prove compatibility" standard that treats a chiller yard like a smokestack.** If the Strategist's screening tool is scenario-specific — triggered by evaporative cooling, by generator fleet size and run-hour exposure, by detention-pond siting, and by capacity-foreclosure geometry — the thesis is right and this brief strengthens it. If the tool is a generic precautionary gate applied to every building over a proximity line, this brief is the argument that will be used to defeat it at the zoning board.
