# Airline Commercial Strategist Brief — Data Centers at Airports: Airside Risks

**Run:** data-centers-at-airports-airside-risks
**Lens:** Airline commercial strategy, hub-and-spoke economics, cost-per-enplanement sensitivity, use-agreement leverage, and carrier response to airport land-use decisions.
**One-line thesis of this brief:** A data center on airport land is a bet that near-term non-aeronautical revenue is worth more than optioned airfield capacity and operational reliability. That is a bet on the airport's own real estate against the carriers' future network. The carriers will settle the bet, not the appraiser — so the decision must be modeled from the carrier's side of the table, in the one arena (non-aeronautical land use) where the carriers have no contractual vote.

---

## 1. Key findings

1. **A data center is a non-aeronautical revenue instrument first, a building second.** Non-aeronautical revenue is roughly 46% of total U.S. airport revenue (2025 ACI-NA benchmark), and under residual and hybrid-residual rate methodologies that money is credited against airline charges. So the first-order carrier reaction to a data-center lease is *approval*: it lowers cost-per-enplanement (CPE). That alignment is real, and it is a trap — it buys carrier silence on the one variable (short-term cost) while doing nothing to protect the two variables carriers actually optimize for at a hub (capacity and reliability). [Source: https://blog.aci.aero/airport-economics/airport-non-aeronautical-revenues-commercial-strategy/]

2. **MWAA has already run this play, at the airport this run is really about.** MWAA sold ~424–433 acres at Dulles to Digital Realty for **$236.5 million** in September 2018 (net ~$207M), explicitly to build a fund "that could be used to reduce the fees added to the tickets of every passenger who boards a flight at Dulles." This is the CPE-lowering mechanism made literal. It is also a permanent conveyance of airfield-adjacent land. [Source: https://biz.loudoun.gov/2018/09/21/what-the-land-sale-at-dulles-airport-means-for-you] [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers]

3. **CPE is a spread, not a number — and non-aero revenue is one of the few levers that moves it without touching the airfield.** Large-hub CPE in FY2024 ran from **$3.93 (ATL) to $36.01 (JFK), median ~$12.88**. Carriers benchmark every hub in their network against that spread and divert marginal growth toward the low end. A data center that shaves a dollar off CPE is genuinely valuable to a carrier — which is exactly why the airport should be suspicious of using CPE relief as the headline justification. [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]

4. **The decision that can hurt the carriers most is made where the carriers have the least say.** Majority-in-interest (MII) clauses give signatory airlines a dual-majority veto over airline-funded *capital projects*. They almost never extend to *non-aeronautical land-use* decisions. So a data-center lease that later forecloses a taxiway, a run-up pad, a de-ice pad, or a parallel-runway option is decided in the one room where carriers cannot vote — and they will remember that when the constrained airfield project finally comes up for an MII vote they *do* control. [Source: http://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/airport-capital-review]

5. **The controlling scenario for the commercial case is not normal operation — it is the reduced-arrival-rate condition.** Banked hubs land arriving waves inside 15–30 minute windows. Any data-center externality that shaves peak arrival rate in specific wind/weather states (thermal plume, generator exhaust reducing visibility, a wildlife-attraction event, an emergency-response closure) does not read as a nuisance in carrier data — it reads as a reliability tax on every connecting bank. When the FAA cut SFO's arrival capacity ~33% in poor-visibility conditions, the trade press called it a "blow to United's hub," not a weather footnote. [Source: https://liveandletsfly.com/sfo-capacity-cut-united-airlines/] [Source: https://simpleflying.com/rolling-banked-hubs-aviation-guide/]

6. **Dulles is the case where the trap is easiest to spring — because United already dehubbed it once.** United mainline carried **~49.9% of IAD enplanements in 2025**, ~62% branded with United Express. IAD has spare airfield capacity precisely *because* United thinned the hub after the Continental merger. That makes foreclosing "future capacity" look free today. It is not free — it is cheap at the bottom of a carrier cycle, and carrier cycles turn. IAD posted the **highest growth rate of the nation's 50 largest airports in 2025**, and MWAA is preparing a Dulles expansion plan. Optioned airfield land is worth most right when you are tempted to sell it cheapest. [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/07/02/dulles-saw-highest-growth-rate-in-2025-of-nations-50-largest-airports/] [Source: https://airlineweekly.skift.com/2021/07/united-airlines-may-finally-get-a-new-concourse-in-washington/]

7. **FMV appraisal systematically underprices the thing the carriers care about.** FAA Order 5190.6C requires non-aeronautical leases at fair market value, and data centers pay premium land rents (Amazon: **$27.72M for 20 acres at Manassas, >$1.3M/acre, $4–6M/yr**). But the appraisal prices the land as real estate. It does not price the *option value of preserved airfield capacity* to a future hub carrier, which is a carrier-side externality no land appraisal captures. The airport can be fully FMV-compliant and still have made a bad network bet. [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers] [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airports/article/53082175/navigating-airport-land-use-regulations-to-grow-nonaeronautical-revenue]

8. **The veto question governs the whole thesis.** Every mitigation the engineers propose — plume modeling, generator siting, lighting shrouding, wildlife management — should be tested against one question: *does it survive the reduced-capacity scenario, and would a hub carrier accept it in a use-agreement negotiation?* If the answer requires the carrier to eat reliability risk it did not price, the mitigation is incomplete regardless of how clean the normal-ops case looks.

## 2. Evidence

### The alignment that makes this dangerous: CPE and residual rate-setting

Non-aeronautical revenue is now ~46% of total U.S. airport revenue (2025 ACI-NA Concessions Benchmarking Survey) [Source: https://blog.aci.aero/airport-economics/maximizing-non-aeronautical-revenues-key-to-airport-financial-sustainability/]. How it reaches the airlines depends on rate methodology: under **pure residual**, non-aero revenue is credited against airline costs dollar-for-dollar; under **hybrid residual** (the norm at U.S. large hubs), the airport carves out some non-aero revenue outside the airline formula, so only credited revenue reduces rates [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/airline-use-agreements]. The commercial point: a data-center lease structured to flow through the residual formula is a direct CPE reduction, and carriers will not oppose their own cost relief. That is the alignment. The danger is that the alignment is entirely on the *cost* axis, and a hub carrier's existential axes are *capacity* and *reliability*, which the lease does not touch — until it does, negatively.

CPE is the number carriers use to rank hubs. FY2024 large-hub CPE spanned $3.93 (ATL) to $36.01 (JFK), median ~$12.88, with outliers above $20 driven by capital programs [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/large-hub/cpe]. Carriers divert marginal growth toward the low-CPE end of their own network and pressure the high-CPE end. A dollar of CPE relief is real leverage — which is why "this data center lowers your CPE" is exactly the kind of true-but-incomplete claim a strategist should distrust.

### The leverage asymmetry: MII covers capital, not land use

An MII clause gives signatory airlines collective veto rights over specified airport decisions, "most commonly capital improvement projects that will be funded through airline rates and charges," typically requiring a dual majority — majority by number *and* by activity (landed weight or enplanements) [Source: http://dwuconsulting.com/airport-finance/articles/airport-capital-review]. Note what MII does *not* generally cover: a non-aeronautical ground lease funded by a third party (the data-center developer), not by airline rates. The airport can execute that lease without an MII vote. So the commercial hazard is structural: **the land-use decision that can constrain a future airfield project is made outside airline consent, but the constrained airfield project later requires airline consent.** The carriers arrive at the second decision already constrained by the first, and with a grievance about a process they were shut out of.

### Why the reduced-capacity scenario controls the commercial case

Hub economics are bank economics. Airlines coordinate arriving and departing waves so passengers connect inside tight windows — arriving flights land within "often 15–30 minutes," then a fixed ground time, then departures [Source: https://simpleflying.com/rolling-banked-hubs-aviation-guide/]. Peak arrival rate is the binding constraint on how large a bank can be. Anything that reduces peak arrival rate in identifiable conditions shrinks the feasible bank, and a smaller bank means fewer viable connections, which is the entire product a connecting hub sells. When runway/arrival capacity is cut, carriers respond mechanically: schedule reductions, and in-the-moment, "all or a portion of the affected bank of an airline's aircraft could be sent to an alternate hub" [Source: https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27983/chapter/6 / banked-hub disruption literature]. The SFO precedent is the cleanest read on carrier framing: an FAA-driven ~33% arrival-capacity cut in poor visibility was reported as a direct hit to United's hub, because that is precisely how the carrier's network math treats it [Source: https://liveandletsfly.com/sfo-capacity-cut-united-airlines/].

The implication for this run: the airport-design review that clears a data center on *normal cooling / clear-day* assumptions is answering the wrong question. The controlling scenario is the state in which the facility's externality (visible plume, exhaust, heat shimmer degrading approach visibility, a wildlife pull, or an emergency-generation event) coincides with the weather/wind state that already compresses arrival capacity. If those two states correlate, the data center is not a marginal reliability cost — it is a multiplier on the airport's worst hours, which are the hours carriers weight most heavily.

### FMV compliance is not network compliance

FAA Order 5190.6C requires non-aeronautical property to be leased at fair market value, and below-FMV non-aero leasing is treated as revenue diversion [Source: https://dwuconsulting.com/dwu-ai/faa-order-5190-6c-airport-compliance-manual]. Data centers clear FMV comfortably — Amazon paid $27.72M for 20 acres (>$1.3M/acre) at Manassas, projected at $4–6M annual revenue [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers]. The commercial critique is not that the rent is too low; it is that the appraisal prices *land as land* and ignores the option value of that same land as *preserved airfield capacity* to a future or returning hub carrier. That option is worth the most in exactly the situation MWAA faces at IAD — a dehubbed field at the bottom of a carrier cycle, where spare capacity looks abundant and therefore cheap to sell. Selling the option at the cyclical low is the classic airport error, and it is invisible to an FMV appraisal.

## 3. Case studies of carrier response to airport decisions

**Digital Dulles / MWAA (2018–present) — the alignment case, running now.** MWAA marketed three Dulles parcels and sold the largest (~424 acres) to Digital Realty for $236.5M, explicitly to fund airline-fee relief and expansion [Source: https://biz.loudoun.gov/2018/09/21/what-the-land-sale-at-dulles-airport-means-for-you]. Carrier response: none adverse — United's cost base fell, which United wants. This is the "carriers approve because it lowers CPE" dynamic in the field. It is also a *land conveyance*, not a lease, which removes even the residual optionality of getting the acreage back. The test this run should apply: were those parcels ever candidates for airfield expansion, run-up/de-ice pads, or a future parallel-runway protection surface? If yes, MWAA monetized capacity option at the trough. If genuinely no, the deal is defensible — but that is a factual airfield question, not a real-estate one, and it should be answered in airside terms.

**United dehubbing of IAD (post-2010 Continental merger) — the cycle that makes foreclosure look free.** United thinned IAD after absorbing Continental's Newark hub, leaving IAD with airfield capacity in surplus to its schedule. That surplus is why MWAA has land and appetite to lease it. The commercial warning: IAD then posted the highest growth rate among the nation's 50 largest airports in 2025, United is building a new concourse, and MWAA is drafting a Dulles expansion plan [Source: https://airlineweekly.skift.com/2021/07/united-airlines-may-finally-get-a-new-concourse-in-washington/] [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2026/07/02/dulles-saw-highest-growth-rate-in-2025-of-nations-50-largest-airports/]. Carrier demand for a field is not static; it is cyclical. Foreclosing capacity when a carrier has just pulled back is selling insurance the moment before you need it.

**The dehubbing template in reverse — CVG, PIT, STL, CLE, MEM.** In each classic dehubbing, the airport had *built infrastructure the carrier then walked away from*: Cincinnati's terminals under Delta, Pittsburgh's midfield terminal under US Airways, St. Louis under TWA/American, Cleveland under Continental/United, Memphis under Northwest/Delta. The lesson usually drawn is "don't overbuild for one carrier." The mirror-image lesson for this run is sharper: **don't permanently foreclose the infrastructure a carrier would need to stay or return.** A data center that eliminates the airfield's growth path does the dehubbing damage pre-emptively — it removes the carrier's option to grow before the carrier has decided whether to. The determining variable in every dehub was the carrier's decision, not the airport's building. A land-use strategy that does not model the 20-year carrier decision is repeating the error from the other direction.

**SFO arrival-capacity cut — how carriers actually score a reliability hit.** When arrival capacity fell ~33% in poor visibility, the framing was a "blow to United's hub" [Source: https://liveandletsfly.com/sfo-capacity-cut-united-airlines/]. No press release from the airport can offset what shows up in on-time and cancel data feeding a carrier's network-planning model. This is the evidentiary standard the thesis argues for: not "does the building avoid the current obstruction surface," but "does independent evidence show it preserves arrival rate in the controlling weather state." Carriers already apply that standard internally; the airport should apply it before approval, not discover it in the next schedule.

## 5. The veto, stated plainly

The airport's fate at IAD is more determined by what United (and any future growth carrier) decides than by anything MWAA builds or leases. A data center that lowers CPE is aligned with the carrier on the one axis that is cheapest to satisfy and silent on the two axes — capacity and reliability — that decide whether a hub lives. Three tests before approval, each framed as *what will the carriers do in response?*:

1. **Capacity option:** Does the parcel sit inside any airfield growth path (parallel runway protection, taxiway, run-up/de-ice pad, apron expansion) in a 20–30 year horizon? If yes, a permanent conveyance or long-dated lease sells the carrier's growth option at a cyclical low. Require the airfield-planning answer in airside terms, not a real-estate appraisal.
2. **Reliability under the controlling scenario:** Does the facility's worst-case externality (peak-demand plume, generator exhaust, wildlife pull, emergency-response closure) correlate with the weather/wind states that already compress arrival rate? If the bad states coincide, the facility multiplies the airport's worst operating hours — the hours carriers weight most.
3. **Use-agreement durability:** Would a hub carrier sign a long-dated, low-CPE use agreement over a field whose airside reliability now depends on a third-party tenant's cooling and generation behavior? If the honest answer is "only until reliability slips," the CPE relief is a short-dated asset and the capacity foreclosure is permanent — a bad trade.

Any Council recommendation that clears a data center on the strength of revenue or CPE relief, without answering these three, is incomplete by construction. The carriers will answer them whether or not the airport does.
