# Emergency Management Director Brief: Data Centers on the Airfield's Doorstep

**Run:** Data Centers at Airports — Airside Risks
**Lens:** Director of Emergency Management, top-tier US hub
**Date:** 2026-07-21

---

I have run the EOC for the power outage that grounded a hub, the active shooter, the hurricane evacuation, and the CrowdStrike morning when every kiosk in the terminal went dark at once. I read this thesis the way I read every new thing somebody wants to build on my property: not "does it clear the obstruction surface," but "what does it do to me at 0300 on the worst night of the year." A data center is not a warehouse with servers. It is a concentrated fuel farm, a battery hazard, a heat source, and a water feature, assembled by people whose entire business model is *not* being interrupted — sited next to an operation whose entire business model is also not being interrupted. Those two continuity plans collide in exactly the scenarios I am paid to survive. Standard height-and-land-use review does not see the collision. I do.

The thesis is correct, and it is correct for a reason the real-estate and airspace shops usually miss: the controlling risk is not the normal operating day. It is the **prolonged utility outage**, when the same regional event that stresses my airfield puts dozens of megawatts of diesel into continuous emergency generation a few thousand feet from my runway, refueled by tanker convoys, bridged by lithium-ion batteries that cannot be put out with water. That is the scenario I would build the screening around.

---

## 1. Key findings

- **The controlling scenario is prolonged emergency generation during a utility outage — and it is correlated with my own worst day.** A grid failure, an ice storm, or a hurricane does not politely hit the data center on a day the airport is calm. The facility's diesel fleet spins up for days precisely when my ARFF and mutual-aid partners are already committed. Any compatibility review that models only "normal cooling" is measuring the wrong day.

- **A large data center is a hazardous-materials site by any honest reading of Part 139.321, whether or not anyone calls it that.** Individual backup generators run 1–4 MW each, "about the size of a train car," each with a fuel tank sized for 24–72 hours of runtime [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/08/qts-aurora-data-center-diesel-generators/]. A campus can field dozens. In one Virginia county, 4,700 generators are permitted at 12 gigawatts of capacity [Source: https://environmentamerica.org/center/resources/false-emergencies-real-pollution/]. That is a concentrated fuel load and spill exposure adjacent to the movement area that no terminal or hangar comes close to.

- **Lithium-ion UPS is the fire my department is worst-equipped to fight.** The September 2025 South Korean government data-center fire took 200-plus firefighters, 60-plus vehicles, and roughly 22 hours to control; responders avoided water to protect equipment, thermal runaway kept reigniting, and the incident took down 647 government services [Source: https://www.networkworld.com/article/4065542/south-koreas-data-center-fire-triggers-global-scrutiny-of-lithium-ion-batteries-and-dr-architecture.html]. Thermal runaway vents toxic, flammable gas and reignites hours after knockdown [Source: https://www.greenlancer.com/post/thermal-runaway]. A 22-hour structural fire that ties up mutual aid is, for me, an airfield-availability event by another name.

- **The thermal plume is a real airspace risk that the thesis is right to separate from "visible vapor."** FAA warns pilots to avoid flight in the vicinity of exhaust plumes from smokestacks and cooling towers because vertical shear and turbulence can extend more than 1,000 feet above the source [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html]. FAA has a dedicated Technical Guidance Assessment Tool for thermal exhaust plume impact precisely because a clear-air, invisible plume can be hazardous while a harmless visible vapor cloud is not [Source: https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html].

- **The stormwater footprint is a wildlife-attractant problem, and wildlife strikes are an EM problem.** Data centers carry large stormwater and (for evaporative designs) process-water features. FAA AC 150/5200-33B and ACRP Report 125 are explicit that retention impoundments attract hazardous birds; detention basins must drain within 48 hours and stay dry between storms [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5200-33B.pdf]. From 1990–2005 the FAA logged 66,392 wildlife strikes, 97.5% birds [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169204613001138].

- **EMI to navigation and communications is under-studied for this specific land use — which is itself the finding.** NAVAID ground monitors do not detect every interference source, and large switching loads and variable-frequency drives are credible emitters [Source: https://www.faa.gov/headquartersoffices/ato/radio-frequency-interference-rfi-radio-spectrum-planning]. There is no FAA study that squarely addresses data-center emissions against airport CNS systems. Absence of evidence is not evidence of compatibility.

- **This thesis strengthens the case for EM at the table — and exposes how often EM is not at the table.** Height review, ALP amendment, and airspace study are run by planning, engineering, and airspace shops. The generator fuel load, the battery fire, the plume, the pond, and the mutual-aid draw are all EM-owned risks that fall between those desks. A review that clears every obstruction surface can still hand me an un-fightable fire and a broken continuity plan.

## 2. Evidence

**Fuel load and spill exposure.** Data-center backup generators typically produce 1–4 MW each, are physically the size of a rail car, and each carry a tank sized for at least 24 hours — 48 or 72 hours at mission-critical or seismic sites [Source: https://letonpower.com/technical-specifications-for-data-center-diesel-generators/] [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/08/qts-aurora-data-center-diesel-generators/]. A single Aurora, Colorado campus sought to add 98 generators to 40 already installed [Source: https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/08/qts-aurora-data-center-diesel-generators/]. Under revised 14 CFR 139.321, the airport operator carries defined responsibility for handling and storing hazardous substances, including tenant fuel storage [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139]. A data center on airport land pushes a large new hazardous-materials inventory into a plan built around jet fuel — and into the same SPCC, fire-code, and spill-response posture.

**Battery fire behavior.** NFPA 855 (2023, referenced by the 2024 IFC) governs stationary energy-storage and lithium-ion installations and requires thermal-runaway mitigation, ventilation, suppression, and an emergency-response plan [Source: https://usmadesupply.com/resources/guides/nfpa-855-lithium-ion-battery-fire-protection]. The failure mode matters more than the code citation: thermal runaway is exothermic, self-sustaining, vents flammable and toxic gas, and reignites after apparent extinguishment [Source: https://www.greenlancer.com/post/thermal-runaway]. South Korea logged 543 battery-fire incidents in 2024, up from 359 in 2023 [Source: https://www.ess-news.com/2025/09/30/south-korea-hit-by-digital-pearl-harbor-as-aging-lges-battery-sparks-data-center-fire/]. The operational signature — long duration, water-avoidance, reignition watch, toxic plume — is the opposite of the aircraft-fire profile my ARFF crews train and certify against.

**Thermal plume and airspace.** AIM 7-6-16 directs pilots to avoid exhaust plumes from smokestacks and cooling towers; turbulence and vertical shear from a hot, vertically directed plume can extend over 1,000 feet above the top of the source, worst in calm, cold, stable air [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html]. FAA's Airport Obstruction Standards Committee tasked and MITRE validated the plume studies underlying the current Technical Guidance Assessment Tool. The thesis is right to insist this be evaluated by clear-air vertical velocity, not by whether a cloud is visible — the two are routinely conflated in land-use argument.

**Stormwater and wildlife.** FAA AC 150/5200-33B and ACRP Report 125 (*Balancing Airport Stormwater and Bird Hazard Management*) treat impounded water inside the separation distances as a hazardous-wildlife attractant, recommend detention over retention, a 48-hour maximum detention, steep-sided narrow basins, and physical exclusion (bird balls, wire grids, netting) where water cannot be relocated [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5200-33B.pdf] [Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169204608000339]. A hyperscale campus's cooling and stormwater design has to be screened against this, not just against drainage code.

**Continuity — the ATL lesson.** On December 17, 2017, an underground switchgear fire at Atlanta cut power for roughly 11 hours, canceling more than 1,000 flights and stranding some 30,000 travelers; the backup feed was co-located with the primary, so a single fire took both [Source: https://www.utilitydive.com/news/11-hour-power-outage-at-atlanta-airport-grounds-thousands-of-flights/513273/]. The lesson the field actually absorbed was *physical separation of redundancy* [Source: https://blog.somax.com/could-the-atl-airport-power-outage-have-been-prevented/]. A data center is the built embodiment of the opposite instinct — extreme power density and redundancy concentrated in one place. Sited near me, it either competes for the same utility feeds and substation capacity I depend on, or it becomes a second critical load the region will expect me to help protect. Both are EM problems.

**Emergency-response doctrine.** The Airport Emergency Plan (AC 150/5200-31, NIMS/ICS-based) already extends ARFF responsibility beyond aircraft to structural fire, hazmat, and medical response, and leans on mutual-aid agreements (ACRP Synthesis 45) for anything that exceeds on-field resources [Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7170197/] [Source: https://crp.trb.org/acrpwebresource13/acrp-synthesis-45-model-mutual-aid-agreements-for-airports/]. A large data center enlarges every one of those hazard categories while adding zero ARFF index capacity. My mutual-aid draw goes up; my staffing does not.

**Documented siting.** These are not hypothetical neighbors. A ~380-acre, six-building, 1.8-million-square-foot data-center project ("Kestrel") is planned on an industrial megasite immediately north of Kansas City International, and a $100B data-center corridor is proposed near KCI [Source: https://www.kctv5.com/2025/08/26/100-billion-data-center-planned-north-kansas-city-airport-pave-way-newcomers/]. Airports are actively marketing surplus land for this use as non-aeronautical revenue [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers]. The pressure to say yes is real, which is exactly why the screening has to be disciplined.

## 3. The preparedness case — for and against the thesis

**For (my read):** The thesis holds because it correctly identifies that the binding constraint is a low-probability, high-consequence, *correlated* scenario, and correlated risk is what standard reviews are worst at pricing. Obstruction and height review is a steady-state, geometric test. It has no way to represent "48 hours of continuous diesel generation and a 22-hour battery fire happening during the same ice storm that closed a runway." The activations that bear on this most directly:

- **ATL 2017** proves that co-located, correlated failures defeat paper redundancy, and that an 11-hour infrastructure event is a national story. A data center concentrates precisely the failure geometry ATL warned against.
- **South Korea 2025** proves the lithium-ion fire is a multi-day, mutual-aid-draining, water-averse event. Drop that fire load next to a Part 139 field and the controlling question becomes runway and mutual-aid availability, not property value.
- **The FAA plume and wildlife ACs** prove FAA already regulates heat sources and water features near flight paths — the data center simply presents both at once, at scale, in a land use FAA has not yet purpose-studied.

For an EM director, this thesis is the argument I have been trying to make for 25 years: the risk that matters is invisible on a normal day and decisive on the worst one, and the people who screen new construction do not carry the pager on that night.

**Against (the strongest honest counter):** Three points cut against overstating the case, and I would not let the report pretend they don't.

1. **Most of these risks are engineerable if EM is in the room early.** Fuel load can be split, diked, and SPCC-compliant; batteries can be sited in dedicated, separated, deflagration-vented rooms per NFPA 855; ponds can be detention-designed and netted; generators can be exhaust-modeled. None of this is exotic. The failure mode is *process* — EM not being consulted — more than *physics*.

2. **A data center can strengthen continuity, not only threaten it.** A neighbor with hardened power, its own generation, and 24/7 staffing can be an asset in a regional outage — a partner in a mutual-aid or islanded-microgrid arrangement rather than only a competitor for fuel. The stronger programs would negotiate that into the lease, not litigate it after.

3. **Comparable heat and fuel sources already coexist with airports.** Power plants, hospital generator farms, and refineries operate near flight paths under existing FAA plume and land-use guidance. The data center is a variation on a managed problem, not a wholly new hazard class. Honest analysis should say so and then argue why *scale and correlation* still warrant a purpose-built screen.

The thesis survives the counter because the counter is really an argument for *process discipline* — which is the thesis. "It's engineerable if EM is consulted early" concedes the whole point: standard height-and-land-use review does not consult EM early, and that is the gap the screening framework must close.

## 4. Anchors a strategist can build on

1. **Atlanta, December 17, 2017 (continuity).** 11-hour outage, 1,000-plus cancellations, co-located backup defeated by one switchgear fire. Use it to frame correlated-failure risk and the "physical separation of redundancy" doctrine a data center inverts [Source: https://www.utilitydive.com/news/11-hour-power-outage-at-atlanta-airport-grounds-thousands-of-flights/513273/].

2. **South Korea government data-center fire, September 2025 (ARFF/mutual aid).** 200+ firefighters, ~22 hours, water avoided, reignition, 647 services down. The concrete picture of a lithium-ion fire as an airfield-availability event [Source: https://www.networkworld.com/article/4065542/south-koreas-data-center-fire-triggers-global-scrutiny-of-lithium-ion-batteries-and-dr-architecture.html].

3. **FAA AIM 7-6-16 + Thermal Exhaust Plume Assessment Tool (airspace).** The authority for evaluating clear-air plume turbulence to 1,000+ feet, and the reason to separate invisible turbulence from visible vapor in any compatibility finding [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html].

4. **FAA AC 150/5200-33B + ACRP Report 125 (wildlife/stormwater).** The regulatory hook for treating a data center's ponds and cooling water as a wildlife-attractant screen — 48-hour detention, dry basins, exclusion measures [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5200-33B.pdf].

5. **14 CFR 139.321 + AC 150/5200-31 (AEP) + ACRP Synthesis 45 (mutual aid).** The doctrine that already makes tenant hazmat, structural fire, and mutual-aid capacity the airport operator's problem — the frame for demanding an EM-led review before approval [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139].

**Screening recommendation for MWAA (EM contribution):** Before any near-airfield data-center approval, require (a) a controlling-scenario emergency-response study modeled on *prolonged emergency generation during a regional utility outage*, not normal operation; (b) an aggregate fuel-inventory and SPCC/spill analysis treating the campus as a single hazmat site under 139.321; (c) an NFPA 855 battery hazard and suppression plan with an explicit ARFF and mutual-aid impact assessment; (d) a clear-air thermal-plume evaluation using the FAA assessment tool against actual approach/departure geometry; (e) a wildlife-attractant assessment of all water features under AC 150/5200-33B; and (f) an EMI/RFI compatibility study against airport CNS systems. EM signs the compatibility finding, not just planning and airspace.
