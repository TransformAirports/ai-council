# Virtual-Chris Brief — Data Centers at Airports: The Lattice

**Run:** data-centers-at-airports-airside-risks
**Lens:** connections, adjacencies, and openings the risk analysis will miss
**Stance:** optimist with a fact-checker's discipline. The other agents will catalog the hazards. My job is to hand the Strategist the alliances and trades that turn "here are the risks" into "here is how MWAA turns a risk it cannot avoid into leverage it can use."

---

## Why this thesis is unusual: MWAA is not a bystander, it is the landlord

Most Council theses ask MWAA to opine on an industry pattern. This one lands in MWAA's own front yard — literally. In 2018 MWAA sold roughly 424 acres of "Western Lands" immediately west of Dulles to a data-center developer for **$236.5 million**, land it had originally banked for a fourth runway. Dulles sits at the edge of Loudoun County's "Data Center Alley" — ~200 facilities, close to 50 million square feet, the densest data-center market on earth, with more in the pipeline. ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/washington-dc/news/data-center/data-center-developer-to-buy-424-acre-site-next-to-dulles-airport-92924), [Loudoun County](https://www.loudoun.gov/ArchiveCenter/ViewFile/Item/13979))

So the honest framing is not "should airports allow data centers near flight paths." MWAA already monetized future airfield capacity to fund present operations. The thesis is really: **can the authority that made that trade build the screening discipline to make the *next* one on its terms — and can it convert the data-center wave from a land-use headache into an energy, revenue, and resilience position it does not currently hold?** That reframing is the whole value of my lens. The risk agents will argue MWAA should be cautious. I will argue MWAA is already committed, and the move now is to lead the standard rather than react to the permit.

## 1. The lattice — connections this thesis activates

- **Real Estate ↔ Western Lands ↔ MWAA's own bond covenants.** The land that funds the airport is the land that constrains the airfield. Every acre leased to a hyperscaler is non-aeronautical revenue the rating agencies love *and* a mortgage on future runway options. The department that books the lease and the department that protects Part 77 surfaces answer to the same CFO — but rarely to the same meeting. Convening them is step one.

- **Airfield Operations ↔ thermal-plume review ↔ the developer's own cooling choice.** The controlling variable is not "data center: yes/no," it is *which cooling system.* Evaporative/cooling-tower designs throw visible plumes and buoyant heat; closed-loop liquid and air-cooled designs throw far less. That means the risk is *contractual*, not fixed — MWAA can price it into the lease. The opposition is negotiable because the developer has a design menu.

- **Grid/Energy ↔ the substation ↔ interconnection queue.** Utility interconnection now runs **5–7 years**; substation transformers have stretched past **160 weeks** of lead time. ([Landgate](https://www.landgate.com/news/power-first-data-centers-in-2025-how-grid-constraints-are-repricing-land-leases-and-revenue), [DatacenterKnowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/build-design/ai-data-center-boom-rewires-us-power-supply-chain)) An airport that co-develops substation capacity holds the scarcest asset in the deal. This is the connection nobody in the land-use review is looking at, and it is where MWAA's leverage is highest.

- **Emergency Management / Public Safety ↔ backup generators ↔ Part 139 response load.** A hyperscale campus running prolonged emergency generation is a fuel-storage, air-quality, and mutual-aid event sitting next to a certificated airfield. The same generators that are a risk in a utility outage are a *resilience asset* if the airport negotiates shared access. One department's hazard is another's redundancy.

- **Wildlife / Environmental ↔ stormwater ponds ↔ AC 150/5200-33C.** Large impervious campuses need detention basins; standing water is a hazardous-wildlife attractant on a certificated field. FAA guidance already tells you the fix — dry basins, riprap, no emergent vegetation. ([AC 150/5200-33C](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf)) This is a solved problem masquerading as a new one; the connection to make is between the developer's civil engineer and the airport wildlife biologist *before* the site plan is drawn.

- **Sustainability / Community Affairs ↔ waste heat ↔ terminal and de-icing loads.** A data center rejects enormous low-grade heat as a nuisance. An airport consumes enormous low-grade heat — terminal HVAC, pavement/apron snow management, de-icing fluid heating. The plume the risk analysis fears is, physically, a district-heating resource the sustainability office would pay for. Same molecule, two ledgers.

- **Federal Affairs ↔ FAA OE/AAA ↔ the plume-study gap.** FAA's obstruction-evaluation process (Form 7460) reliably tests *height* against Part 77 surfaces but has no mandatory thermal-plume trigger — plume guidance lives in the advisory AIM (§7-6-16), addressed to pilots, not to permitters. ([FAR/AIM](https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html)) That gap is exactly the thesis's point — and it is also an opening: MWAA can require the study FAA does not, and become the reference case.

## 2. Three pattern matches

**A. The cell tower / wind-farm "glint and plume" precedent (aviation, recent).** When wind turbines and, earlier, tall stacks proliferated near airfields, the FAA process caught *height* but was slow to catch secondary effects — radar clutter, and for power plants, thermal plumes. The Byron Municipal Airport fight (a proposed plant <3 miles out, pilots reporting invisible plumes lofting light aircraft 300–500 feet on final) forced the issue, and the MITRE study of 2012 followed, producing AIM guidance rather than a binding standard. ([AOPA](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2010/august/05/thermal-plumes-a-potential-danger-near-airport), [Aviation & Airport Development Law](https://www.aviationairportdevelopmentlaw.com/2012/11/articles/federal-aviation-administration-faa/faa-finally-issues-guidance-on-plume-hazards-to-aircraft-from-power-plants-or-does-it/))
*Mapping:* Data-center cooling is the power-plant plume problem, relocated and multiplied. The lesson is not "block it" — the lesson is that when federal guidance lags a technology, the operators who write their own screening standard set the de facto rule. MWAA can be the airport that defines "compatible" before FAA does, exactly as a few airports defined turbine-siting practice ahead of formal circulars.

**B. Railroads and the grain elevator, 1870s (economic history).** Railroads discovered that the scarce asset was not track — it was the *terminal and the siding* where grain met the line. Whoever controlled the interchange point priced the whole flow. Data-center power is the same structure: GPUs are abundant, land is findable, but **grid interconnection is the siding** — and it is jammed for half a decade. ([Ascend Analytics](https://www.ascendanalytics.com/blog/large-load-interconnection-queues-data-center-grid-access))
*Mapping:* An airport with banked land, an existing utility relationship, and room for a substation controls the interchange. MWAA should stop thinking of itself as a landlord renting dirt and start thinking of itself as the grain-elevator operator who owns the one siding everyone needs. That is a fundamentally stronger negotiating chair.

**C. Mycelial nutrient trading in a forest (biology).** Mycorrhizal networks move carbon and nutrients between trees along fungal threads, routing surplus from where it is abundant to where it is scarce — heat and sugar as currency. The network's value is not any node; it is the *routing*.
*Mapping:* The data center is a node that produces surplus heat and consumes power; the airport is a node that consumes heat and controls land and grid access. Run as separate boxes, each fights the other (plume vs. runway). Wired as a network — waste heat to terminal loads, generators to shared resilience, substation to shared queue-jump — the surplus of one becomes the input of the other. The thesis's "risk" is an unrouted resource. My whole argument is: route it.

## 3. The opportunity register (ranked by 24-month actionability)

1. **Publish an MWAA "Airside-Compatible Data Center" screening standard.** *Most actionable.* The thesis already asks for the decision tool; make it a public, branded standard — cooling-type disclosure, mandatory thermal-plume CFD study, generator air-quality and fuel plan, dry-basin stormwater, EMI test against nav aids, lighting cutoff spec. **First step:** direct Planning + Ops + Environmental to draft a one-page term sheet of required studies within 60 days; it costs almost nothing and immediately reprices every future lease conversation. First-mover airports become the citation everyone else copies.

2. **Negotiate waste-heat and standby-power rights into the next ground lease.** *High value, needs a willing developer.* Trade favorable lease terms for a right of first refusal on rejected heat and negotiated access to campus backup generation during airport emergencies. **First step:** add both clauses to the standard MWAA data-center lease template before the next parcel goes to market.

3. **Co-develop substation / interconnection capacity as a shared asset.** *Highest dollar upside, longest lead.* Because MWAA already needs resilient power (see PIT's islanding microgrid), a jointly funded substation serves both the campus and the airport and lets MWAA monetize its position in the queue. **First step:** commission a load-and-interconnection feasibility study with the serving utility and Loudoun before committing acreage.

4. **Position MWAA as the national reference case for aviation–data-center compatibility.** *Reputation play, cheap.* Given Dulles's location at the center of Data Center Alley, MWAA is the most credible voice in the country on this exact question. **First step:** offer to co-author an ACRP synthesis or FAA working-group input on data-center siting near airfields — turning a local problem into industry authorship.

5. **Bundle the data-center energy build with airport decarbonization goals.** *Slower, politically useful.* Shared generation, waste-heat recovery, and possible on-site renewables let MWAA report the campus as part of its own sustainability trajectory rather than against it. **First step:** have Sustainability score the next proposal for net airport energy and emissions effect, not just lease revenue.

## 4. Collaboration paths — the three hardest oppositions

**Opposition 1: Airfield Operations / the ALP guardians vs. Real Estate.** Ops sees mortgaged capacity and a plume near final approach; Real Estate sees the non-aeronautical revenue that keeps cost-per-enplanement down.
*Path from against to with:* Ops does not actually oppose revenue — it opposes *unpriced* risk. Give Ops the veto it wants in the form of the screening standard (Opportunity 1), and it gains authority rather than losing runway. **Who convenes:** the CFO, because both report to the same balance sheet. **First meeting:** not "should we do this" but "what studies make you comfortable" — turning a boundary fight into a checklist Ops co-authors.

**Opposition 2: The FAA regional office vs. a novel land use with no clear rule.** The regulator's instinct with an unmapped risk is delay, because the AIM gives it guidance for pilots but no permitting hook.
*Path:* Bring FAA a finished screening protocol and a plume CFD study *before* it has to invent one. Regulators move faster reviewing a proposal than authoring a policy. **Who convenes:** MWAA's Federal Affairs plus FAA Airports District Office. **First meeting:** MWAA presents its draft standard and asks FAA to pressure-test it — casting the authority as partner, not applicant.

**Opposition 3: Loudoun County / community and grid stakeholders vs. more data-center load.** The county is increasingly wary of data centers' grid draw, water use, and tax-vs-nuisance balance; another campus reads as more of the same.
*Path:* MWAA's federal land and existing power relationship let it offer what a greenfield developer cannot — a *managed* project with shared substation, curtailment discipline (the model Texas SB 6 now mandates — grid-cost recovery, backup-power disclosure, peak curtailment), and waste-heat reuse that reduces net community impact. ([Logistics Viewpoints](https://logisticsviewpoints.com/2025/12/17/from-microgrids-to-hypergrids-the-unprecedented-power-demands-of-data-centers-combined-with-the-deep-pockets-of-the-hyperscalers-building-them-are-creating-a-new-grid-architecture-that-scales-the-m/)) **Who convenes:** MWAA Community Affairs + county economic development. **First meeting:** frame the campus as the *best-behaved* data center in the county because the airport, not a merchant developer, sets its operating envelope.

## 5. Pull-ready observations

1. **"MWAA is not deciding whether to allow data centers near Dulles. It sold the 424-acre Western Lands to a data-center developer in 2018 for $236.5 million. The only open question is whether the next deal happens on the airport's terms or the market's."**

2. **"The scarce asset in a data center is not the land or the chips — it is the grid interconnection, and it is jammed for five to seven years. An airport with banked land, a utility relationship, and room for a substation controls the one siding everyone needs. That is a landlord who should be pricing like a toll operator."**

3. **"The controlling variable is the cooling system, not the building. Evaporative cooling throws a plume; closed-loop does not. That means the airside risk is a lease clause, not a law of physics — and anything that is a lease clause is negotiable."**

4. **"FAA's obstruction process reliably measures height and says almost nothing about heat. When federal guidance lags a technology, the operator who writes its own screening standard sets the de facto rule. Sitting at the center of Data Center Alley, MWAA is the most credible airport in America to write it."**

5. **"The plume the risk analysis fears is, molecule for molecule, a district-heating resource the sustainability office would pay for. Run the data center and the terminal as separate boxes and they fight over a runway. Wire them together and the waste heat of one warms the other."**
