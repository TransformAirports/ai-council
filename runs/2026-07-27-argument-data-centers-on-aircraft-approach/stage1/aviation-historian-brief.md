# Aviation-Historian Brief — Data Center in the RWY 19R/1L Airfield Envelope at IAD

**Prepared for:** The Strategist, "Data Centers on Aircraft Approach" run
**Question I am answering:** Where does this thesis sit in the industry's arc, and what did the last three times this debate happened look like?
**Bottom line:** The recommendation to *not* build in the 19R departure / 1L approach surfaces is not a contrarian call. It is the position the industry has arrived at every time it re-learned the same lesson: airfield-envelope land, once converted to non-aeronautical use, is effectively unrecoverable, and the airports that gave it away paid to buy it back — or moved. The thermal-plume and land-use objections are likewise not novel; the FAA has treated both as hazards for decades. What *is* new is the intensity of the temptation, and Dulles sits at its epicenter.

---

## 1. Key findings — the macro arc this thesis sits inside

- **The load-bearing pattern is "monetize the vacant land vs. protect the airfield envelope," and the industry has consistently learned it the hard way.** Post-deregulation and especially post-2010, airports have been pushed to diversify into non-aeronautical revenue (now ~46% of U.S. airport revenue). Land development is the frontier of that push. The historical record shows the revenue is real but the airfield-protection mistakes are close to irreversible.

- **Airfield land is an option you can only sell once.** Stapleton (relocated at ~$4.8B because it was boxed in) and O'Hare (paid billions and razed a neighborhood to *buy back* the land it needed to reconfigure runways) are the two cases that most directly frame this thesis. Both are stories of development that foreclosed future capacity.

- **This proposal is against the regulatory current, not with it.** FAA land-use compatibility guidance has tightened, not loosened, over the past 15 years — the 2012 RPZ interim guidance and AC 150/5190-4B push sponsors to *remove or mitigate* incompatible development near the runway, not add it. The proposal asks MWAA to move the wrong direction on a trend line the FAA controls.

- **The thermal-plume objection is a recognized, quantified FAA hazard — not speculation.** The AIM formally warns that exhaust plumes cause "turbulence and vertical shear" reaching "over 1,000 feet above the... stack," "most critical... in and around approach and departure corridors." FAA plume-assessment practice uses a ~4.3 m/s vertical-velocity hazard threshold. Data centers with on-site/back-up generation and large heat-rejection sit squarely inside this decades-old body of practice built for power plants.

- **The current data-center-on-airport-land wave is documented and accelerating** — Cedar Rapids, Manassas, Colorado Springs, Buckeye, and an Air Force base-leasing program in 2025 — so the board pressure behind this proposal is a real macro force, not a one-off. The argument is stronger for conceding that the trend is genuine and then drawing the line at *location inside the airfield envelope.*

- **Dulles is the epicenter, and MWAA has already done this once.** Loudoun County is the largest data-center market on earth. MWAA reportedly already monetized ~433 acres of Dulles land to Digital Realty in 2018. That precedent is the strongest thing the proponents have — and the cleanest way to frame the counter: the question is not *whether* MWAA sells land to data centers (it has), but whether *this particular parcel*, in the 19R/1L surfaces, is the one parcel it should not.

- **The honest counter-case:** non-aeronautical land revenue is a legitimate, FAA-sanctioned strategy, and a data center that clears obstruction (Part 77 / TERPS), plume, glare, and wildlife review is a compatible use *somewhere* on the property. The thesis wins only if it stays disciplined about *location* rather than sliding into "data centers are bad neighbors for airports." The airfield-envelope argument is the durable one; the general one is not.

## 2. Evidence

**The revenue pressure is structural and real.** Non-aeronautical revenue is roughly 46% of total U.S. airport revenue (2025 ACI-NA benchmarking), and land development is the growth frontier as traffic-linked revenue plateaus [Source: https://blog.aci.aero/airport-economics/airport-non-aeronautical-revenues-commercial-strategy/]. Industry advisers now market airport land explicitly as prime data-center real estate — "robust infrastructure... reliable access to power grids and advanced connectivity," able to "command premium prices" [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers].

**The wave is national and specific.** Named 2023–2025 deals include a $750M project at the Eastern Iowa Airport (Cedar Rapids), a 20-acre Amazon parcel for $27.72M by Manassas Regional Airport, a ~100-acre "Project Edge" at Colorado Springs, and a 2,069-acre data-center park at Buckeye Municipal (AZ) [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers]. The Department of the Air Force opened base land to private AI data centers in 2025 (>100 MW, >$500M capex thresholds) [Source: https://www.eplaneai.com/news/air-force-plans-to-lease-base-land-for-private-ai-data-centers]. This is a genuine macro cycle, not a local anomaly.

**Dulles is the epicenter, and MWAA already sold in.** Loudoun County hosts the world's densest data-center cluster ("Data Center Alley," 25M+ sq ft), directly adjacent to IAD [Source: https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/loudoun-county-is-home-to-26-million-sq-ft-of-data-centers-dulles-cloud-south-could-add-another-56-million-sq-ft-if-it-passes/]. A trade source reports Digital Realty bought ~433 acres of Dulles land for $236.5M in 2018, generating ~$207M for the Airports Authority *(secondary source; verify against MWAA/DOT records before quoting the dollar figures)* [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers]. MWAA is separately pursuing a utility-scale solar project at Dulles, already drawing outside comment [Source: https://www.pecva.org/region/loudoun/pec-weighs-in-on-dulles-airport-solar-project/]. The airport is under active, layered pressure to convert land to energy/data uses.

**The thermal-plume hazard is codified, not hypothetical.** FAA AIM 7-6-16 states exhaust plumes "can cause significant air disturbances such as turbulence and vertical shear," effects that "can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the... stack," and that "these hazards are most critical during low altitude flight in calm and cold air, especially in and around approach and departure corridors" [Source: https://faraim.org/faa/aim/chapter-7/section-7-6-16.html]. FAA plume-impact assessment practice treats vertical velocity in excess of ~4.3 m/s as a potential hazard to aircraft operations, formalized in a 2015 FAA technical guidance/assessment tool [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf]. The FAA has been forced to rule on power-plant plumes near airports for years [Source: https://www.aviationairportdevelopmentlaw.com/2012/11/articles/federal-aviation-administration-faa/faa-finally-issues-guidance-on-plume-hazards-to-aircraft-from-power-plants-or-does-it/]. A data center's generation and heat-rejection load is exactly the profile this practice was built to screen.

**The regulatory trend runs against incompatible development in the envelope.** FAA's 2012 interim RPZ guidance directs regional/ADO staff to consult headquarters whenever "buildings and structures or specific land uses" — including transportation, fuel and utility facilities — would sit in the RPZ, and instructs sponsors to "protect against, remove, or mitigate" incompatible development [Source: https://crp.trb.org/acrpwebresource13/faa-interim-guidance-on-land-uses-within-a-runway-protection-zone/]. AC 150/5190-4B (Airport Land Use Compatibility) is the current codification of that expectation [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150_5190_4b_Land_Use_Compatibility.pdf]. Grant Assurance 21 obligates the sponsor to keep land at or near the airport compatible with operations; FAA enforces via the Part 13/Part 16 process [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/aip/grant_assurances/assurances-airport-sponsors-2025]. The author's grant-assurance concerns track live FAA doctrine.

**The "you only sell it once" lesson has hard numbers.** Stapleton was "boxed in" by residential development — roadways and I-70 were routed *under* the runways — and Denver's inability to acquire adjacent land (a 1962 request for 1,355 acres of Rocky Mountain Arsenal land was blocked) ultimately forced relocation to DEN, which opened in 1995 on a deliberately oversized ~34,000-acre land bank precisely to never repeat the mistake [Source: https://simpleflying.com/denver-replace-original-international-airport/]. At O'Hare, reconfiguring the airfield required acquiring 433 acres, leveling ~500 homes in Bensenville, and relocating ~1,000 graves from St. Johannes Cemetery, amid years of litigation — the cost of *reacquiring* airfield land developers and residents had taken [Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/as-ohare-expanded-time-lapse-shows-demise-of-bensenville-neighborhood/].

## 3. Two cases that most directly frame this thesis (pre-packaged)

**Case A — Stapleton, Denver: the airport that couldn't protect its envelope and had to move.**
*Stapleton, 1929–1995: hemmed in by residential growth and blocked from acquiring adjacent land (1,355-acre arsenal request denied, 1962), Denver abandoned an entire airport rather than fight for expansion room — replacing it with DEN on a ~34,000-acre land bank sized specifically so the mistake could never recur.* **Lesson for MWAA's decision:** the expensive error is not forgoing one data-center lease; it is converting the one corridor that preserves runway/approach optionality. Denver spent a new airport to buy back the optionality Stapleton gave away. [Source: https://simpleflying.com/denver-replace-original-international-airport/]

**Case B — O'Hare Modernization: paying billions to reacquire land that should have been protected.**
*O'Hare, 2005–2020s: to relocate and add runways, Chicago acquired 433 acres, demolished ~500 Bensenville homes, and moved ~1,000 graves under a multibillion-dollar program fought through federal court.* **Lesson for MWAA's decision:** the airfield-envelope acre is cheap to protect today and ruinously expensive to recover later. A 30-to-50-year data-center ground lease inside the 19R/1L surfaces converts a reversible planning choice into an irreversible physical one exactly where future capacity lives. [Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/as-ohare-expanded-time-lapse-shows-demise-of-bensenville-neighborhood/]

**Case C (local rhyme) — Dulles itself, 2018 → 2025: MWAA has already sold to the data-center market, which is why *location discipline*, not prohibition, is the winning frame.**
*IAD, 2018: ~433 acres reportedly sold to Digital Realty (~$236.5M) as Loudoun became the world's largest data-center market; 2025: a new proposal now targets the 19R departure / 1L approach surfaces.* **Lesson for MWAA's decision:** the board's revenue instinct is legitimate and has precedent — so the credible objection is not "no data centers" but "not this parcel." Concede the trend, then hold the airfield envelope. [Source: https://www.landapp.com/post/maximizing-airport-land-with-data-centers] *(dollar figure secondary; verify)*

## 5. Source-quality note (first-hand vs. reinterpretation)

- **Primary / authoritative:** FAA AIM 7-6-16; FAA thermal-exhaust-plume technical guidance (2015); FAA RPZ interim guidance (2012) via TRB/ACRP; AC 150/5190-4B; FAA Grant Assurances (2025). These carry the airspace, land-use-compatibility, and plume claims directly.
- **Contemporary reporting (reliable but secondary):** CBS Chicago on O'Hare/Bensenville; DataCenterDynamics on Loudoun; Simple Flying on Stapleton→DEN (Stapleton facts are well-documented history; Simple Flying is a popularizer — pair with USGS EROS/ULI case study for a stronger cite).
- **Advocacy / trade, verify before quoting numbers:** LandApp's airport-data-center piece is the source of the **Dulles 2018 433-acre / $236.5M / $207M** figures and the other named deals — useful for the anchor but *confirm against MWAA board minutes or DOT land-release records before the strategist prints the dollar amounts.*
- **What I did not find and the writer should not assume:** a documented FAA obstruction (Part 77) determination or a completed plume/glare/wildlife study *for this specific parcel.* The author's own framing (uncoordinated with the ADO, not on an approved ALP) is consistent with that gap — the strongest version of the argument is procedural ("this hasn't cleared the analysis that FAA doctrine requires") rather than a claim that the hazard is already proven.
