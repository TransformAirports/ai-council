# Infrastructure-Economist Brief: The Land-Economics Case Against a Data Center in IAD's Approach/Departure Surfaces

**Prepared for:** the writer strengthening the argument against MWAA building a data center in the proposed IAD location (within the RWY 19R departure / RWY 1L approach surfaces).
**Angle:** capital economics, opportunity cost, and grant-compliance economics — the money side of a decision the source argument frames mostly in planning/safety terms.
**Date:** 2026-07-27

---

## The one-sentence economic thesis

MWAA already proved it can monetize surplus airport land for data centers — it sold 424 genuinely surplus acres to Digital Realty for $236.5 million in 2018 — so the question is not *whether* to capture data-center value but *which* land to sell; putting a data center on aeronautically strategic land inside the approach/departure surfaces trades a small, recoverable revenue stream for an irreversible, multi-billion-dollar capacity option the Authority can never buy back.

That framing matters because it denies proponents their strongest rebuttal ("data centers are lucrative and Loudoun is the market for them"). Both are true. Neither justifies siting one on protected airfield land.

---

## Key findings (numerically grounded)

1. **MWAA has a working template for data-center monetization — and it used the *opposite* kind of land.** In 2018 the Authority sold 424 acres of "western lands" to Digital Realty Trust for **$236.5 million (~$558,000/acre)**. Those 424 acres were the *surplus* left over after ~414 of an 854-acre parcel (acquired starting 2005) were reserved for the fourth runway. The precedent is monetize-the-surplus, protect-the-core. The current proposal inverts it. [Source: https://www.loudounnow.com/business/424-acres-at-dulles-airport-could-be-sold-for-data-centers/article_4ad47d90-b4db-56d6-a084-21953da14bf0.html]

2. **The land under discussion is the airport's most capital-intensive-to-replace asset class: runway capacity.** A new parallel runway at a major US hub now runs roughly **$1 billion** — Charlotte Douglas's fourth parallel runway is a **$1.006 billion** project, and recent runway programs at O'Hare and Atlanta have exceeded **$1.1 billion**. IAD's fourth runway (1L/19R, opened 2008) and a long-planned fifth runway are precisely the future capacity a data center in the approach/departure surface could foreclose. [Source: https://buildermuse.com/infrastructure/airport-runway-construction-cost-per-mile-2026/] [Source: https://www.flydulles.com/d2-projects-future-fifth-runway]

3. **The revenue is recoverable; the airspace is not.** Data-center land value in the region is real and rising — entitled Loudoun land trades **north of $2 million/acre**, and a November 2025 sale set a county record above **$6 million/acre**. But that same liquidity means MWAA can capture data-center value on *other* parcels at any time. Approach and departure surfaces, once built out and encircled by a hardened, hard-to-relocate facility plus its stormwater and power infrastructure, cannot be re-created at any price. The economics are asymmetric and irreversible. [Source: https://www.loudounnow.com/news/loudoun-s-data-center-land-values-surge-by-19b/article_3b98906a-e58c-11ef-9140-1361f3c59268.html]

4. **FAA rules may make the promised revenue partly illusory.** Federally obligated airport land can be leased/sold for non-aeronautical use only after an FAA-approved land-use change or release, and only if it does **not interfere with current or future aeronautical use**. Any sale/lease must be at **fair market value**, and proceeds must be **reinvested in the airport** under the Revenue Use Policy. A parcel inside the approach/departure surface is exactly the case where FAA release is least likely — so the deal that underwrites the revenue projection may not be approvable. [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airports/article/53082175/navigating-airport-land-use-regulations-to-grow-nonaeronautical-revenue] [Source: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/12/08/2023-27017/policy-regarding-processing-land-use-changes-on-federally-acquired-or-federally-conveyed-airport]

5. **The grant-assurance exposure is a financial risk, not just a compliance footnote.** The Revenue Use and fair-market-value assurances the source argument cites (#19–#22, #29) are enforceable; violations can trigger AIP grant ineligibility and civil penalties. For an airport that funds capital programs partly through federal AIP and PFC-backed debt, jeopardizing grant standing is a balance-sheet risk that dwarfs a single ground-lease income line. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/publications/orders/compliance_5190_6/order-5190-6c-compliance-chapter-22]

6. **Replacement-cost inflation makes preserving the option more valuable every year, not less.** FAA's estimate of eligible NPIAS project costs rose **55% between the 2020 and 2024 reports ($43.6B → $67.5B)**, and construction escalation is running **4–6%/year**. The future runway or protective buffer MWAA would forfeit today gets more expensive — and more impossible — to rebuild tomorrow. [Source: https://buildermuse.com/infrastructure/airport-runway-construction-cost-per-mile-2026/]

7. **The plume and lighting concerns have a cost signature, too.** FAA guidance treats thermal exhaust plumes from on-site power generation as a real navigation hazard evaluated through AC 150/5190-4B and FAA's plume-impact assessment tool. If the facility later requires mitigation, curtailment, or relocation of NAVAIDs/approach lighting, those costs land on the airport — an unpriced liability in any pro-forma that shows only lease income. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150_5190_4b_Land_Use_Compatibility.pdf] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf]

## Evidence section (mechanism + sources)

**The strongest economic mechanism: optionality asymmetry.** Airport airfield land is a real option on future capacity. Its value is not the current-use cash flow; it is the right to build a runway, taxiway, or protective surface later, when demand or a competitor's constraint makes it decisive. Selling that land for a data-center ground lease is selling a call option on billions of dollars of future capability for a fixed, comparatively small premium. Option theory says you do not write that option unless the premium exceeds the option's value — and a ~$200–600k/acre-class land deal does not exceed the value of a ~$1B runway plus the traffic it unlocks. This is the quantitative core the writer should lead with.

**Precedent proves the point rather than undercutting it.** The Digital Realty transaction shows MWAA *can* and *did* do data centers correctly: 424 acres of true surplus, sold at fair market value, revenue returned to the enterprise, no aeronautical interference, land that then entered the county tax base at ~$0.20/$100 in the Metro district. [Source: https://www.loudounnow.com/business/424-acres-at-dulles-airport-could-be-sold-for-data-centers/article_4ad47d90-b4db-56d6-a084-21953da14bf0.html] The 2018 deal and the current proposal differ on the single variable that matters: whether the parcel is aeronautically strategic. Use the precedent affirmatively — "here is how we do this right" — to make refusal look disciplined rather than anti-development.

**The market is not going anywhere, so there is no urgency premium.** Loudoun's data-center assessed base surged 78.7% ($23.7B → $42.3B) to roughly 23% of the county tax base, and land records climbed from ~$2M/acre entitled to a >$6M/acre print in late 2025. [Source: https://www.loudounnow.com/news/loudoun-s-data-center-land-values-surge-by-19b/article_3b98906a-e58c-11ef-9140-1361f3c59268.html] A market this deep and this liquid means MWAA forfeits nothing by declining *this* site and offering a compliant, non-strategic parcel instead. The scarcity is on the aviation side (approach surfaces), not the data-center side (buyers and capital are abundant).

**Regulatory economics cap the upside.** Because proceeds must be reinvested in the airport and priced at fair market value, MWAA cannot treat this as free money to a general fund; the "revenue diversification" argument is bounded by the Revenue Use Policy. [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airports/article/53082175/navigating-airport-land-use-regulations-to-grow-nonaeronautical-revenue] And FAA's 2023 policy on land-use changes on federally acquired/conveyed land raises, not lowers, the bar for converting protected airfield land. [Source: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/12/08/2023-27017/policy-regarding-processing-land-use-changes-on-federally-acquired-or-federally-conveyed-airport]

**Replacement cost is the tie-breaker.** With runway-class projects at ~$1B and climbing 4–6%/year against a 55% NPIAS cost jump in four years, the "we can always build capacity elsewhere later" rebuttal gets weaker annually. [Source: https://buildermuse.com/infrastructure/airport-runway-construction-cost-per-mile-2026/] Every year of deferral makes the preserved option more valuable and its foreclosure more expensive.

## Strongest counter-case (treat it honestly)

The most defensible pro-data-center argument is **non-aeronautical revenue diversification under enterprise-fund pressure**: hub airports increasingly lean on non-aeronautical income to hold down airline cost-per-enplanement, and a data-center ground lease is high-margin, low-headcount, long-duration income of exactly the kind rating agencies like. In a market where the county banked a $19B land-value surge, an airport sitting on developable acreage faces real fiduciary pressure to monetize.

The rebuttal is not "revenue is bad." It is **site selection**: the diversification thesis is fully satisfiable on non-strategic parcels (the 2018 template), so choosing the approach-surface parcel adds no incremental revenue capability while adding irreversible aeronautical risk and grant-compliance exposure. The counter-case argues *for* data centers at IAD in general and *against nothing* about this specific site. That is the seam to press.

A second counter-point — "the project won't penetrate the surfaces, so it's fine" — fails on economics even if true on geometry: non-penetration protects today's operation but still forecloses tomorrow's runway/buffer option and still invites plume, lighting, and wildlife-attractant (stormwater) liabilities the pro-forma ignores.

## Caveats and limitations

- **The current proposal's financial terms are not public.** I found no disclosed acreage, price, or lease structure for the 2025/2026 IAD data-center proposal at issue. The economic magnitudes here are inferred from the 2018 comparable and regional land data, not from the deal itself. The writer should not assert a specific dollar figure for this proposal.
- **The fourth runway's isolated construction cost is not publicly broken out.** Sources give the broader D2 program and Aerotrain figures (~$1.4B each) but not a standalone 1L/19R number. I therefore anchor "runway cost" on comparable new parallel runways (Charlotte ~$1.006B; O'Hare/Atlanta >$1.1B), not on IAD's own history. [Source: https://www.airport-technology.com/projects/washingtondulles/]
- **Land-value figures are county-wide and parcel-specific, not appraisals of the subject parcel.** The $2M–$6M/acre range describes Loudoun transactions generally; the airport parcel's fair market value would require its own appraisal.
- **Some cited land-economics and cost figures come from trade/journalistic sources** (Loudoun Now, Buildermuse, Aviation Pros). Treat these as directional. The regulatory claims (FAA compliance manual, Federal Register, ACs) are primary and load-bearing; lean on those for anything quoted.
- **FAA releasability is a judgment, not a certainty.** I assert that release of approach-surface land is "least likely," which is well-supported by the interference test but is not a documented FAA denial of this specific parcel.
- **"Thermal plume" causation is real per FAA guidance but not quantified here.** FAA explicitly does not itself regulate or model plumes; responsibility sits with local land-use planning. Do not overstate FAA enforcement on that point.
