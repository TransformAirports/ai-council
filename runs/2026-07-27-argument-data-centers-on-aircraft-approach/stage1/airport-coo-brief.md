# Airport COO brief — Data center on the IAD approach/departure surfaces

**Framing from the operator's chair.** I run the airfield tomorrow morning regardless of
who wins the land-use argument. The author's recommendation — do not build the data center
in the currently proposed location — is correct from an operations standpoint, but the
memo is stronger if it stops arguing "data centers are bad" and starts arguing "this siting
converts revenue land into a permanent, un-relocatable operational liability the airport
will own for 40 years." Everything below is aimed at that reframe.

---

## Key findings (operator's read)

1. **The plume concern is not speculative hand-waving — the FAA has a named tool for it.**
   FAA maintains *Thermal Exhaust Plume Impact on Airport Operations: Technical Guidance and
   Assessment Tool*, built on MITRE's Exhaust-Plume-Analyzer. The widely applied hazard
   threshold is a **critical average vertical plume velocity of 4.3 m/s**, and studies cited
   in that literature find significant turbulent effects can extend **more than 1,000 ft
   above the top of a stack or cooling tower**. A large data center's heat-rejection and
   any on-site generation put this squarely in scope on the approach to 1L. The author
   should cite the FAA tool by name; it moves the plume point from "I'm worried" to "FAA has
   a screening standard and this site should be run through it."

2. **The wildlife-attractant point has hard numbers behind it.** AC 150/5200-33C recommends
   a separation of **10,000 ft between the aircraft movement area and any hazardous wildlife
   attractant for airports serving turbine (turbojet) aircraft** (5,000 ft for piston-only),
   and a **5-statute-mile perimeter** where an attractant could cause hazardous movement
   into the approach/departure airspace. A stormwater *retention* pond (standing water) is
   exactly the attractant the AC warns against; the AC's own fix is that basins stay dry
   between rains. Putting a wet pond inside the departure/approach corridor of a turbine
   hub is the textbook thing 33C tells you not to do.

3. **The right process gate is Part 77 / 7460, and it hasn't been cleared.** Any structure
   penetrating the 14 CFR Part 77 surfaces — the 100:1 approach slope running 20,000 ft from
   the runway, or >200 ft AGL — requires a **FAA Form 7460-1** filing at least **45 days**
   before construction, triggering an Obstruction Evaluation/Airport Airspace Analysis
   (OE/AAA). "It doesn't penetrate the surface" is not the finish line: the OE/AAA also
   tests ALP consistency and effect on NAVAIDs, radar, and buried cable. No coordination
   with the FAA ADO means the one federal determination that actually binds the airfield
   has not happened. That is the cleanest procedural kill-shot in the memo.

4. **The strongest argument is capacity land, not any single hazard.** Land inside the
   approach/departure surfaces and the runway protection zones is the scarcest asset a hub
   owns. A data center is a **40-year, un-relocatable, hardened structure with deep utility
   and fiber commitments** — the opposite of the flexible, low-intensity use that belongs
   near a runway end. Once it is built, the option to extend the runway, shift a threshold,
   or add capacity is gone. The operator frames this as **optionality destruction**, not
   just obstruction.

5. **The lighting/NAVAID concern is real but needs the specific mechanism.** The issue is
   not "bright lights are bad" — it is that a large illuminated facility adjacent to the
   RWY 19R Approach Lighting System can degrade a pilot's ability to acquire the ALS/threshold
   pattern in low visibility, and that unstudied RF/EMI from a large electrical load can
   affect glideslope, localizer, and radar. Both are testable in the OE/AAA and the flight-
   procedures review. State them as "must be studied and cleared," not as certainties.

6. **Honest counter-case: non-aeronautical revenue is legitimate and FAA-encouraged.** The
   grant assurances require the airport to be **as self-sustaining as possible** and to
   charge **fair market value** for non-aero land; peers like DFW earn ~65% of revenue from
   non-aeronautical sources. So the memo cannot argue "no data center anywhere." Its
   defensible line is siting + process: right revenue idea, wrong parcel, and not yet on an
   approved ALP or through OE/AAA.

7. **The grant-assurance exposure is genuine but should be argued as risk, not verdict.**
   Assurances 19, 20, 21, and 29 are the load-bearing ones (operation/maintenance, hazard
   removal, compatible land use, ALP consistency). Assurance 22 (economic nondiscrimination)
   is the weaker citation — it obligates the airport to remain *available for aeronautical
   use*, which is really the same capacity-preservation point. Lead with 20/21/29; treat 22
   as supporting, not primary.

## Mechanism (why this fails in execution, in operator terms)

A data center is the single worst neighbor you can put at a runway end because it stacks
**four independent airspace insults** on one parcel: (1) a fixed physical obstruction tested
against Part 77 surfaces; (2) continuous **thermal plume** turbulence on final and initial
climb; (3) a **standing-water wildlife attractant** (the stormwater pond) inside the strike
envelope; and (4) **RF/EMI and light** adjacent to the ALS and NAVAIDs. Any one is
survivable with mitigation. Together, on the approach/departure surface of a turbine hub,
they form a permanent constraint the airport cannot walk back. And unlike a hangar or a
cargo building, a data center's economics and its fiber/power tie-ins make it effectively
impossible to relocate once operational. The operator inherits all four risks and the lost
capacity land, in perpetuity, for a lease payment.

## Named cases and concrete anchors

- **Loudoun County "Data Center Alley" is adjacent to IAD.** The world's largest data center
  concentration already sits next to Dulles; documented externalities include heat radiating
  from server halls and emissions from backup diesel generation. This is the real-world
  proof that the plume/generation concern is not theoretical for this specific airport.
- **DFW non-aero revenue (~65%; largest commercial land agreement ~$2.4M/yr).** Use as the
  counter-anchor: airports *do* monetize land — which is exactly why the argument must be
  about siting and process, not about rejecting the revenue.
- **AC 150/5200-33C separation criteria (10,000 ft turbine / 5,000 ft piston / 5-mi
  perimeter).** The quantitative spine of the wildlife point.
- **FAA plume tool + 4.3 m/s / >1,000 ft above stack.** The quantitative spine of the
  turbulence point.
- **Part 77: 100:1 slope, 20,000 ft, >200 ft AGL trigger, 45-day 7460 notice.** The
  procedural spine.

## Counterevidence the memo must absorb (so it doesn't get knocked down)

- **"It doesn't penetrate the surface, so it's fine."** Rebut with: non-penetration clears
  only obstruction; it does not clear plume turbulence, wildlife, EMI, ALS interference, or
  ALP consistency. Those are separate determinations.
- **"Revenue diversion / self-sustaining mandate says take the money."** Rebut with: fair-
  market non-aero revenue is encouraged *on compatible parcels*; assurances 20/21 cap that
  discretion at the runway end.
- **"Planning already reviewed it in 2025."** Rebut with: a height/land-use screen is not an
  ALP action or an OE/AAA. ALP changes must be evaluated with the same FAA guidance (the ACs,
  ALP SOP 2.00) whether or not you are inside a master-plan cycle.
- **Mitigation exists (dry detention, plume caps, lighting shrouds, EMI shielding).** True —
  which is why the honest ask is "route it through OE/AAA and the ALP process and let the
  determinations decide," not "ban it." That framing is harder to attack and more likely to
  actually stop a bad siting.

## The strongest version of the recommendation

Do not build **at this parcel** without: (a) a filed 7460-1 and a favorable OE/AAA;
(b) an FAA-approved ALP depicting the parcel as non-aeronautical development; (c) a thermal-
plume screen using the FAA tool; (d) a 33C wildlife assessment of the stormwater design; and
(e) NAVAID/ALS/EMI and flight-procedures review. Absent those, the airport is trading
irreplaceable capacity land and four unresolved airspace risks for a lease check — a bad
trade any operator who has run a Monday-morning push would refuse.

### Caveats on my own evidence
- The 4.3 m/s plume threshold is most explicitly codified in CASA (Australian) MOS Part 139;
  the FAA guidance/tool applies the same physics but FAA guidance is advisory, not a
  regulatory bright line. Frame as "screening standard," not "legal limit."
- FAA hosts (faa.gov PDFs) blocked direct fetch; separation and grant-assurance specifics
  are drawn from FAA search snippets and a secondary summary (avplot). Verify exact section
  numbers against AC 150/5200-33C and the AIP Sponsor Assurances PDF before publication.
- I did not confirm the current IAD master-plan status on a reserved future runway; the
  capacity argument holds on RPZ/approach-surface land regardless, but do not assert a
  specific planned 5th runway without checking the current ALP.
