# Chief Engineer Brief — Data Center on the RWY 1L/19R Surfaces at IAD

**Lens:** program-delivery, constructability, airfield design standards, asset/land condition.
**Bottom line up front:** The author's recommendation is defensible on engineering grounds, but the memo leads with its weakest argument (height/surface penetration) and treats its strongest arguments (irreversible land alienation, thermal-plume turbulence, wildlife attraction, EMI/glare near the approach lighting) as secondary. A revised argument should invert that order. The durable engineering case against *this location* does not depend on whether the building penetrates a Part 77 surface — it depends on what a 24/7, energy-intensive, heat-and-water-rejecting, security-fenced industrial use permanently does to airfield-reserve land, to the airspace above it, and to MWAA's federal grant obligations.

---

## 1. Key findings (engineering / standards-grounded)

1. **Height is the weakest objection; lead with land, not altitude.** Data centers are low-rise — typically 1–3 stories, roughly 40–60 ft plus rooftop mechanical. A structure can sit *below* the Part 77 approach and transitional surfaces and still be the wrong thing in the wrong place. The author's own memo concedes this ("even if the project doesn't penetrate these surfaces"). The strongest engineering argument is not "it's too tall" — it is that this parcel is airfield-reserve land inside the 1L/19R design environment and should not be alienated to a 30-to-50-year single-tenant industrial use. Any argument built on surface penetration invites the easy rebuttal: "the FAA will issue a Determination of No Hazard, so it's fine." *(Confidence: high on the framing; see §2.1.)*

2. **A thermal plume is an invisible obstruction the FAA actually evaluates.** Energy-intensive data centers with on-site generation and heat-rejection (cooling towers, generator exhaust) produce vertical thermal plumes. The FAA maintains a "Technical Guidance Assessment Tool for Thermal Exhaust Plume Impact" precisely because rising hot air creates turbulence and vertical shear in the approach/departure corridor. Widely used screening thresholds (CASA-derived, applied internationally) treat vertical plume velocity of **4.3 m/s** as the point requiring analysis and **10.6 m/s** as a higher-concern threshold; studies cited in FAA plume work indicate significant turbulence can extend **more than 1,000 ft above the top of a stack or cooling tower**. On a low approach to 1L, that is exactly the wrong altitude band. *(Confidence: medium-high that FAA evaluates plumes; medium on the exact numeric thresholds — these are screening values, not a bright-line FAA rule; see caveat in §2.2.)*

3. **The stormwater pond is a textbook wildlife attractant, and the standard is specific.** FAA AC 150/5200-33C treats stormwater management facilities that hold standing water as hazardous wildlife attractants. The AC's guidance: detention basins should be engineered to **remain dry between rainfalls**; where water will stand, the basin needs paved/concrete bottoms or steep-sided design to suppress nesting habitat. The AC recommends **5 statute miles** of separation between the airport air operations area and an attractant that could move wildlife into approach/departure airspace, and flags the **10,000-ft** turbine-aircraft separation. Siting a wet retention facility *directly under the departure/approach surface* is the opposite of the design intent. *(Confidence: high.)*

4. **The approach lighting system and navaids are an EMI/glare exposure, not just a lighting nuisance.** The proposal sits near the RWY 19R approach lighting system (ALS). Bright, continuous data-center site lighting can degrade a pilot's ability to acquire the ALS pattern at decision height; large glazed/metal facades and any solar arrays introduce glare. Equally important and under-analyzed in the memo: high-density electrical infrastructure — on-site substations, medium-voltage switchgear, thousands of servers, and rotating/UPS plant — is a potential source of electromagnetic interference to FAA navaids, radar, and communications. This cannot be waved off; it requires a specific FAA study, which has not happened. *(Confidence: high that the exposure exists and requires study; low on magnitude without a filed 7460 and FAA analysis.)*

5. **No FAA filing has occurred, and that is a process failure with teeth.** Any construction that may affect navigable airspace requires an **FAA Form 7460-1** notice under **14 CFR Part 77**, filed at least **45 days** before construction; the aeronautical study runs **~60+ days** and can return a Determination of Hazard, Notice of Presumed Hazard, or conditions. Building without it exposes MWAA to a civil penalty of **$1,000/day**. The memo is right that this has not been coordinated with the FAA Airports District Office (ADO) — and that omission alone is sufficient to pause the project. *(Confidence: high.)*

6. **This is an Airport Layout Plan (ALP) and grant-assurance problem before it is a land-use problem.** MWAA operates under AIP grant assurances. The relevant obligations — **#19 Operation & Maintenance, #20 Hazard Removal & Mitigation, #21 Compatible Land Use, #22 Economic Nondiscrimination, #29 Airport Layout Plan** — are not aspirational. Development inside the airfield environment must be reflected on an FAA-approved ALP, and a 2025 "land use review focused on height and preliminary environmental" is not an ALP action. The author's AC citation is *correct*, not a slip: **AC 150/5190-4B, "Airport Land Use Compatibility Planning" (Sept 2022)**, is the current governing document and explicitly ties compatible-land-use planning to grant-assurance compliance. *(Confidence: high on framework; medium on verbatim assurance wording — see §2.5.)*

7. **The land-banking argument is real and specific to IAD.** MWAA's July 2025 Master Plan preserves long-horizon airfield growth, including a fifth runway that "has been in the airport's designs since 1958," was carried in the 1985 master plan, and was FAA-approved in 2005. IAD is a rare US hub with genuine, board-endorsed room to grow to ~90M passengers. Converting strategically located airfield-reserve land to an industrial use that is effectively permanent (data centers are 20–30 year committed assets with deep tenant fit-out and utility interconnects) forecloses optionality that MWAA has protected for **six decades**. *(Confidence: high on the master-plan facts; medium on whether this exact parcel is a future-runway site — see §2.6.)*

8. **The honest counter-case: data centers are a compatible *revenue* use — just not here.** The FAA encourages airport sponsors to generate non-aeronautical revenue from underutilized land. A data center at IAD is not intrinsically incompatible; Loudoun County is the world's largest data-center market and MWAA capturing that value is rational. The defensible position is therefore **locational, not categorical**: put the data center on genuine non-aeronautical land, outside the RPZ-adjacent airfield environment, outside the approach/departure surfaces, and away from the ALS — after a 7460 filing and ALP action. This concession makes the argument stronger, not weaker, because it removes the "you're just anti-development" rebuttal. *(Confidence: high.)*

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 Height/surface framing and the Part 77 process
Under 14 CFR Part 77, any construction that may affect navigable airspace must be noticed to the FAA on Form 7460-1 at least 45 days before construction; the FAA runs an aeronautical study (typically 60+ days) and issues a determination (Determination of Hazard, Determination of No Hazard, Notice of Presumed Hazard, Does Not Exceed, etc.). Knowing/willful failure to notice carries a civil penalty of $1,000/day. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/central/engineering/part77] [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-77] The key engineering point: a Determination of No Hazard on *height* does not resolve plume, glare, EMI, wildlife, or land-use questions — those are separate exposures the memo correctly raises.

### 2.2 Thermal exhaust plume
The FAA's Airport Obstruction Standards Committee (AOSC) studied exhaust-plume impacts on flight safety, and the FAA publishes a "Technical Guidance Assessment Tool for Thermal Exhaust Plume Impact." [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/environmental/land_use/Technical-Guidance-Assessment-Tool-Thermal-Exhaust-Plume-Impact.pdf] Screening thresholds in wide international use (originating in Australian CASA Part 139.370 practice) treat vertical plume velocity **≥4.3 m/s** as requiring analysis and **10.6 m/s** as a higher screening threshold; site-specific meteorological analysis is expected above those values. Studies referenced in this work predict significant plume turbulence extending **>1,000 ft above the top of the stack/cooling tower.** [Source: https://www.aviationairportdevelopmentlaw.com/2012/11/articles/federal-aviation-administration-faa/faa-finally-issues-guidance-on-plume-hazards-to-aircraft-from-power-plants-or-does-it/]
**Caveat:** The 4.3/10.6 m/s figures are screening thresholds (CASA-derived), not a codified US numeric limit; the FAA evaluates plumes case-by-case. Do not represent 4.3 m/s as "the FAA limit." The defensible claim is: *the FAA recognizes thermal plumes as a flight-safety hazard and evaluates them, and a data center with on-site generation/heat rejection under the 1L approach is exactly the case that guidance exists to catch.*

### 2.3 Wildlife attractant / stormwater
AC 150/5200-33C, "Hazardous Wildlife Attractants On or Near Airports" (Feb 2020, current), governs. Detention basins "should remain totally dry between rainfalls"; where water may stand, the facility should include paved/concrete bottoms or ditch/swale design to prevent nesting-habitat vegetation. Recommended separation is **5 statute miles** between the AOA and an attractant that could move wildlife into approach/departure airspace; the EPA/FAA 10,000-ft criterion applies to turbine-aircraft runway ends. [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5200-33C.pdf] A proposed *wet* retention facility beside the development, under the departure/approach surface, is inconsistent with this AC.

### 2.4 Data-center physical characteristics (why the hazards are non-trivial)
Data centers house dozens of backup generators, "many capable of producing between 1 and 3 MW each," collectively rivaling a conventional power plant. [Source: https://insideclimatenews.org/news/12112025/data-center-diesel-generators-noise-pollution/] A Loudoun County substation fire forced nearby data centers onto diesel backup, audible and detectable (fumes) from a neighboring parking lot — a direct local precedent for on-site generation reality. [Source: https://insideclimatenews.org/news/12112025/data-center-diesel-generators-noise-pollution/] Cooling-tower plumes are "a stream of saturated exhaust air… visible when water vapor condenses," and "may present fogging or icing hazards to [their] surroundings" — a moisture-and-thermal exposure directly relevant to a runway environment. [Source: https://www.staxengineering.com/stax-hub/the-environmental-impact-of-data-centers/] Virginia regulators are actively weighing expanded data-center diesel-generator use, confirming on-site generation is the norm, not the exception, in this market. [Source: https://virginiamercury.com/2025/12/16/virginia-regulators-weigh-expanded-use-of-data-centers-polluting-generators/]

### 2.5 Grant assurances and ALP
The relevant Airport Sponsor Assurances: **#19 Operation and Maintenance** (do not permit activity that interferes with operations/maintenance); **#20 Hazard Removal and Mitigation** (clear/protect terminal airspace, prevent future hazards); **#21 Compatible Land Use** (restrict land use in the airport vicinity to activities compatible with normal operations, including landing/takeoff); **#22 Economic Nondiscrimination** (keep the airport available for aeronautical use); **#29 Airport Layout Plan** (keep an FAA-approved ALP current; development must be reflected on it). [Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/aip/grant_assurances/airport-sponsor-assurances-aip.pdf] [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/publications/orders/compliance_5190_6/order-5190-6c-compliance-appendix-a] AC 150/5190-4B (Sept 16, 2022), "Airport Land Use Compatibility Planning," is the current land-use compatibility AC and exists "to help airports comply with their grant assurances concerning all compatible land use issues, including obstructions… wildlife and bird hazard attractants, and protection of persons and property on the ground." [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150_5190_4b_Land_Use_Compatibility.pdf]
**Caveat:** The wording above is paraphrased from FAA summaries; for a memo that quotes assurances verbatim, pull exact text from the FAA Airport Sponsor Assurances PDF and Order 5190.6C before publication.

### 2.6 IAD future-capacity / land-banking
MWAA's Board approved a 20-year Master Plan in July 2025 envisioning growth to ~90M annual passengers, a fifth runway (~10,500 ft × 150 ft, parallel to 12-30 on the south side), and 24 additional gates; FAA approval is still required. [Source: https://www.ffxnow.com/2025/07/17/new-master-plan-for-dulles-airport-envisions-growth-to-90-million-annual-passengers/] The fifth runway "has been in the airport's designs since 1958," was in the 1985 master plan, and both the fourth and fifth runways were FAA-approved in 2005. [Source: https://virginiamercury.com/2025/07/23/fifth-runway-at-dulles-draws-attention-as-expansion-plans-take-off/] [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/business/d2-projects-future-fifth-runway]
**Caveat:** The board-approved fifth runway parallels 12-30 (south side); the data-center parcel is on the 1L/19R (north-south) surfaces. Do not claim the parcel *is* the fifth-runway site. The correct, defensible claim: MWAA has a 60-year discipline of protecting airfield-reserve land for future capacity, and 1L/19R runway-end land is exactly the kind of asset that discipline exists to protect (extensions, RPZ, taxiway/apron, navaid siting). Design standard for that reserve: AC 150/5300-13B, "Airport Design."

### 2.7 Loudoun regulatory context (constructability/permitting reality)
Loudoun County ended by-right data-center development in March 2025; new data centers now require a special-exception legislative review with public hearings. [Source: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/loudoun-county-virginia-eliminates-by-right-data-center-development] This matters for MWAA's argument two ways: (a) even the host jurisdiction now treats data-center siting as requiring heightened scrutiny; (b) MWAA's own ALP/7460 process is the airport-side analog and has not been run.

## 3. Cost / schedule / program realities a strategist can use

- **Irreversibility is the cost that financial models miss.** A hyperscale data center is a 20–30 year committed asset: deep raised-floor fit-out, dedicated medium-voltage interconnect and on-site substation, fiber, water, and generator yards. Unwinding it to recover the land for a runway extension or RPZ need is not a lease non-renewal — it is a demolition-and-remediation program measured in years and tens of millions. Capacity foreclosed for a 10-year revenue stream is a bad trade against a 60-year airfield asset.
- **The FAA process is the schedule.** A 7460 filing (45-day pre-notice) plus aeronautical study (60+ days), plus an ALP amendment/approval and an environmental determination, plus (given plume, EMI, glare, wildlife) likely a formal FAA airspace study, realistically puts a *defensible* approval path at **12–24+ months** before a shovel — and that assumes a No-Hazard outcome. Any "we can start this year" schedule is optimism bias.
- **Interface risk lands on MWAA, not the developer.** If a plume, glare, or EMI finding later raises approach minimums or forces a navaid relocation, the operational and capital consequence falls on the airport and its airlines — not the data-center tenant. That asymmetry is the structural reason to resolve it before, not after, construction.

## 5. Where I would not overclaim (intellectual honesty)

- **Do not assert the building penetrates a Part 77 surface.** We don't know its height/location precisely; lead with land, plume, wildlife, EMI/glare, and process instead.
- **Do not present 4.3 m/s as a hard FAA limit.** It is a screening threshold; the honest claim is that the FAA evaluates plumes and this use is a classic trigger.
- **Do not claim the parcel is the fifth-runway footprint.** It is on the north-south (1L/19R) surfaces; the fifth runway is on the 12-30 side. The land-banking argument is about airfield-reserve discipline generally.
- **Concede that data centers can be a legitimate airport revenue use** — the objection is location and process, not the land use in the abstract. That concession is the argument's strongest defensive move.
- **Whether specific EMI to FAA navaids will occur is unknown** without a filed 7460 and FAA study. Frame it as an unquantified exposure requiring study, not a proven interference.
