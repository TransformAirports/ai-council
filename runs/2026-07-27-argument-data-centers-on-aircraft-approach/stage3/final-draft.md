# Protect the Land First: Why the Dulles Data Center Should Not Be Built as Sited

An ALSF-2 approach-lighting system extends 2,400 feet from a runway threshold, placing light infrastructure far beyond the pavement edge.[^1] The parcel proposed for a data center sits directly under that final geometry: inside the RWY 19R departure surface and the RWY 1L approach surface, alongside the approach lights, at the least-recoverable phase of flight. The recommendation stands: MWAA should not build the data center in the currently proposed location. But the strongest reason is not the hazard list. It is that this specific ground is strategic airfield capacity the Authority has carefully reserved—and a data center is a commitment you cannot undo.

**Lead with the irreversibility, not the hazards.** Every individual objection in the original review—glare near the approach lights, thermal plume, electromagnetic interference, a wildlife-attracting stormwater pond—is a real concern, but each is also something the FAA is designed to *evaluate and condition*, not automatically reject. Glare near approach lights is subject to published FAA review criteria; EMI to a NAVAID or radar is a recognized "substantial adverse effect" that gets studied, not assumed.[^2] Argue the case on those items alone and you are handing the other side a conditions memo. What conditions cannot cure is the permanent alienation of the land itself.

That land matters because the capacity plan is not hypothetical. On July 16, 2025, the MWAA Board reaffirmed a master plan envisioning growth toward roughly 90 million annual passengers and a fifth runway—a build-out planned in some form since the 1950s, carried in the 1985 master plan, and FAA-approved alongside the fourth runway.[^3] Dulles set an all-time record of 27.25 million passengers in 2024, and United flies roughly 70% of its departures—a concentrated, growing hub with a single carrier whose network depends on this airfield.[^4] The approach and departure surfaces off 1L/19R are precisely the kind of ground you protect to preserve a future runway extension and to keep the airfield compatible with FAA airport design standards. You do not need to prove the extension will be built by a date certain. You need only to recognize that a hyperscale data hall, once built, forecloses the option for the life of the asset. Capacity land is optionality. A data center converts it to cash and pours the option in concrete.

The geometry underneath the parcel is unforgiving. Under 14 CFR 77.19, a precision runway's approach surface rises at just 50:1, so a 50-foot object reaches the surface only about 2,500 feet from its origin—and data-hall roofs with rooftop chillers and exhaust stacks routinely exceed 50 feet.[^5] The departure surface is tighter still, allowing only 25 feet of height 1,000 feet off the departure end.[^6] "Even if it doesn't penetrate" is not comfort; it is a razor-thin margin an operator should not spend on a non-aeronautical tenant. And whatever is built there—plume, glare, or structure—sits in the band where an aircraft on a standard 3-degree glidepath is barely 200 feet above the ground at 3,000 feet from the threshold.[^7]

**The regulatory gate is real, and it should be the demand.** Construction in an approach surface requires a Form 7460-1 filing and an FAA obstruction evaluation ending in a Determination of No Hazard or Hazard; skipping the notice carries a $1,000-per-day civil penalty.[^8] Grant Assurances 19, 20, 21, 22, and 29 bind MWAA, and 20 and 21 trace to statute—49 U.S.C. 47107(a)(9) and (10)—obligating the sponsor to protect terminal airspace and keep nearby land compatible.[^9] Frame the objection on the exact standard the FAA still applies after Section 163 of the 2018 Reauthorization Act narrowed its reach: whether development (A) materially impacts safe and efficient aircraft operation, (B) endangers people or property on the ground, or (C) diminishes the value of prior federal investment.[^10] That last prong is not abstract; two 2026 federal grants alone put roughly $64 million into Dulles—a documented floor beneath the decades of AIP-funded airfield and NAVAID value at stake.[^11] The stormwater pond compounds the problem: current guidance in AC 150/5200-33C recommends 10,000 feet of separation from turbine-aircraft movement areas, and a pond at the runway end sits almost entirely inside that standard.[^12]

**Now the strongest counter-case, honestly.** MWAA has run this play here and called it compliant. MWAA sold 424 acres of Dulles "Western Lands" to Digital Realty for $236.5 million—about $558,000 an acre—with the proceeds intended to hold down Dulles airline costs.[^13] Ashburn next door carries an estimated 70% of global internet traffic; airport-adjacent, power-and-fiber-served land is among the most valuable non-aeronautical real estate in American aviation.[^14] Grant Assurance 24 requires the airport to be as self-sustaining as possible—and on that reading this project *advances* the assurances the original letter invokes—while the FAA rates the overall plume flight-disruption risk as low, significant mainly to light aircraft in calm, cold, or unstable conditions—not to the transport-category jets that serve this airport.[^15]

That case is serious, and it is also answered by its own best example. That sale is not precedent *for* this project—it is the rule this project breaks. The Western Lands were the surplus that remained *after* the fourth runway and its support area were built.[^16] The discipline was: protect capacity first, monetize only what's left over. The current parcel inverts that order, selling the reserved capacity itself. Concede every conditionable hazard; you still cannot condition your way out of an irreversible commitment of land inside an active precision runway's protected surfaces.

So the answer is the gate, not a slogan. No approval on a circulated project email. Require FAA ADO coordination, a completed obstruction evaluation, glare, plume, and EMI analyses, a compliant stormwater design, and a formal ALP amendment consistent with the current Dulles master plan—because this parcel was never designated for non-aeronautical development. And reject *this* siting regardless of how the studies come out, because it forecloses reserved capacity. The Authority operates Dulles under a federal lease running to 2100, under active federal pressure to modernize the airport.[^17] Sell the surplus. Keep the runway.
