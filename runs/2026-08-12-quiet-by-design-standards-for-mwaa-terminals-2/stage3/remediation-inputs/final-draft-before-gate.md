# The Loud Terminal Is the Unintelligible One

**A communications-integrity standard for MWAA terminals — argued as safety engineering, delivered through the Design Manual.**

*Run: quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2 · Final draft (Stage 3) · 2026-08-11*

---

## Executive Summary

MWAA holds approximately $6.99 billion of committed capital at IAD and $2.39 billion at DCA under the fifteen-year Airline Use and Lease Agreement effective January 1, 2025.[^1] On July 29, 2026, the President, the U.S. Transportation Secretary, United's CEO, and MWAA jointly announced a Dulles transformation described at more than $20 billion, provisional pending Board action.[^14] Every square foot of that work passes through the seven-volume MWAA Design Manual — "a mandatory guide with the force of law on the airport property."[^2]

The recommendation is narrow: a tiered communications-integrity standard added to the Design Manual this capital cycle, issued by the Office of Engineering under delegated standards-setting authority, taken to the Board as an information item at the next Business Administration Committee, and escalated to a resolution only if AAAC consultation surfaces material airline objection or an AUL cost-center reclassification. Reference application, subject to phase verification: the DCA Terminal 1 concourse replacement; fallback, the earliest IAD C/D element still in programming. Concourse E, opening this fall, carries the first commissioned STI and ambient baseline under representative traffic.

Keep "quiet-by-design" as the internal engineering name. In every board and press document, say "communications-integrity standard." Otherwise the framing dies on cable news the first Friday after a DCA incident. After the January 29, 2025 midair collision and the February 2026 NTSB final report, no board member should sign a policy paraphrasable as "reduced safety broadcasts."[^3] The standard is defensible only as the opposite: a floor that raises the probability the announcement that matters — the gate change, the shelter-in-place, the IROPS diversion — reaches the passenger who needs it. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 requires a Speech Transmission Index of at least 0.45 at 90% of measurement locations in each Acoustically Distinguishable Space, average 0.50.[^4] ACRP 175 targets 0.50 with 10–15 dB(A) signal-to-noise;[^5] IEC 60268-16:2020 supplies the measurement method.[^6] The reverberant, cross-broadcast terminal is the one that fails these numbers.

Three propositions carry the case. Design-time acoustic treatment runs roughly two to four times below retrofit at commercial-benchmark midpoints.[^7] The enforcement vehicle already exists: the Design Manual binds consultants and contractors today, and extending it into tenant audio is the design objective, contingent on verification of the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest provisions.[^8] And the window is narrow. Debt-service coverage compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× through 2029 as ~$5.5 billion of new debt phases in,[^9] which is why the amendment must be scoped to work still in programming, not programs already in design. The Board question is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## The Loud Terminal Is the Unintelligible One

Start not at an airport but in a hospital intensive-care unit. Trade-press summaries of a Johns Hopkins ICU observation put alarm rates at 771 per bed per day[^10] — a number that recurs across the alarm-fatigue literature the Joint Commission cites as its evidentiary base. Clinicians had stopped hearing the alarms. The alarm that mattered sounded like the alarm that did not. The Joint Commission's response was not to reduce alarms in the name of patient comfort. It was National Patient Safety Goal NPSG.06.01.01, phased in 2014 and 2016: every accredited hospital had to inventory its alarms, rank them, govern them, and prove it.[^11] Indiscriminate broadcast was itself the safety failure.

Now picture — as an operating pattern, not a dated observation — the IAD international connecting bank in the late-afternoon peak. A gate change pages overhead in Concourse C. A TSA advisory. Two boarding calls at adjacent gates. A courtesy page. A concessions employee paging a colleague. On paper, operations serving the passenger. In a reverberant holdroom shared with two adjacent gates, it is the alarm no one hears. IAD moved 29.01 million passengers in 2025, 10.53 million of them international — the fastest 2025 growth among the fifty largest U.S. airports.[^12] DCA moved 24.89 million, down 5.4% after the January collision, softened government-related travel, and the 43-day federal shutdown.[^13] Every one of those passengers expected the announcement that mattered to reach them. Whether the buildings were specified to let it is the open question.

MWAA is about to specify, from the studs up, an enormous run of new terminal space. The July 29 announcement covers more than 5 million square feet: Concourses C and D replaced, AeroTrain extended, mobile lounges retired, Saarinen's Main Terminal preserved.[^14] It mentions concourses, baggage systems, and CBP facilities. It does not mention a single acoustic or public-address specification. The $20 billion-plus figure is provisional; the committed AUL figures are firm — roughly $6.99 billion at IAD and $2.39 billion at DCA, the latter including the eventual replacement of the Terminal 1 main concourse.[^15] The anchor tenant has noticed the price. Cranky Flier described United CEO Scott Kirby as visibly reluctant and cited an airline briefing pegging fully loaded IAD transformation cost per enplanement at $90.64, against $12.88 today.[^16] That reluctance strengthens the case for a Design Manual amendment. A standard that lives in the Manual survives program shrinkage, re-scoping, or a change of administration. A case built on program durability does not.

The obvious objection first. After NTSB AIR-26/02, no board member wants a policy paraphrased as "airport cuts safety announcements."[^3] That instinct is correct — and it is the argument. The silent-airport literature equates quietness with subtraction. The evidence supports the inverse. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 sets an intelligibility floor for emergency voice systems, scoped to Acoustically Distinguishable Spaces, because a terminal is not one acoustic environment.[^4] You fail that floor by being loud, reverberant, and cross-broadcast: precisely the condition an unspecified terminal defaults to. High ambient noise pushes STI down the qualitative bands of IEC 60268-16.[^6] The louder the terminal, the less intelligible the emergency announcement. Every acoustic and PA-zoning requirement in the recommended standard flows from that inversion.

The peer set usually cited is, on close reading, a category error. London City has run a silent policy since 2008.[^17] Helsinki-Vantaa (since 2015) and Copenhagen moved information to screens and apps.[^18] All three are small, single-terminal, point-to-point operations — quieter to begin with. Schiphol is the real comparison: a large intercontinental transfer hub whose reduced-announcement regime rests on FIDS density and a passenger base that expects screens plus app.[^18] None of the four has published measured intelligibility, missed-boarding, or channel-redundancy data. They are policy analogues, not design precedents. MWAA does not need to import their brand. It needs to out-engineer them.

SFO is the closest North American precedent, and it shows both the model and the failure mode. SFO reports its Quiet Airport program removed roughly 90 minutes of daily announcement time, cut individual paging from 492 to 261 occurrences per day, and reduced onsite noise by more than 40%.[^19] Every figure is SFO's own. The same record holds documented passenger complaints and missed-flight anecdotes.[^20] Borrow the operating model: zoned delivery, discontinued cross-terminal final calls, gate-area retention. Quote the outcome numbers as SFO reports them, not as facts. Announcement reduction without intelligibility engineering and channel redundancy is subtraction, not design.

The recommendation must pass one test above all: the people who most need to hear. 28 CFR 35.160 obligates MWAA to ensure effective communication with people with disabilities.[^21] The 2010 ADA Standards §219 and §706 require assistive-listening systems in each assembly area where audible communication is integral to use.[^22] Under 14 CFR Part 382's December 2016 kiosk amendment, airline-installed kiosks at airports with 10,000-plus enplanements must meet accessibility design specifications, with the rule establishing a floor that at least 25% of installed machines in each location be accessible.[^23] ACRP Report 239 treats digital-first substitution as partial, not sufficient, for travelers with disabilities and older adults.[^26] A regime that reduces broadcast without specifying redundant visual, digital, staffed, and hearing-loop channels compounds exclusion. A standard that mandates those redundancies binds MWAA harder than current practice, not softer. One schedule note: the Saarinen Main Terminal was determined National Register-eligible in 1978, and Section 106 consultation with the Virginia SHPO and ACHP runs six to eighteen months, longer with a Memorandum of Agreement[^25] — reason to keep the amendment's first applications outside the eligible-district envelope.

Governance is the technology. The newer automated announcement platforms can be specified in Design Manual terms: centralized management, replay and audit logs of every broadcast, elimination of manual-initiation errors, templated multi-language delivery.[^27] This is the Joint Commission move applied to airports — inventory, rank, govern, log — run by a standing Announcement Governance Committee (co-chaired Engineering/Operations/Communications/IT, rotating air-carrier member) reporting annually to the Board's Business Administration Committee. Enforcement matters as much as the platform: annual acoustic inspections keyed to lease renewal, 30/60/90-day cure notices, lease-management escalation only for pattern noncompliance. Without those mechanics, airlines and concessionaires read the standard as paper.

Which raises the tenant question. At preferential-use gates, the podium PA is effectively airline-owned. United operates about 50 jetbridge gates and moves about 67% of IAD traffic; American holds roughly 53% of DCA market share.[^28] A standard that cannot reach tenant audio governs half the soundscape. Nobody is claiming today's Design Manual reaches the podium microphone. The argument is that the amendment, once verified against the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest provisions, should be written to reach it.[^8] That verification runs on a critical-path airline-consultation lane: confidential station-manager briefings in weeks one through three, formal AAAC briefing, a thirty-day comment window, and a written MWAA position on cost-recovery categorization by week twelve.

The financial case rests on rework avoidance, not new capital. Commercial pricing guides put new-construction acoustic ceiling treatment at $5–$15 per square foot and retrofit panel treatment at $18–$45. Those are non-airport, medium-confidence benchmarks, but they point the same direction: design-time treatment runs roughly two to four times below retrofit at the midpoints.[^7] An order-of-magnitude range for the full stack — ceiling premium, FIDS redundancy, mobile notification platform — of $12–$40 million sits between 0.17% and 0.57% of the committed IAD program.[^7] The window is narrow and directional: debt-service coverage compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× through 2029 as some $5.5 billion of new debt phases in.[^9] Added specification cost inside programs already in design cuts against coverage, which is why the amendment must be scoped to work still in programming, not to programs in design or procurement. That distinction is the difference between the easy Board sell and the wrong one.

The standard writes itself as a two-tier document. Mandatory at both airports: an Acoustically Distinguishable Space map as a design deliverable; intelligibility floors per NFPA 72 Chapter 24 and ACRP 175; measurement per IEC 60268-16:2020, commissioned under representative traffic with a cure period rather than a certificate-of-occupancy gate;[^29] accessibility floors per 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, and Part 382; hearing conservation per 29 CFR 1910.95;[^30] audit-logged announcement governance; and concessionaire televisions and gate-lounge audio — the loudest ambient contributors most standards miss — kept inside scope: no ambient television audio in holdrooms except captioned displays, concessionaire audio held within an authority-defined ambient limit (specific threshold to be established in the Design Manual amendment). Then the paragraph most silent-airport writing omits: an IROPS override. During declared irregular operations, the Airport Duty Manager holds named authority to exceed announcement-density templates; every deviation is logged; a post-event reconciliation goes to the Governance Committee. A standard that does not survive the first Wednesday afternoon of gate holds will not be believed for the Wednesday it was written for. Tiered by context: PA zoning depth, sensory-quiet zone scope (ANSI/ASA S12.60's 35 dB(A) benchmark),[^31] tenant-audio enforcement mechanics, and the physical toolkit for a Saarinen-eligible head-house versus a new-build midfield concourse versus a Pelli-era DCA terminal.

Exercise it first where friction is lowest. Subject to project phase — which the Council did not verify as part of this research pass — the DCA Terminal 1 concourse replacement is the natural choice: smaller, less politicized, less encumbered by the July 2026 political stack around Dulles. If Terminal 1 has passed scope definition, the reference application shifts to the earliest IAD C/D element still in programming. Either way, Concourse E — opening this fall with fourteen new United gates[^32] — carries the first commissioned STI and ambient baseline under representative traffic. That baseline becomes the number every future MWAA facility is measured against.

The strongest counter-case is redundancy: NFPA 72, ADA §219, and ACRP 175 already set the floors that matter, so a new MWAA standard mostly regulates the discretionary space above them. Half-right. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 binds *emergency* voice; it does not require ADS mapping as a general design deliverable, does not govern routine PA, does not compel governance of manual-initiation error. ADA §219 sets receiver counts, not intelligibility floors for daily announcements. ACRP 175 is guidance, not code. The amendment's marginal content — the ADS map at each design milestone, representative-traffic commissioning, audit-logged governance, channel-redundancy floors, the IROPS override, media limits, a tenant-audio boundary tied to the AUL — is exactly what the existing floors do not require. The contrarian's alternative, a mandatory 30%-design communications review with no new thresholds, belongs inside the amendment as the enforcement checkpoint, not as a substitute. Review without a specification is a review of nothing. The premature-specification objection is more serious: rigid overlays written before program-brief maturity drive change orders, and STI gates on the critical path in this coverage window would be a bond-rating conversation. Hence verification-with-cure-period commissioning, airline-recovery exposure excluded, and the ADS map as a milestone deliverable rather than a TCO condition.

Return to the ICU. In 2013 the Joint Commission did not tell hospitals to reduce alarms. It told them to prove they knew which ones mattered, that those would be heard, and that the machinery for governing the rest was on the record. Ten years later the ICUs are not silent. They are audible in the right places, at the right times, to the right people. That is what a communications-integrity standard buys an airport. The Board question is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## Airport Decision Card

**Owner and instrument.** MWAA President and CEO sponsors. Vice President for Engineering drafts and issues the amendment under the delegated standards-setting authority exercised for annual Design Manual revisions. The Board receives it as an information item at the next Business Administration Committee (target: November 2026 cycle; confirm when the meeting notice publishes). A formal resolution is initiated only if AAAC consultation surfaces material airline objection or the 2025 AUL cost-center analysis requires MII treatment.

**External framing convention.** Internal engineering name: "quiet-by-design." External policy name in all Board papers, press releases, DOT correspondence, and airline briefings: "MWAA Communications-Integrity Standard." MWAA Communications drafts and enforces the convention.

**Airline-consultation lane (parallel to drafting, on the critical path).**
- Weeks 1–3: confidential briefings to United and American executive station managers and capital counsels.
- Week 4: formal AAAC briefing at the next scheduled meeting.
- Weeks 4–8: thirty-day comment window on the draft amendment language.
- Week 10: written MWAA response letter cataloging airline concerns.
- Week 12: written MWAA position on cost-recovery categorization keyed to specific 2025 AUL cost centers (Terminal Rentals, Landing Fees, Airline Support Areas, etc.).
- No amendment language enters any scope document until the Week 12 position letter is on the record.

**First 180 days, branched.**
- **Days 1–30 — verification.** (a) Current acoustic, PA-zoning, STI, and ambient-noise content of the Design Manual and Specifications; (b) DCA Terminal 1 project phase; (c) 2025 AUL MII and consultation text as it applies to acoustic/PA/tenant-audio content; (d) tenant-technology-standards reach; (e) NFPA 72 edition in force at each AHJ (Loudoun/Fairfax Fire Marshals for IAD, DC Fire Marshal for DCA); (f) Section 106 posture with Virginia SHPO and ACHP for any Saarinen-adjacent scope; (g) FAA Part 139 emergency-communications compliance baseline.
- **Days 30–60 — drafting.** Mandatory-tier language keyed to NFPA 72 Ch. 24, ACRP 175, IEC 60268-16:2020 (representative-traffic measurement, verification-with-cure-period), 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, 14 CFR Part 382, 29 CFR 1910.95. Acoustic engineering via task order under an existing on-call A/E IDIQ if available; new Brooks-Act QBS if not.
- **Days 60–90 — stakeholder.** Airline-consultation lane above; Fire Marshal briefings at each AHJ; DOT Office of Public Affairs coordination given the July 29 co-brand; ACI-NA and A4A briefings.
- **Days 90–120 — branched application.** **Branch A** (Terminal 1 in scope-definition): draft DCA Terminal 1 pilot application language. **Branch B** (Terminal 1 past scope-definition): qualify the earliest eligible IAD C/D element still in programming. Under either branch: instrument the Concourse E baseline STI and ambient measurement.
- **Days 120–180 — adoption and baseline.** Board information item; publish the amendment in the annual Design Manual revision; commission the first ADS map and baseline STI/ambient reading at Concourse E under representative traffic within 90 days of opening.

**Procurement path (per line item).**
| Line item | Vehicle | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Acoustic engineering support | Task order under existing on-call A/E IDIQ if available; new Brooks-Act QBS if not | Multi-month if new solicitation; identify vehicle in Week 1 |
| Concourse E baseline measurement | Task order under existing commissioning contract if scope permits | Otherwise an add to the acoustic-engineering task order |
| Mobile notification platform | MWAA IT governance path; evaluate against any active enterprise-notification contract | Cyber review required; potential AAAC briefing depending on cost recovery |
| FIDS redundancy | Rides inside identified existing capital program element | Confirm program element in Week 1 |
| Governance-platform license | Standard IT procurement | Include cyber review |

**Cost and funding source.**
- Capital stack $12M–$40M one-time,[^7] allocated roughly 60/40 IAD/DCA proportional to committed program footprint. Capital-vs-operating split refined during scoping.
- Airline recoverability characterized in the Week 12 position letter under the 2025 AUL cost-center matrix. Directional expectation, subject to that letter: ceiling treatment premium recoverable inside Terminal Rentals; mobile notification platform non-recoverable MWAA IT; commissioning verification recoverable inside program financing.
- Funding candidates: program contingency inside the committed AUL work; PFC amendment for the mobile notification platform; General Airport Revenue Bonds only if a stand-alone capital element requires it.
- Five-year operating tail: platform license, audit-log staffing, and commissioning verification, ~$0.8M–$2.5M/year across both airports.
- FTE: 0.5–1.0 across Engineering, Operations, and IT at each airport. Confirm whether new duties fall inside existing position descriptions or require reclassification and bargaining review.

**Ownership after adoption.** Engineering: standard content and revision. Operations at each airport: commissioning acceptance under representative traffic and IROPS override authority (Airport Duty Manager). IT: platform and audit-log stewardship. Communications: external framing convention. Announcement Governance Committee: audit-log review, template control, IROPS post-event reconciliation, annual public report to the Business Administration Committee.

**Tenant-audio enforcement architecture.** Annual acoustic inspection keyed to lease renewal or Tenant Technology Standards audit; violation notice with 30/60/90-day cure; escalation to lease-management action only for documented pattern noncompliance. Framework drafted before the AUL text is closed so it informs, rather than lags, the AUL consultation.

**Dependencies.** 2025 AUL MII and cost-recovery text (step 1c); tenant technology standards apparatus (1d); Section 106 consultation with Virginia SHPO and ACHP on any Saarinen-adjacent application, 6–18 months, longer with MOA (1f); Fire Marshal AHJ concurrence at each airport (1e); FAA Part 139 emergency-communications compliance (1g);[^24] represented-workforce bargaining posture on any new duties; DOT Office of Public Affairs coordination given the July 29 co-brand.

**Leading indicators.**
1. Amendment language published in the next Design Manual annual revision and cited by section in the first eligible major terminal-package scope documents.
2. Commissioned STI and ambient readings at Concourse E under representative traffic within 90 days of opening, benchmarked against ACRP 175 and NFPA 72.
3. Missed-boarding, service-recovery, and ADA-effective-communication complaint rates at amendment-governed facilities, benchmarked against non-amendment facilities.

**Failure mode / stop conditions.**
- If the current Design Manual already contains equivalent or stronger acoustic and PA-zoning content, the recommendation contracts to a governance-and-verification amendment.
- If the 2025 AUL MII text materially constrains tenant-audio enforcement, the tenant tier drops from mandatory to recommended pending renegotiation.
- If DCA Terminal 1 is past scope-definition, the reference application shifts to the earliest eligible IAD C/D element still in programming.
- If the Concourse E baseline shows existing MWAA design already meets ACRP 175 and NFPA 72 under representative traffic, mandatory application contracts to governance and IROPS behavior; the acoustic performance section becomes recommended pending measured cause.
- If the first commissioning cure-period cost exceeds an MWAA-set per-project threshold (indicative $500,000), Engineering holds further application pending amendment revision.

**What would change this recommendation.**
- A measured MWAA STI/ambient baseline showing existing spaces already meet ACRP 175 and NFPA 72 targets under representative traffic.
- MWAA-published PA telemetry showing announcement density already below the peer-airport reduced-practice band.
- Publication of a peer-airport primary measurement methodology (e.g., SFO publishing measured intelligibility data), permitting direct-copy operating patterns rather than fresh MWAA engineering.
- An AAAC outcome in which airlines commit to a shorter-form standard as a negotiation concession, reducing the amendment's marginal content.
- FAA publication of Part 139 amendments touching PA and communications, moving requirements outside MWAA's discretion.
