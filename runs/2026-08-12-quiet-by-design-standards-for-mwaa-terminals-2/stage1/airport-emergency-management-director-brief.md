# Quiet-by-Design at MWAA — Read from the EOC Floor

**Agent:** Airport Emergency Management Director (research chair)
**Run:** `quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2`
**Position:** Director of Emergency Management, top-tier US hub airport, 25 years in seat; prior tenure at another large hub. Speaks from the chair EM directors actually sit in, not the one described in the AEP binder.

I am reading this thesis from the EOC watch desk, not from the design charrette. My question is not whether the terminals get quieter on a Tuesday afternoon. It is whether the quiet standard MWAA writes into the Design Manual holds up on the worst day — the day the CRJ hits the water on final for 33, the day a Georgia Power switchgear fire kills primary and backup at the same time, the day a CrowdStrike-shaped push notification kills every FIDS in every concourse at 04:11 UTC, or the day someone with a rifle steps out of a jetway at Concourse E. Quiet done well strengthens the EOC. Quiet done as a passenger-comfort motif erodes it. That is the entire brief.

## 1. Key findings

- **A quiet-by-design standard written from the surge case backward strengthens EM capability; one written from the normal-day passenger-experience case erodes it.** The variable that matters is not average ambient dBA. It is whether the emergency communications system (ECS) hits its intelligibility threshold when the terminal is loaded, the concessions are running, the crowd is moving, and the operator on the mic is under stress. NFPA 72 sets the design target — 0.70 STI or 0.45 CIS at every occupant location in each acoustically distinguishable space (ADS) [Source: https://www.commercialintegrator.com/security/mass_notification/mass-notification-intelligibility/]. Any MWAA "quiet" standard that does not name that number and require field verification of it is not a standard, it is an aspiration.
- **The regulatory floor is Part 139.325 and AC 150/5200-31C, and both are written in the language of notification, not silence.** 14 CFR 139.325(e) obligates the certificate holder to have procedures for notifying the responsible facilities, agencies, and personnel of an accident's location, victim count, and information necessary to carry out their responsibilities as soon as it is known [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.325]. AC 150/5200-31C institutionalizes NIMS/ICS and organizes the AEP around the hazard-specific functions that decide what actually gets said on the PA when the plan is triggered [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/150_5200_31c_chg1.pdf]. The quiet standard sits above these two documents, not around them.
- **The domestic reference document for terminal PA design already exists, and it is not a passenger-experience document.** ACRP Research Report 175 is the standing US guidance on public-address intelligibility in airport passenger terminals — the design intent, the ambient measurement approach, the finish selection, and the announcement discipline [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24839/improving-intelligibility-of-airport-terminal-public-address-systems]. Any MWAA quiet-by-design volume that does not explicitly bind to ACRP 175 for the PA and ADS chapters is inventing content NIMS-literate reviewers already have in a bookshelf.
- **The activation matrix is not the call tree.** In every EOC I have run, the difference between a contained event and a catastrophic one is how many of the right people are at the table in the first thirty minutes. That is a person-to-person, phone-in-your-hand relationship — not a position line on an org chart. A quiet-by-design standard that assumes the EOC will get "clean" information over a PA network misreads the actual failure mode. What matters is whether the ECS reaches the fifteen zones that matter — hold rooms, sterile corridors, baggage make-up, AeroTrain platforms, curb, TSA queues — with a voice a fatigued 65-year-old with hearing loss can parse. That is a design decision, made once, at 30% design, or it becomes a change-order fight.
- **Every recent activation the field remembers points the same direction: redundant channels win, single-mode assumptions lose.** FLL 2017 is the case study on what happens when PA zoning cannot deliver a coordinated, authoritative message during an active-threat event: passengers self-evacuate onto active aprons, ground stops follow, and law enforcement self-deploys into a coordination problem [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. ATL 2017 is the case study on what happens when the redundant power system that was supposed to keep PA and FIDS alive shares a room with the primary [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2017/12/17/us/atlanta-airport-power-outage/index.html]. CrowdStrike 2024 is the case study on what happens when the "visual-first" assumption fails everywhere on Earth at the same time [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption]. Quiet-by-design has to survive all three.
- **A quiet standard that pushes information out to smartphones is a standard that has silently transferred emergency communication to a channel MWAA does not own, cannot commission, and does not test.** The passenger who needs the announcement most — infrequent traveler, sensory-processing difference, low English proficiency, low battery, no data plan, unlocked airside terminal — is the passenger least reached by a push notification and most reached by an intelligible voice from a ceiling speaker they can locate. IAD's 36%+ international passenger share makes that not a rounding error [Source: airport context §6].
- **Continuity of operations is where the "quiet" conversation becomes an EM conversation.** Announcement volume goes up during IROPS, weather events, and the hours after an incident. MCO closing at 10:30 Wednesday for Ian and holding closure until sustained winds dropped below 40 kt is a coordinated shutdown message across a passenger population that had to be told, in every terminal, over the ECS, more than once [Source: https://blogmickey.com/2022/09/orlando-international-airport-announces-closure-ahead-of-hurricane-ian/]. Standards written for a quiet Tuesday must not degrade the terminal's ability to carry a loud Wednesday.
- **DCA is not a hypothetical anymore.** The January 29, 2025 PSA 5342 / UH-60 midair killed 67 and drew more than 500 personnel into the response — with family reunification and first-responder trauma care as EM-owned functions the airport had to execute in real time [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/29/us/dca-midair-collision-rescue-heroes]. FAA CertAlert 24-09 (Oct 2024) had already told certificated operators to review AEPs against sabotage, hijack, unlawful interference, and coordination with FBI/TSA/law enforcement [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/airport_safety/certalerts/part_139_certalert_24_09]. Any MWAA quiet-by-design standard adopted post-2025 is adopted into a terminal that has now used its family-assistance and mass-notification capacity in earnest. It will be read against that memory.

## 2. Evidence — sources

- **NFPA 72 intelligibility requirement for emergency communications systems.** Industry secondary summaries of NFPA 72 Chapter 24 give the operative numbers: 0.70 STI as the design target and 0.45 CIS ("Fair" on the Common Intelligibility Scale) as the minimum at every occupant location within each Acoustically Distinguishable Space [Source: https://www.commercialintegrator.com/security/mass_notification/mass-notification-intelligibility/]; NFPA 72 (2010 and later editions) frames the ADS and voice-messaging chapters that a designer must indicate on drawings [Source: https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/359395/Midches_Aug2016/Docs/NFPAFireAlarmCode.pdf]. NFPA 72 itself is a paywalled standard; the primary reference is the current edition. For a quiet-by-design standard, MWAA's own citation should be the current NFPA 72 chapter, not a secondary summary.
- **14 CFR 139.325 Airport Emergency Plan.** Codifies the certificated operator's AEP obligations, including 139.325(e) notification procedures — the "who gets told what, how fast" requirement that governs everything the PA is used for in an activation [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.325].
- **AC 150/5200-31C Airport Emergency Plan.** FAA guidance that ties the AEP to NIMS/ICS and organizes it into the hazard-specific functions the EOC actually runs from [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/150_5200_31c_chg1.pdf].
- **FAA Part 139 CertAlert 24-09 (Oct 15, 2024).** Directs certificated operators to review AEPs against sabotage, hijack, and unlawful-interference scenarios and to coordinate with FBI, TSA, and law enforcement — the exact scenarios in which unified, zoned PA is not optional [Source: https://www.faa.gov/airports/airport_safety/certalerts/part_139_certalert_24_09].
- **ACRP Research Report 175 — Improving Intelligibility of Airport Terminal Public Address Systems.** The domestic reference for PA intelligibility in terminals; provides design guidance for existing and new terminals, background-noise data, finish selection, and system-design principles [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24839/improving-intelligibility-of-airport-terminal-public-address-systems].
- **ACRP Synthesis 115 — Practices in Airport Emergency Plans.** Compiles AEP practices with case studies from CLT, IAH, FLL, LAS, SEA, and two smaller airports [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/26077/practices-in-airport-emergency-plans]. Relevant because FLL is one of the case studies and its 2017 AAR is the practical case against under-zoned PA in an active-threat event.
- **ACRP Report 93 — Operational and Business Continuity Planning for Prolonged Airport Disruptions.** The continuity-of-operations counterpart researchers frequently pair with the AEP body of work [Source: https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/acrp/docs/acrp03-18_fr.pdf].
- **FLL Active Shooter AAR (Broward County Aviation Department, 2017).** Documents the uncontrolled self-evacuation onto active aprons, the FAA ground-stop response, and law-enforcement self-deployment following the January 6, 2017 event [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]; press summary of BCAD-identified corrective actions including communications-systems improvements and EOC relocation [Source: https://airportimprovement.com/article/fort-lauderdale-int-l-shares-lessons-learned-2017-active-shooter-tragedy/].
- **ATL 2017 power outage.** 11-hour outage from an underground switchgear fire; the redundant backup was in the same fire zone as the primary and failed with it; ~30,000 travelers affected; more than 1,000 flights canceled that day; 80–100 aircraft parked on taxiways [Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hartsfield-jackson-atlanta-international-airport-outage-power-back-on-after-chaos-grounded-delta-flights/] [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2017/12/17/us/atlanta-airport-power-outage/index.html].
- **CrowdStrike July 2024 Delta disruption.** Faulty vendor push crashed Windows endpoints globally; Delta canceled more than 7,000 flights over the following days, disrupting 1.3M+ passengers; airports resorted to hand-written boarding passes and manual passenger information [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption].
- **MCO Hurricane Ian closure (Sep 28, 2022).** Commercial operations ceased at 10:30 Wednesday; EOC activated; nonessential employees released; airport explicitly declined shelter role; reopening tied to sustained winds under 40 mph [Source: https://blogmickey.com/2022/09/orlando-international-airport-announces-closure-ahead-of-hurricane-ian/].
- **DCA midair collision (Jan 29, 2025).** 67 fatalities; more than 500 personnel involved in the response; family reunification and first-responder trauma care treated as EM-owned functions; NTSB (Feb 2026) attributed the collision to overlapping systemic failures across FAA/ATC and military operations [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/29/us/dca-midair-collision-rescue-heroes] [Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/01/27/nx-s1-5689091/ntsb-dca-midair-collision-black-hawk-helicopter].
- **SFO Quiet Airport program.** Reported reductions of ~90 minutes/day of announcement time and ~40% at gate-seating areas; individual paging reduced from 492 to 261 occurrences (47%) and from 145 to 58 minutes cumulative (60%) [Source: https://airport.nridigital.com/air_dec20/san_francisco_international_airport_noise]. The measurement methodology behind the reduction figures is not disclosed in primary MWAA-usable form; researchers should treat these as reported program outputs, not audited compliance data.

## 3. The preparedness case — for and against the thesis

**For the thesis (why quiet-by-design strengthens EM):**

The single hardest fight in the EOC during an activation is being heard. Not being right, not being fast — being heard. If MWAA writes a Design Manual volume that names STI/CIS thresholds by ADS, requires field verification at commissioning, prescribes PA zoning granularity down to the hold-room and the sterile-corridor loop, forbids concession audio from spilling into egress paths above a stated level, and mandates ECS voice priority above all tenant systems, the EOC gains a capability it currently has to negotiate for on a per-project basis. This is not a passenger-experience story. It is a life-safety story that happens to leave the terminal quieter on a normal day as a byproduct. FLL 2017 is the strongest argument for it: the corrective action set that came out of the Broward AAR was, in effect, a quiet-by-design retrofit — coordinated messaging, PA zoning upgrades, multi-channel notification [Source: https://airportimprovement.com/article/fort-lauderdale-int-l-shares-lessons-learned-2017-active-shooter-tragedy/]. Writing it into MWAA's manual now means concourses replaced in the IAD $20B program open with the standard already in the walls, not chased through change orders.

**Against the thesis (why quiet-by-design can erode EM):**

There are three ways this goes wrong.

First, if "quiet" is defined against announcement volume rather than intelligibility, it will produce a terminal that is quieter on Tuesday and less understandable on the day of the event. Speech intelligibility is not the inverse of ambient dBA. It is a function of signal-to-noise ratio, reverberation time, PA coverage geometry, and message length. The right target — STI ≥ 0.70 by NFPA 72 for the ECS overlay [Source: https://www.commercialintegrator.com/security/mass_notification/mass-notification-intelligibility/] — can coexist with lower ambient noise only if the standard says so explicitly.

Second, if the standard leans on visual and mobile-push channels as substitutes for voice, it has effectively outsourced part of the ECS to devices MWAA does not commission. CrowdStrike proved that "all screens dark, at once, everywhere, no warning" is not a black-swan hypothetical [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption]. ATL 2017 proved that when the primary and backup are physically coupled, both die together [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2017/12/17/us/atlanta-airport-power-outage/index.html]. Any quiet standard that credits FIDS and app push toward emergency reach without segregated power, network, and vendor stacks is over-crediting them.

Third, if the standard is not written into the seven-volume MWAA Design Manual and its Specifications, it will not bind consultants, contractors, or tenants. The Design Manual is the enforcement vehicle. A policy memo is not [Source: airport context §7].

**Exercises and activations that most directly bear on this decision:**

- Any active-threat full-scale exercise MWAA runs at IAD or DCA — the intelligibility of the ECS in hold rooms, on the AeroTrain, and in the sterile corridor during simulated evacuation is the empirical test of the standard.
- The triennial Part 139 emergency exercise, which is the compliance instrument for the AEP itself under 139.325 [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.325].
- The DCA response following January 29, 2025 — a live activation still being metabolized inside MWAA. The lessons from family reunification, media handling, and terminal-side notification are the freshest, and are the most credible internal argument for the standard [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/29/us/dca-midair-collision-rescue-heroes].

## 4. Anchor events and program details for the Strategist

1. **FLL Jan 6, 2017 active shooter and the Broward AAR.** The definitive US case against under-zoned terminal PA in an active-threat event; the corrective action package effectively prefigures a quiet-by-design ECS specification [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf].
2. **ATL Dec 17, 2017 power outage.** 11 hours, ~30,000 travelers, backup failed with primary; the standing US case for physical separation of primary and backup power feeding ECS, PA, and FIDS [Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hartsfield-jackson-atlanta-international-airport-outage-power-back-on-after-chaos-grounded-delta-flights/].
3. **CrowdStrike July 19, 2024.** The standing case that "visual-first, app-first" cannot be credited as ECS reach; Delta's 7,000-flight, 1.3M-passenger cascade is the operational cost of the assumption failing [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Delta_Air_Lines_disruption].
4. **DCA Jan 29, 2025 PSA 5342 / UH-60 midair.** The freshest MWAA-specific activation; 67 fatalities; 500+ personnel; family reunification executed on-site. Any standard adopted now will be read against this event [Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/29/us/dca-midair-collision-rescue-heroes].
5. **ACRP Report 175 and Synthesis 115 as the reference stack.** Report 175 sets the terminal PA-intelligibility floor MWAA should bind to; Synthesis 115 provides the peer-airport AEP practice base, including FLL, that a defensible MWAA standard cites explicitly [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24839/improving-intelligibility-of-airport-terminal-public-address-systems] [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/26077/practices-in-airport-emergency-plans].

**The EM verdict for the Strategist.** Quiet-by-design at MWAA is a net positive for EM capability, but only if the standard is written from the surge case backward, names STI/CIS thresholds at the ECS level, prescribes ADS-level zoning and field verification at commissioning, keeps the ECS ownership with the airport rather than the airline or tenant, and forbids any credit for visual/mobile channels as substitutes for voice reach. Written that way, it converts a passenger-experience initiative into an emergency-preparedness upgrade the EOC has needed for a decade. Written as ambient-dBA reduction alone, it erodes the capability that keeps a bad day from becoming a catastrophic one.
