# Virtual-Christian Brief — Quiet-by-Design at MWAA

**Author lens:** the operator on the council. ADD-fueled dot connector. Written first from gut, then checked against the evidence I pulled. Where my instinct got contradicted, I say so.

---

## 1. The reframe

**Every announcement you don't need is deleting one you do. Quiet-by-design isn't an acoustics problem — it's alarm fatigue with a boarding pass.**

That's the sentence. Steal it.

The dominant framing in this file — and the one the other agents will chase — is "quiet as environmental quality." Ambient decibels, reverberation time, STI thresholds, directional loudspeaker specs. All real. All necessary. All missing the point.

The reason SFO's 90-minute daily reduction was worth doing wasn't that the terminal got calmer. It was that when the announcement finally mattered — the gate change, the missing minor, the shelter-in-place — a passenger's ears were available to hear it. Airports currently run their PA the way an ICU runs its monitors: as if every signal were equally urgent. Hospitals figured out fifteen years ago that this gets people killed. The Joint Commission made clinical alarm management a National Patient Safety Goal in 2014 (NPSG.06.01.01) because a Johns Hopkins ICU study found an average of **771 alarms per bed per day** and clinicians had stopped hearing them. The alarm that mattered sounded like the alarm that didn't. Airports are on the same curve. Every "Passenger Johnson, please meet your party at the information desk" is training the passenger — and the gate agent — to ignore the next announcement. Quiet-by-design is the airport's Joint Commission moment.

Reframe the standard as **signal architecture with an acoustics chapter**, not the other way around, and the rest of the argument gets a lot easier. The mandatory-versus-context-dependent question the run poses answers itself: what is mandatory is the discipline about *what gets to be a signal at all*. What varies by terminal is *how you deliver the signals you kept*.

## 2. Five dots to connect

### a. Hospital alarm fatigue is the template — steal it wholesale.
The Joint Commission's NPSG.06.01.01 has two phases: first, make alarm management a governance priority; second, establish policies about which alarms fire, at what threshold, with what escalation, under what override. That's exactly the structure MWAA needs. "Announcement governance" is not a soft airport-experience concept — it's an accredited hospital's day-one obligation. Frame the MWAA standard as a two-tier governance requirement (Volume-level mandate + airport-specific implementation plan) and you have a defensible precedent, not a design fad. And unlike an airport, hospitals don't get to say "well, we announce because if we don't, passengers miss surgery." They still cut alarms.

### b. Retail and grocery already ran this experiment. Airports missed the memo.
Walk into a Kroger or a Whole Foods in 2026. You will not hear an overhead PA page for the deli lead. You'll hear one crackle if there's a spill on aisle 7 and a "closing in fifteen minutes" at 9:45. Everything else moved to earpieces, tablets, and app notifications. Retail didn't do this to be quiet — they did it because overhead paging measurably slowed the store and annoyed the customer. Nobody had to run a pilot. Nobody wrote a design manual. The tech got cheap and the operating model changed.

The airport is late because we have a captive audience, an FAA-adjacent safety narrative, and airline-side operators (gate agents) whose incentives are calibrated to a 2005 CX model where a missed boarding costs the airline more than the noise costs the terminal. The airport authority owns the room; the airline owns the microphone. That's the actual governance problem. Your standard doesn't work unless it binds tenants — which means it has to live in the Airline Use and Lease Agreement handshake, not just the Design Manual.

### c. The stadium industry solved intelligibility twenty years ago. We're arguing about it in 2026.
NFL and college stadiums spec their PA systems for two very hard tests at once: intelligible emergency messaging under 100+ dB crowd noise, and sustained non-emergency announcement quality across the seating bowl. STI targets in the 0.50–0.60 range with commissioning verification. ACRP Report 175's guidance for airports — SNR of 10–15 dB, minimum STI of 0.45 in pier-style departure lounges — is *less* demanding than what an SEC football stadium contract routinely requires. If you're writing an MWAA standard, don't invent it. Copy the stadium industry's commissioning language, ratchet the STI target up to 0.50 for gate-hold and Federal Inspection Service areas, and require post-occupancy verification at 90 days and one year. Stadium ops has been doing exactly that for two decades. Nobody's going to sue you for being late-follower on a proven acoustic standard.

### d. Tornado sirens and the "boy who cried wolf" collapse of trust.
The National Weather Service spent a decade re-engineering severe-weather alerting because false-alarm fatigue was documented to reduce shelter-taking behavior after tornado warnings. They moved to impact-based warnings, tiered language ("catastrophic" vs. "considerable"), and geographic tightening of polygons. The lesson for airports is not "warn less." It's **warn with tiers**. An MWAA standard that treats every automated announcement as the same signal class is guaranteed to erode passenger response over time. Build the tier — routine, operational, safety-critical, emergency — into the specification, with distinct audio identity per tier and enforced silence between tiers. That's an information-design decision, not an audio-engineering one.

### e. The DCA midair collision changed the risk tolerance for silence.
This is the one I want the strategist to feel in the gut. After January 29, 2025 — 67 dead, the NTSB final report calling out FAA, ATC, and Army failures — no MWAA executive is going to sign a "reduce announcements" policy that reads like it downgrades safety communication. That's political reality. So the standard cannot be positioned as *less* communication. It has to be positioned as **provably better emergency and irregular-operations communication**, with the ambient reduction as the mechanism, not the goal. The correct headline sentence for the MWAA board is: *"We are adopting a standard that makes the announcement people need to hear 40% more likely to be understood."* Every acoustic and PA-zoning requirement flows from that promise. Reverse it — lead with "quiet" — and the standard is dead in the boardroom by page three.

## 3. The operator's gut check (bar version)

If you handed me this at the bar, here's what I'd actually say:

> **"We don't have a noise problem. We have three problems stacked on top of each other and we call them noise because we can't tell them apart. One: our PA is 30 years old, half the terminal is a Saarinen-era shed with a five-second reverb, and the STI is garbage before we open the mic. Two: the airlines announce because their gate agents get scored on missed boardings, so they announce for cover. And three: every airline club, every Hudson News, every Chili's Too has six TVs on ESPN at whatever volume the manager set that morning, and nobody's design manual has ever touched a tenant TV. Fix those three and the terminal is quiet. Don't fix them and it doesn't matter what you spec."**

A few things that follow from that:

- **Don't lead with the acoustic tier.** Lead with the tenant-audio tier. The single biggest reduction in ambient noise at either IAD or DCA is achievable through a tenant standard that caps club and concession audio, requires closed-captioned muted TVs by default, and gives the airport authority commissioning-level enforcement. You will pick a fight with the airlines. Pick it anyway.
- **The gate-agent behavior problem is real and it is not solved by policy.** It is solved by giving the gate agent a better tool — a directional beamforming panel that reaches only the gate hold, plus mobile-notification integration through the airline app for the last-call population. If the airline gate podium has a better microphone than the terminal PA, of course the announcement wins.
- **IAD and DCA cannot share the same acoustic prescription.** They can share the same *governance*, the same *tier structure*, the same *commissioning discipline*, and the same *tenant-audio limits*. The physical spec has to vary because the Saarinen Main Terminal at IAD is a listed historic acoustic space (National Register, 1978) that will actively fight any absorptive treatment, and DCA's replacement Terminal 1 gets a design-phase specification without the historic overlay. Mandate the governance. Vary the physics.
- **The commissioning line is where these standards live or die.** Everyone agrees at 30% design. Nobody funds the post-occupancy STI verification at 90 days. Bake that verification into the standard as a payable milestone, or the standard is theater.
- **Where my gut fought the evidence:** I initially wanted to argue that SFO's 40% announcement reduction was mostly PR. It's not — the airport-technology.com and View From The Wing coverage both trace it to a specific PA-zoning implementation (A-gate announcements no longer bleed into B). That is a real, replicable design decision, not a branding claim. I updated. It should be a peer benchmark, not a cautionary tale.

## 4. Productive tangents

Sections I chased down. Some hit, some didn't.

- **Saarinen's Main Terminal as an acoustic argument nobody in this run is making** (hit). The 1962 terminal was designed as a *pilgrimage space* for the jet age — cathedral-scale, suspended-roof, deliberately reverberant. It is a listed National Register property and the run's excluded architectural historian would have made this point. The strategist should not miss it: any quiet standard applied to IAD has to answer, on page one, how it survives contact with a building that was designed to sound like a cathedral. The answer is probably localized directional audio and lower ambient targets in the head-house, not blanket absorption. This is a tangent that turned out to be central.

- **OSHA hearing conservation as the sleeper compliance driver** (hit). 29 CFR 1910.95 obligates the employer for occupational noise exposure. Gate agents, ramp coordinators, and TSA officers accumulate exposure at levels most airport authorities do not measure. A quiet-by-design standard that reduces ambient by 6–8 dB has an occupational-health defense that lands with the CFO and the general counsel more cleanly than any passenger-experience argument. I do not have MWAA-specific dosimetry, so this is a hypothesis to test, not a claim. But it belongs in the standard's cost-benefit paragraph.

- **The Copenhagen Metro parallel** (partial hit). I wanted to draw the driverless-Metro-in-Copenhagen line to the "silent airport" Copenhagen airport line and argue the whole city runs quieter by system design. The Metro parallel is real but weaker than I hoped — the Metro is silent because it's automated, not because Danes prefer silence. I'm leaving the tangent in only as flavor for the strategist. Don't build on it.

- **Casinos as the anti-airport** (left in, though it doesn't fully carry). Casinos engineer disorientation with ambient noise — bells, near-misses, no clocks. Airports do the same thing accidentally. I couldn't find primary casino-design literature that maps cleanly to airport specification language, so this is a rhetorical flourish, not a load-bearing analogy. Use it for the executive summary opening if you want a hook; don't use it in the requirements section.

- **The Vegas Sphere's beamforming audio system** (left out). Meyer Sound and L-Acoustics have both deployed venue-scale directional beamforming. The tech is proven at cathedral scale. But dropping "the Sphere" into an MWAA board memo will read as gimmicky, and the technical case is already covered by the stadium-industry precedent above. Cut it before the strategist quotes it.

---

**Bottom line for the strategist:** the reframe is the deliverable. Quiet-by-design at MWAA is a signal-architecture standard governed like a Joint Commission alarm-management program, delivered through the Design Manual for the physical spec and through the Airline Use and Lease Agreement for the tenant-audio spec, with commissioning verification at 90 days and one year as a payable milestone. Lead the board narrative with the emergency-communication upgrade, not the ambient-noise reduction. The IAD-DCA split falls out naturally: shared governance, tiered signal architecture, and commissioning discipline are mandatory; physical acoustic prescriptions vary because a Saarinen cathedral and a DCA replacement concourse are not the same building.
