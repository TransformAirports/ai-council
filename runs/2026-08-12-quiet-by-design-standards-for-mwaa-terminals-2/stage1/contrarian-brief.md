# Contrarian Brief — Quiet-by-Design Standards at MWAA

**Run:** `quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2`
**Author role:** Contrarian
**Position:** Against the thesis, on its actual terms

## Posture

The thesis says MWAA should set a common quiet-by-design standard across IAD and DCA and lock the requirements in before designs and procurements harden. I am going to argue that this is the wrong intervention at the wrong time, measured with the wrong instruments, imported from the wrong peer set, and priced against the wrong airline agreement window. The version that would actually be adopted by MWAA's Office of Engineering — a new Design Manual volume with STI floors, LEQ ceilings, PA zoning diagrams, and quiet-zone minimums — is materially worse than the do-less alternative I lay out at the end. Everyone already agrees on the shape of good practice: intelligibility over volume, localized over global paging, visuals and staffing primary, audio secondary. The disagreement is whether a bi-airport mandatory standard is the right vehicle. It is not.

## 1. The strongest counterargument, in bullets

- **The mandatory floor already exists — twice.** NFPA 72 already binds emergency mass-notification STI at 0.45 minimum with 0.50 average per acoustically distinguishable space; ADA §219 already requires assistive listening in every assembly space where a PA is provided; ACRP 175 already publishes an application-standard STI floor of 0.45–0.50 for airport passenger spaces. MWAA does not need a new standard to make its terminals meet the intelligibility bar that matters most. It needs to enforce the ones already binding it. A new standard layered on top will primarily regulate the discretionary space above the floor — passenger comfort — where the case for uniform prescription is weakest.
- **The instrumentation the standard would use does not work in the environment being measured.** IEC 60268-16:2020, the international standard for STI, expressly does not cover fluctuating noise. Airport terminals are fluctuating-noise environments by definition — rolling-bag pulses, gate rushes, jet-bridge activations, unpredictable crowd swells. Any MWAA compliance threshold expressed as a single STI or LEQ number will be gamed by consultants running STIPA sweeps at empty-terminal 3:00 a.m. hours, which is precisely how existing airport commissioning already produces STI numbers untethered to what passengers actually hear.
- **The named peer set does not transfer.** London City is a single-terminal, business-heavy, single-runway operation with a captive frequent-traveler base; Helsinki is a small-hub with near-universal smartphone penetration and a single-carrier anchor; Schiphol runs a genuinely dense FIDS-plus-app model against a passenger base that expects it. IAD is a 29-million-passenger, 36%+ international, connection-dominant Star Alliance hub built around a Saarinen preserved terminal, an aging concourse system being replaced during the standards window, and a passenger cohort that is disproportionately non-English, infrequent, and connecting under time pressure. Copying the peers' surface artifacts (screens-only, no paging) into that geometry produces the opposite of the peers' outcome: more missed connections, more staff labor at gates, more airline recovery cost.
- **The exemplar figure the movement is built on has no published methodology.** The number that recurs in every quiet-airport deck — SFO removed "over 90 minutes of daily announcements" and achieved "a reduction of more than 40 percent in overall onsite noise" — appears exclusively in secondary reporting and SFO's own communications. No measurement protocol, no baseline dB record, no independent audit is publicly available. A Council that grounds a bi-airport standard on that figure is grounding it on a press release.
- **The commissioning burden lands in the tightest coverage window MWAA will see this decade.** MWAA plans roughly $5.5B in new debt 2025-2028, with adjusted debt per O&D enplanement peaking near $400 in 2028 and net revenue coverage compressing from 1.63x to about 1.3x. A quiet-by-design standard adds a critical-path commissioning gate — STI verification, ambient-sound baselining, PA-zone spillover surveys, ALS coverage checks — to every substantially renovated space at IAD and DCA. Commissioning findings that block occupancy at IAD Concourse C/D replacement or DCA Terminal 1 replacement translate directly into airline recovery disputes under the 2025 Use and Lease Agreement. Adding another failure mode to those two schedules is not free.
- **Airline behavior under the new lease will neutralize the standard in the direction that matters.** Airline club audio, gate-podium PA, boarding-call scripts, and airline mobile-app push notifications drive most of the noise a passenger actually experiences at a hub gate. Those systems are governed by tenant agreements, airline standard operating procedures, and airline preservation-of-brand equity clauses — not the MWAA Design Manual. A "common standard" that binds MWAA-owned base building and leaves the actual sources of noise inside the tenant envelope produces the worst of both outcomes: cost imposed on the authority, noise unchanged for the passenger.
- **A standard specified before the design is not always cheaper.** The economics literature on airport capital delivery does not support the axiom that "earlier specificity is cheaper." Design-specification errors and consultant omissions are one of the top drivers of change orders on airport programs; a rigid quiet-by-design overlay written before program-brief maturity produces exactly the kind of downstream change-order exposure the thesis is trying to prevent. The right analogue is not LEED at project start; it is prescriptive commissioning specifications written before construction documents and then argued line-by-line during CMAR or CMc buyout.
- **The genuinely useful intervention is smaller.** A one-page mandatory "Communications-Environment Design Review" checkpoint owned by the Office of Engineering — invoked at 30% design on every terminal package — would deliver most of the benefit the standard is chasing, without importing the enforcement burden, the airline dispute exposure, the commissioning risk, or the false-precision metric regime. The Council should recommend the review, not the standard.

## 2. Evidence

The intelligibility floor and the assistive-listening obligation are already binding, not aspirational. NFPA 72 requires that "at least 90 percent of the measurement locations within each ADS have a measured Speech Transmission Index of not less than 0.45 (0.65 CIS) and an average STI of not less than 0.50 STI (0.70 CIS)" for mass-notification systems [Source: https://www.suppressionsystems.com/voice-intelligibility-in-mass-notification-systems/]. ACRP Research Report 175 — the domestic reference the run manifest already names — reports that "codes and application standards typically recommend a minimum STI of 0.45 or 0.50" and calls out a target "signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 10 to 15 dB" for announcements [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/5]. ADA §219 requires that "in each assembly area where audible communication is integral to the use of the space, an assistive listening system shall be provided," with receivers per Table 219.3 and 25% minimum hearing-aid compatible [Source: https://centerforhearingaccess.org/ada-standards/]. Any new MWAA standard sits on top of an already-mandatory floor.

The measurement problem is admitted inside the standards themselves. IEC 60268-16:2020, the STI standard MWAA would necessarily reference, explicitly notes that "the document does not cover the case of fluctuating noise on the STI, although some general comments on dealing with this complex issue are provided" [Source: https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26771]. The same summary observes that "when measuring in real-world environments, impulsive noise during the measurement (e.g., door slams, dropped objects) will corrupt the result." Airport terminals are a fluctuating-plus-impulsive-noise environment; that is not the corner case, it is the operating condition.

The exemplar figure is not primary-sourced. The claim that SFO's quiet-airport program produced "90 fewer minutes of announcements per day" and "a reduction of more than 40 percent in overall onsite noise" appears in trade press, SFO communications, and passenger-experience blogs, but the underlying methodology is not published; the industry aggregation piece that most Council members would encounter says the program "removed over 90 minutes of unnecessary daily noise and achieved a reduction of more than 40 percent in overall onsite noise" without producing baseline, receiver location, or LEQ integration windows [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/san-francisco-airport-removed-90-minutes-of-daily-noise-travelers-say-it-changed-everything/]. The airport context packet itself flags that "the measurement methodology behind the reported reduction was not verified in primary sources."

The peer transfer case is weak. London City's own policy statement is that "no tannoy announcements are to be made throughout the departure lounge" with reliance on screens, and the policy is anchored in "extensive research carried out at the London airport" — an airport of ~5.9M passengers with a single terminal and no connecting-hub geometry [Source: https://www.holidayextras.com/airport-parking/london-city/on-airport/silent-terminal.html]. LCY's own noise apparatus is oriented at aircraft emissions and community boundary compliance, not terminal-interior passenger noise [Source: https://www.londoncityairport.com/corporate/environment/noise-management-and-monitoring/noise-action-plan]. The extrapolation to a 29M-passenger, hub-and-spoke, 36%+ international IAD is not supported by any primary-source study.

The counter-effect on passengers is documented. Coverage of the SFO program itself notes that "passengers who choose to spend time shopping in duty-free stores or dining at airport restaurants are at an increased risk of missing their flights" under a quiet regime and that "for many passengers, especially those unfamiliar with the local airport or language, the absence of announcements can be a significant source of stress" [Source: https://simpleflying.com/airport-quiet-policy-frustrating-passengers-sfo/]. That population — infrequent, non-English, older — is disproportionately IAD's. ACRP Report 239 assesses airport programs for travelers with disabilities and older adults and treats the digital-first substitution channel as a partial, not sufficient, response [Source: https://opendoorsnfp.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/ACRP-Reseach-Repor-239.pdf].

The regulatory and safety stack constrains the standard's flexibility on the downside. Under 14 CFR Part 139.319 and .325, each certificated airport must maintain an effective emergency communications system and airport emergency plan with communication procedures adequate to guide implementation [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139]. Any quiet-by-design standard that reduces PA capability without preserving a documented emergency-communication reversion mode is not FAA-defensible. This is a floor MWAA cannot design under.

The financial context tightens the argument further. The airport context packet documents "approximately $5.5 billion of new debt between 2025 and 2028" and "net revenue debt service coverage is expected to fall from 1.63x in 2024 to roughly 1.3x over the next five years" [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md §3]. A new standard whose principal effect is to add commissioning gates and airline-recovery exposure to Concourse C/D replacement and DCA Terminal 1 replacement is not neutral.

## 3. Scenarios where the thesis is wrong

**Scenario A — Standard adopted, tenants unbound.** MWAA adopts a bi-airport quiet standard binding base-building acoustics, PA zoning, and FIDS density. Airlines and concessionaires retain autonomy over gate-podium PA, club audio, boarding scripts, and mobile-app push. Measured cabin-side passenger-noise experience does not change. MWAA has bought regulatory friction and airline recovery disputes for no passenger-experience delta. Failure mode: the standard binds the wrong actor.

**Scenario B — Standard adopted, IAD Concourse C/D commissioning slips.** STI verification, ambient-sound baselining, and ALS coverage checks are added to the critical path for the largest IAD program element in a generation. The commissioning agent flags STI non-compliance in the central Aeroquay area (predictable — Saarinen-adjacent finishes are reverberant, crowd noise is impulsive, and IEC 60268-16 doesn't validly cover the case). The finding blocks TCO. Airline recovery on delayed opening triggers a dispute under the 2025 Use and Lease Agreement. The standard produces the exact delivery pathology it was supposed to prevent.

**Scenario C — Standard adopted, emergency reversion untested.** DCA's post-January-2025-collision operating tempo relies on frequent live PA during irregular ops and helicopter-route flag events. A "quiet-first" default PA volume calibration reduces intelligibility during a genuine mass-notification event because the reversion protocol was validated in commissioning but not in a live exercise with a full concourse of passengers under distraction. NTSB or FAA post-incident review identifies the standard as a contributing factor. The standard becomes a liability document.

**Scenario D — Standard adopted, digital-substitution assumption fails on the population that matters.** IAD's international arrivals cohort — many first-time visitors, many without US-carrier apps installed, many without US mobile data — cannot use the app channel the standard treats as the redundant primary. Gate agents absorb the labor. United's DOT-mishandled-connection metric rises. Airline demands relief from PA restrictions in the Use and Lease. The standard is reopened within 24 months of adoption, at cost to the authority's credibility.

**Scenario E — Standard adopted, DCA and IAD diverge in enforcement.** DCA leadership treats the standard as a design manual chapter and enforces it project-by-project; IAD leadership treats it as an operational policy and enforces it inconsistently against tenants. The "common MWAA standard" produces two operating regimes that airlines have to reconcile against a single lease. The complaint file grows.

## 4. Data points a strategist would have to address or concede

1. **NFPA 72 already sets the emergency-communication STI floor.** "At least 90 percent of the measurement locations within each ADS have a measured Speech Transmission Index of not less than 0.45 (0.65 CIS) and an average STI of not less than 0.50 STI (0.70 CIS)" [Source: https://www.suppressionsystems.com/voice-intelligibility-in-mass-notification-systems/]. A new MWAA "mandatory" STI number in that range is redundant with the code that already binds.
2. **The STI measurement standard admits it does not cover the airport case.** IEC 60268-16:2020: "The document does not cover the case of fluctuating noise on the STI" [Source: https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26771]. Any strategist proposing a bi-airport STI threshold has to defend the measurement method against the standard's own scoping exclusion.
3. **The SFO exemplar figure has no published methodology.** "Removed over 90 minutes of unnecessary daily noise and achieved a reduction of more than 40 percent in overall onsite noise" [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/san-francisco-airport-removed-90-minutes-of-daily-noise-travelers-say-it-changed-everything/]. The context packet itself notes the methodology was not verified. This is the load-bearing precedent; it does not bear the load.
4. **The passenger downside is documented in the peer coverage itself.** Passengers under quiet regimes are "at an increased risk of missing their flights"; "for many passengers, especially those unfamiliar with the local airport or language, the absence of announcements can be a significant source of stress" [Source: https://simpleflying.com/airport-quiet-policy-frustrating-passengers-sfo/]. This cohort is disproportionately IAD's.
5. **The financial window is the wrong one.** From the airport context packet: "$5.5 billion of new debt between 2025 and 2028"; coverage "expected to fall from 1.63x in 2024 to roughly 1.3x over the next five years" [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md §3]. Adding a commissioning-and-airline-recovery vector to the tightest coverage window is a bond-rating conversation, not just an operational one.

## The concession that would make the thesis defensible

The version of this thesis that survives contact with MWAA's actual governance, finance, and airline geometry is much smaller than the one on the table:

> MWAA should add a single mandatory "Communications-Environment Design Review" gate at 30% design to every substantially renovated terminal package, owned by the Office of Engineering, referencing the intelligibility floor already binding under NFPA 72 and ADA §219 and the accessibility population profile in ACRP 239, and producing a project-specific commissioning specification for STI, ambient-sound, PA zoning, FIDS density, quiet-zone provision, and tenant-audio boundary. No new bi-airport thresholds. No new Design Manual volume. No binding number that has to be argued during buyout or commissioned inside a method the international standard admits does not cover the case.

That version delivers the governance intervention the thesis is trying to achieve without importing the standardization pathology or committing MWAA to defend a false-precision number in the tightest coverage window of the capital cycle. It is the version the Council should argue for. The version on the table is the version I have to argue against.
