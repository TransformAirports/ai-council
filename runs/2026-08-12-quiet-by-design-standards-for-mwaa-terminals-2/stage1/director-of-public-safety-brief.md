# Director of Public Safety — Stage 1 Brief

**Run:** `quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2`
**Voice:** Director of Public Safety, top-tier US hub airport (police, ARFF, PSAP under one command)
**Independent artifact.** No Stage 1 peer briefs consulted.

## 1. Key findings

1. **"Quiet" is not a public safety question until it is.** Ninety-nine days out of a hundred, a lower ambient in a departures hall reduces cognitive load and helps my dispatchers and officers hear their radios. On day one hundred it is the day a passenger falls off a jet bridge, a bag with wires is left at a checkpoint, or someone reports gunfire at Baggage Claim 4. That is when zoned paging, intelligibility floors, and the operational scripts around them stop being an experience question and become a life-safety question. A quiet-by-design standard that does not price in day one hundred is not a standard — it is a marketing document.
2. **Voice intelligibility is already a code requirement, and it is one the industry chronically fails to meet.** NFPA 72, Chapter 24 requires that emergency communication systems (ECS) deliver a Speech Transmission Index (STI) of at least 0.45 at 90 percent of measurement points within each acoustically distinguishable space, with an average of 0.50 [Source: https://www.commercialintegrator.com/security/mass_notification/mass-notification-intelligibility/]. ACRP Research Report 175, the domestic reference for airport PA, recommends STI ≥ 0.45 and a 10–15 dB signal-to-noise ratio in departure lounges [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/5]. Most large-hub terminals we would benchmark against do not measurably meet those numbers today. A quiet-by-design standard for MWAA is, in operational reality, the first time a large US authority would commission its PA system to the codes it is already supposed to meet.
3. **Silence and safety announcements are not in tension if the standard is written correctly.** SFO's Quiet Airport program removed roughly 90 minutes of daily announcement time and reduced onsite noise by more than 40 percent, but *retained safety and security messaging as an explicit carveout* and pushed the reduction through zoning and script discipline, not by disabling channels [Source: https://www.airport-technology.com/features/san-francisco-quiet-airport/]. The design question for MWAA is not "how many messages" but "which zones, which triggers, which speakers, which floors — and who is authorized to override." Any standard that does not name the override authority is unfit for use.
4. **The dominant failure mode in a live incident is not under-communication — it is un-coordinated self-evacuation.** The 2013 LAX Terminal 3 shooting killed TSA Officer Gerardo Hernandez; roughly 4,500 passengers self-evacuated across Terminals 1, 2, and 3, and 20,000 more sheltered in place, disrupting 1,500 flights [Source: https://www.lawa.org/media/16740]. The 2017 FLL shooting killed five in Baggage Claim; 90 minutes later, an unsubstantiated "shots fired" radio call in Terminal 1 and the Hibiscus Garage produced a self-evacuation that injured roughly forty additional people, some with broken bones [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. Newark 2019 and JFK 2016 followed the same script — a loud noise, an unverified radio call, and a stampede. A "quiet" terminal that lacks a zoned voice-command channel with high signal-to-noise ratio during an incident is a terminal that concedes the information environment to rumor and cell phones.
5. **Federal alerting has moved. A "quiet by design" standard has to move with it.** The FCC now requires wireless carriers to deliver location-based routing for wireless 911 calls within 30 seconds of call origination [Source: https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-399578A1.pdf]. Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA) were used operationally at LAX during the 2016 false-active-shooter event — the first documented deployment of a city WEA on airport property during a live evolution [Source: https://www.lawa.org/news-releases/2016/news-release-63]. Any MWAA standard should require compatibility with WEA geo-fence issuance, the MWAA/regional PSAP call flow, and the FIDS/digital sign network as *coordinated channels* — not three unrelated systems the dispatcher hopes to reach one at a time.
6. **ADA and Section 504 make redundant visual channels a requirement, not a courtesy.** ADA effective-communication rules and ADAAG §4.28 require visual alerting equivalent to audible alarms; airport terminals are called out specifically for visual synchronization of announcements including gate changes, delays, and emergencies [Source: https://www.ada.gov/resources/effective-communication/]. A quiet-by-design standard that shifts more information onto visual channels is *not a compliance risk* — it is a compliance opportunity, so long as the visual channels are engineered for latency, redundancy, and legibility. The compliance risk is the opposite: shifting the load without upgrading the visual estate to the reliability standard we today only apply to audio.
7. **The MWAA operating environment amplifies both the case for the standard and the cost of getting it wrong.** MWAA Police enforce authority regulations, the county codes of Arlington, Fairfax, and Loudoun, and the laws of the Commonwealth of Virginia across terminals, roadways, the Dulles Toll Road, and the Dulles Access Highway [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/policefireems/mwaa-police-department]. The MWAA Fire and EMS Department stands at approximately 163 personnel — 16 chief officers, 27 fire officers, 21 master firefighters, 90 firefighters, 9 civilian — and answers mutual aid across Arlington, Alexandria, Loudoun, Fairfax, and DC [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Washington_Airports_Authority_Fire_and_Rescue_Department]. That footprint is not sized to run a compliance program for two separately-designed audio estates that do not talk to each other. The standardization argument, from my chair, is primarily a resilience and interoperability argument.
8. **The right execution vehicle is the MWAA Design Manual, not a standalone policy.** MWAA already binds consultants and tenants through a mandatory seven-volume Design Manual updated annually [context packet §7]. A "Quiet-by-Design Volume" or annex — with mandatory STI floors, PA zoning topology, voice-alarm intelligibility commissioning, tenant audio limits, and quiet-zone acoustic performance — is the vehicle that survives design reviews, procurement, and change orders. A resolution or standalone policy will get argued as a change order on every project.

## 2. Evidence

### Codes and regulations that already govern the acoustic estate

NFPA 72 Chapter 24 sets the intelligibility standard for emergency communication systems: STI ≥ 0.45 at 90 percent of measurement points inside each acoustically distinguishable space, with an average STI ≥ 0.50 across the space [Source: https://www.commercialintegrator.com/security/mass_notification/mass-notification-intelligibility/]. The same chapter requires that ECS be capable of reproducing prerecorded, synthesized, or live messages in compliance with the voice-intelligibility provisions [Source: https://prod-edam.honeywell.com/content/dam/honeywell-edam/hbt/en-us/documents/literature-and-specs/whitepapers/notifier-us/hon-ba-fire-nf-wp-massnotification-m-wp-mn-05-10.pdf?download=false].

International Building Code §907.5.2.1.1 requires that audible notification appliances produce 15 dBA above the average ambient level, or 5 dBA above any maximum lasting 60 seconds or longer, whichever is greater; §907.5.2.2 requires emergency voice/alarm communication systems (EVAC) for assembly occupancies exceeding 300 occupants — the entire passenger side of every MWAA terminal [Source: https://engineersuniverse.com/studios/fire-alarm/fire-alarm-codes-by-building-type]. NFPA 415 provides the airport-specific overlay for terminal construction, smoke control, and egress [Source: https://www.nfpa.org/product/nfpa-415-standard-on-airport-terminal-buildings-fueling-ramp-drainage-and-loading-walkways/p0415code].

ACRP Research Report 175, the domestic airport reference, recommends STI ≥ 0.45 and SNR of 10–15 dB in departure lounges [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/5]. Report 175 also documents that many existing large distributed sound systems in airports are in poor condition for intelligibility purposes and require significant calibration before measurements can even be trusted [Source: https://www.suppressionsystems.com/voice-intelligibility-in-mass-notification-systems/]. Read that finding twice: the industry standard is honored in the breach.

### The ARFF and law-enforcement backdrop that any terminal standard has to respect

14 CFR §139.319 requires that at least one ARFF vehicle reach the midpoint of the farthest runway serving air carriers, or a point of comparable distance on the movement area, within three minutes of the alarm and begin agent application; other required apparatus must reach that point within four minutes [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.319]. That number is not a terminal number, but it constrains where crews, dispatch, and stations sit — and every second of ambiguity in an alarm channel is a second an ARFF crew is not rolling. Alarm intelligibility, radio interoperability, and dispatch console latency are the *terminal-side* levers on that response time.

TSA 49 CFR Part 1542 requires airport operators to maintain a security program including law enforcement personnel adequate to support screening operations and incident response [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-XII/subchapter-C/part-1542]. The precise LEO response-time floor is contained in the airport's approved and SSI-protected ASP, and cannot be quoted from public sources — but the industry norm is measured in minutes to a checkpoint, and the terminal's alarm and paging architecture is a *precondition* for making those numbers.

MWAA Police enforce MWAA regulations, Arlington/Fairfax/Loudoun county codes, and the laws of Virginia across a footprint that includes the terminals and roughly all of the DTR and DIAAH [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/policefireems/mwaa-police-department]. The MWAA Fire and Rescue Department was constituted from the FAA's ARFF branch at the authority's stand-up in 1987 and today runs approximately 163 personnel serving both IAD and DCA with mutual aid across the region [Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Washington_Airports_Authority_Fire_and_Rescue_Department]. Any standard MWAA promulgates has to be operable by exactly this force — not a hypothetical one.

### Peer and case study evidence

**SFO Quiet Airport (2018–2020):** publicly reported to have removed more than 90 minutes of daily announcement time and reduced onsite noise by roughly 40 percent through zoned paging, script shortening, and message triage — with automated announcements banned *except* those tied to safety and security [Source: https://www.airport-technology.com/features/san-francisco-quiet-airport/]. Underlying measurement methodology is not publicly documented in primary sources and would need to be verified before quotation as an engineering benchmark rather than an operating precedent.

**LAX (November 1, 2013):** Paul Ciancia entered Terminal 3 at the departure level and killed TSA Officer Gerardo Hernandez. Approximately 4,500 passengers self-evacuated across Terminals 1, 2, and 3, roughly 20,000 sheltered in place, and 1,500 flights and 171,000 passengers were disrupted [Source: https://www.lawa.org/media/16740]. The 2013 after-action review specifically flagged a communication gap with passengers as a driver of the chaotic evacuation, and LAX's subsequent WEA deployment during the 2016 false-active-shooter event was the direct programmatic response [Source: https://www.lawa.org/news-releases/2016/news-release-63].

**FLL (January 6, 2017):** the primary event killed five in the Baggage Claim area of Terminal 2 at approximately 12:54 PM. At approximately 2:22 PM — 90 minutes later — radio traffic carried unsubstantiated reports of shots fired in Terminal 1 and the Hibiscus Garage, and a chaotic self-evacuation followed. About forty people were injured in the stampede, some with broken bones [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. The after-action review also flagged that the Emergency Operations Center itself was locked down during the second event, isolating command from the response [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf].

**Newark (September 2, 2019) and JFK (August 14, 2016):** both terminals suffered stampede-scale self-evacuations from a single unverified event — a loud noise mistaken for gunfire, a triggered alarm without coordinated messaging — with passengers spilling onto tarmacs and past checkpoints [Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/airport-false-alarms-expose-danger-panicked-evacuations-experts-say-n639456]. Research cited in the same coverage notes that crowd density above four people per square meter is where movement efficiency collapses and turbulence begins [Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/airport-false-alarms-expose-danger-panicked-evacuations-experts-say-n639456].

### The PSAP and NG911 stack

The FCC has adopted rules requiring wireless carriers to route 911 calls using device-based location, delivered to PSAPs within 30 seconds of call origination [Source: https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-399578A1.pdf]. NG911 requires PSAPs to receive 911 traffic in IP-based SIP format with location conveyed via PIDF-LO or equivalent [Source: https://101voice.com/emergency-services/next-generation-911-ng911/]. The practical question this creates for MWAA — whether the passenger calling 911 from a DCA jet bridge lands in the MWAA dispatch center, an Arlington PSAP, or a wireless carrier's default routing — is not resolved by acoustic policy. It is a governance question the quiet-by-design standard should force onto the same page as PA zoning and mass-notification.

### Accessibility

The ADA's effective-communication requirements mandate that people with hearing, vision, or speech disabilities receive information equivalent to that provided to others [Source: https://www.ada.gov/resources/effective-communication/]. ADAAG §4.28 provides the technical criteria for visual alarms — including spacing in long corridors typical of airport terminals — precisely because audible alarms are not adequate as a stand-alone channel [Source: https://www.corada.com/documents/adaag-manual/4-28]. Terminals are called out in accessibility guidance as environments where visual replication of gate changes, boarding, and emergency announcements is expected [Source: https://accessibe.com/glossary/visual-alerting-device]. A quiet-by-design shift that reallocates communication load toward visual and mobile channels is affirmatively supported by ADA principles — provided the visual infrastructure is specified, redundant, and commissioned to the same reliability the audio system was.

## 3. The public safety case — for and against the thesis

**For the standard, from my chair.** A common quiet-by-design volume in the Design Manual is the fastest path to end the fiction that MWAA's PA systems already meet the STI targets code says they must. It forces every future terminal package at IAD and DCA — Concourse C/D replacement, DCA Terminal 1, every follow-on tier — to be commissioned to a defensible intelligibility number. It gives my Fire Marshal and my Chief of Police a written engineering basis for tenant enforcement (airline clubs, concessions, gate podium equipment) that today rests on goodwill. It gives dispatch a zoned, override-capable channel architecture instead of a legacy paging system that broadcasts everything to everyone. And it makes visual, digital, and staffed channels first-class citizens rather than compensating controls that appear at the end of the project when the audio budget is exhausted.

Standardization also gives me interoperability. My dispatchers, my ARFF crews, and my patrol officers work both airports. Two different notification architectures, two different override protocols, and two different tenant enforcement postures cost me minutes and clarity in an incident. One standard, with airport-specific supplements for terminal geometry and passenger mix, is how I get a common operating picture between IAD and DCA.

**Against the standard, from my chair.** Two hazards. The first is that the standard is written by acousticians and experience designers and adopted by a board that hears "quiet" as a passenger amenity. If the mandatory tier reduces PA coverage, or lowers speaker density, or restricts override authority to reduce spillover between zones, the standard has moved a life-safety lever for a comfort gain. Any tier that touches speaker layout, coverage, redundancy, override, or fire-alarm integration should require the AHJ (my Fire Marshal) and Public Safety sign-off, not a courtesy review.

The second is that "quiet-by-design" cannot become the vehicle by which the industry's default failure mode — under-intelligible PA systems that no one has ever measured — is quietly codified. If a mandatory tier sets an STI floor of 0.45 in normal operations but a commissioning agent is not appointed, or post-occupancy verification is not funded, we will have written our own violation. NFPA 72 already requires 0.45. The value the standard adds is not the number; it is the *proof* that we hit the number, on day one and again on year three.

## 4. Specific incident references, certification facts, and operational realities a strategist can use

1. **Fort Lauderdale, 2017.** Primary event 12:54 PM; secondary self-evacuation from unsubstantiated shots-fired radio call at 2:22 PM; ~40 additional injuries in the stampede; EOC itself locked down and cut off from the response [Source: https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ft.-Lauderdale-Hollywood-International-Airport-AAR.pdf]. The lesson for a "quiet" standard is that ambient acoustics matter far less than *the zoned voice-command channel a commander uses to shut down a rumor within 90 seconds.*
2. **LAX, 2013.** TSA Officer Gerardo Hernandez killed in Terminal 3; ~4,500 self-evacuated, ~20,000 sheltered in place, ~1,500 flights and ~171,000 passengers disrupted [Source: https://www.lawa.org/media/16740]. Communication-gap-with-passengers was the after-action's primary finding; LAX's WEA deployment during the 2016 false alarm was the direct fix [Source: https://www.lawa.org/news-releases/2016/news-release-63].
3. **NFPA 72 STI 0.45 / 0.50.** The intelligibility floor for emergency communication systems is not a nice-to-have — it is code [Source: https://www.commercialintegrator.com/security/mass_notification/mass-notification-intelligibility/]. Most large-hub PA systems in the US have never been measured to it. Any MWAA quiet-by-design volume that does not require STI commissioning by an independent acoustician and post-occupancy re-verification is a standard in name only.
4. **14 CFR §139.319 — 3 minutes to runway midpoint.** The ARFF response clock starts at the alarm [Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-139/subpart-D/section-139.319]. Terminal-side alarm architecture, dispatch console latency, and radio interoperability are inputs to that time, which is why "terminal PA" is an ARFF issue and not just a comfort issue.
5. **FCC location-based 911 routing — 30-second delivery.** As of the 2023–2024 rules, wireless carriers must deliver device-based location to the PSAP within 30 seconds of call origination [Source: https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-399578A1.pdf]. Any MWAA standard that shifts information load onto passenger mobile devices and mobile notifications has to answer *which PSAP owns the terminal-jet-bridge 911 call* — and that answer is a design-manual question, not a communications-plan afterthought.
