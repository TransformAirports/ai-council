# Airport CEO Brief — Quiet-by-Design Standards for MWAA Terminals

**Author lens:** Chief executive officer, top-tier US hub airport, 20 years in airport leadership, 8 in this seat.
**Run:** `quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2`
**Date:** 2026-08-10
**Audience:** MWAA Board of Directors chair and vice chair, through the Strategist.

---

## 1. Key findings — for the chair to absorb in one read

1. **This is a standards-governance decision, not an acoustics decision.** The thesis lives or dies inside the MWAA Design Manual and the 2025 Use and Lease Agreement — the two instruments that bind consultants, contractors, and airlines before a single ceiling tile is specified. Frame it as an amendment to the Design Manual (which MWAA itself describes as having "the force of law" on the property) and it is a straightforward board vote; frame it as a policy overlay and it becomes an airline-negotiated line item at every gate handoff for the next 14 years [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/business/airports-authority-design-manual].

2. **Ratings-neutral to ratings-positive if written correctly.** MWAA is heading into the tightest coverage window of this capital cycle — Fitch, Moody's, and S&P all sit at the AA−/Aa3 band with stable outlooks, and net debt service coverage is projected to compress from 1.63x in 2024 toward roughly 1.3x as ~$5.5B of new debt phases in. A standard that measurably reduces late-stage rework in a $22.5B+ IAD program and a DCA Terminal 1 replacement is a rating agency story, not a rating agency risk [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/financial-statistics/investor-relations/credit-ratings]. The rating downside is not the acoustic spec; it is the political spectacle of a poorly-managed rollout during rating-agency surveillance season.

3. **Majority-in-Interest is the real veto point, not the board.** The 2025 AUL is a hybrid compensatory agreement running through 2039. Standards that lift design or commissioning cost above thresholds recoverable through airline rates will trigger consultation and, depending on MII language not verified in this packet, a carrier vote. United (IAD) and American (DCA) are the two counterparties that matter. Any standard that materially affects airline club spaces, gate podium audio, or airline-controlled FIDS overlays must be negotiated before it enters the Design Manual, not after [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/news/metropolitan-washington-airports-authority-completes-negotiations-new-use-and-lease-agreement].

4. **The federal stack is unusually loud on this file.** MWAA operates under a federal lease from the U.S. DOT. The July 2026 IAD announcement was fronted by the President and the Transportation Secretary — federal actors have already priced themselves into the IAD story. Meanwhile the PFC cap remains frozen at $4.50 (unchanged since 2000) and AIP is authorized only through FY2028, which forces every incremental design requirement to be paid from bonded debt, airline rates, or non-airline revenue rather than a federal fund with slack in it [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791].

5. **Post-collision, DCA is not a normal terminal.** The January 2025 midair collision, the resulting NTSB report, and the FAA's route and runway restrictions have permanently elevated the DCA public-safety and emergency-communication file. Any quiet-by-design standard at DCA will be read first through the lens of "does this degrade our ability to communicate during an activation?" A standard that cannot answer that question in one sentence, tied to a measurable Speech Transmission Index (STI) threshold and a documented commissioning regime, does not survive first contact with the DCA fire chief or the FAA regional office.

6. **The board composition rewards multi-year, low-controversy standards.** MWAA's 17-member board is appointed 7-4-3-3 by Virginia, DC, Maryland, and the President, with six-year terms. That structure makes any single-jurisdiction political win fragile and any *institutional* standards package durable. This is exactly the kind of file a chair with a one-year officer term (Uncapher, 2026) can move without spending capital he needs for the capital program [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airport-business/news/55332705/metropolitan-washington-airports-authority-board-elects-2026-officers].

7. **The precedent to cite is SFO, not London City.** Peer benchmarking should lead with a US carrier-heavy hub — SFO's Quiet Airport program removed roughly 90 minutes of daily announcement time and cut gate-area announcements by about 40% starting in 2018 and expanding in 2020 [Source: https://www.internationalairportreview.com/news/127415/san-francisco-airport-quiet-airport-programme/]. LCY, HEL, and CPH make good design references but poor political ones; a Virginia board will not accept "because Helsinki did it," and neither will American Airlines at DCA.

8. **The long-tenure lesson: standards that outlast the executive win.** The two Dulles decisions everyone now cites — the Saarinen main terminal (kept) and the mobile lounges (retired after 64 years, only after a program-scale reset) — both illustrate the same rule. Architectural and operational standards fossilize into cost drivers that are politically unremovable until a generational capital event forces the reckoning [Source: https://simpleflying.com/washington-dulles-20-billion-revival/]. Quiet-by-design is a chance to set the standard *before* fossilization, at the moment MWAA is spending the money anyway.

## 2. Evidence — through the CEO lens

### 2.1 The bond-rating lens

Rating agency methodology for airport revenue credits is boring, disciplined, and well-documented. Moody's US Airports scorecard weighs debt service coverage, days cash on hand, debt per O&D enplanement, and — the factor most relevant to MWAA — carrier concentration. Coverage benchmarks cluster around a 1.25x senior-lien minimum, with days cash on hand at the Aa band running near 72 days on 2024 medians; carrier concentration weakens the scorecard as it approaches ~50% [Source: https://ratings.moodys.com/api/rmc-documents/74345].

MWAA's disclosed trajectory tells the story: ~$5.5B of new debt between 2025 and 2028, adjusted debt per O&D enplanement peaking near $400 in 2028, coverage compressing toward ~1.3x. That is a system deliberately spending its balance sheet on a generational IAD program plus DCA amenity and concourse work, and telling the rating agencies exactly that [Source: https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/mwaa-goes-to-market].

Where this thesis meets the credit lens: standards that raise per-project soft cost by 1–2% but reduce the incidence of nine-figure change orders during commissioning are a rating-agency positive. The story to tell in the next issuance roadshow is not "we adopted a quiet standard" — it is "we tightened terminal specifications before entering a $22.5B capital cycle, and the projected rework line in our program contingency reflects it." That sentence is worth basis points on the all-in cost of the next issuance.

Where this thesis creates rating risk: standards written as an unfunded mandate on airlines under a hybrid compensatory framework — where costs flow back through the rate base but airlines contest the recovery — invite an airline dispute during exactly the surveillance quarters when Fitch and Moody's are re-examining coverage. A single well-timed United Airlines letter to the board makes a downgrade watch a real conversation. This is not hypothetical; it is the pattern every large-hub CEO has seen at least once.

### 2.2 Airline use-and-lease structure

MWAA's 2025 AUL is a 15-year hybrid compensatory agreement, effective January 1, 2025, that at execution paired with a ~$9B capital program (~$6.99B at IAD, ~$2.39B at DCA). The IAD program has since been publicly re-scoped to a $20B+ level in the July 2026 federal announcement, which itself is a change-in-fact the airline signatories are entitled to evaluate under the terms of the agreement [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airports/press-release/12263924/metropolitan-washington-airports-authority-united-signs-longterm-lease-at-dulles].

For the CEO, the AUL matters in four specific ways for a quiet-by-design standard:

- **MII veto over capital projects.** Standard MII language in hybrid agreements gives signatory airlines a consultation or approval right on capital projects above defined dollar thresholds and on any change that would materially affect the rate base. Specific MII thresholds for the 2025 MWAA AUL were not verified in this packet; treat this as a definitional gap the Strategist must close before recommending a mandatory tier.
- **Recovery mechanics for design standards.** In a hybrid compensatory system, terminal-related cost recovery is generally through terminal rentals and per-passenger charges. A standard applied at design intent (spec, not retrofit) drops cleanly into the recovery base. A standard applied as a rolling upgrade to occupied airline space drops less cleanly, and creates the argument.
- **Tenant-controlled audio and FIDS.** Airline gate podium PA, airline club audio, and airline-branded overlay FIDS are the enforcement problem. The Design Manual binds design; the AUL and downstream tenant agreements bind operation. A standard that does not follow through into the tenant agreements is a design win and an operating loss.
- **The 2039 window and the political horizon.** The next AUL negotiation window is a full CEO turnover away. Standards written now into the Design Manual carry forward through that window; standards written as policy overlays do not.

### 2.3 The federal stack

Three federal calendars run on top of the MWAA calendar and this thesis has to respect all three.

- **FAA Reauthorization** — the 2024 Act (PL 118-63) authorized AIP at $4B annually through FY2028, held the PFC cap at $4.50, and did not create a new funding stream for terminal soft standards [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791]. The next reauthorization fight in 2028 will be where any PFC-cap movement happens, and where a mature, evidence-backed MWAA position on terminal-quality standards is worth more than the same position offered cold.
- **TSA and CBP as involuntary co-designers.** TSA checkpoint acoustic environments and CBP FIS hall PA architectures are federal-partner-controlled and do not follow the MWAA design calendar. Any mandatory-tier standard has to be scoped around the federal envelope, not through it.
- **The DOT lease.** The airports are federal property. This is the single fact that separates MWAA governance from a normal city or port authority and it means every large standards decision has a background reader at the DOT Secretary's office. The July 2026 IAD event demonstrated that this reader is currently very awake.

### 2.4 Board governance and host region

The 17-member board with 7-4-3-3 appointment and six-year staggered terms is designed for continuity over factional wins. That is a feature. Standards decisions that survive a chair transition are the class of decision this board is built to make; single-year branding decisions are not. Chair Uncapher's one-year officer term (2026) and Vogel's vice-chair term are enough to move a Design Manual amendment; they are not enough to defend a contested airline dispute [Source: https://www.aviationpros.com/airport-business/news/55332705/metropolitan-washington-airports-authority-board-elects-2026-officers].

The host-region politics are asymmetric. IAD's political constituency is the Virginia congressional delegation, the DOT, United Airlines, and the loudoun/Fairfax business community — all currently aligned around the $22.5B transformation. DCA's constituency is the DC and Northern Virginia delegation, American Airlines, and — after the January 2025 collision — every family and employee affected by that event. A quiet-by-design standard that reads as "we spent less time on public communication after 67 people died" is a political disaster at DCA regardless of technical merit. The standard must lead with intelligibility and emergency-communication thresholds, not with announcement reduction. The order matters.

### 2.5 The CEO's calendar as evidence

A large-hub CEO's week in this decade is roughly: 30–40% capital program governance and rating-agency preparation; 20–25% government affairs (federal, state, delegation); 15–20% airline relations and AUL execution; 10–15% operations reviews (safety, security, IROPS); balance in board and community. Peer CEOs at ATL, DFW, LAX, MIA, and ORD are visibly spending time on the same three files right now: airline agreement renegotiation, federal partner integration on modernization dollars, and post-2024 climate-and-resilience commitments in new bond covenants. Quiet-by-design is a file the operator runs; the CEO's job is to make sure the file does not create airline, federal-partner, or ADA/accessibility exposure that lands on the CEO's calendar later.

### 2.6 Long-tenure strategic patterns, 2000–2025

Three patterns from the last quarter century worth naming:

- **Standards adopted at program scale beat standards adopted as retrofits.** DFW's design-guideline discipline through the TRIP-era gate renewals, and DEN's baggage-and-tech overspecification lesson, both point the same way. Retrofits cost 3–5x design-intent adoption once tenants are in.
- **Airline agreements that leave the operator with unilateral standards authority age well; those that don't, don't.** ATL and DFW have both benefited from strong operator-side standards clauses; PIT's post-USAir hub-decline period is the cautionary case of an operator whose leverage evaporated when the anchor carrier retreated.
- **The Saarinen/mobile-lounge dyad at IAD is the local case in point.** The mobile lounges were an innovation in 1962 and an operational millstone by 1985. It took 64 years and a $22.5B program reset to remove them [Source: https://simpleflying.com/washington-dulles-20-billion-revival/]. The lesson is that early standards choices calcify into cost centers. The corollary is that the moment MWAA is opening a program of this scale is the correct moment to set the next generation of standards, not to postpone them.

## 3. The financial-and-political case, as the CEO sees it

**For adopting the standard now, at the Design Manual tier:**

- Rework and change-order avoidance across the IAD program alone justifies the exercise. Even a 25 basis-point reduction in program contingency draw against a $20B+ notional is a $50M institutional saving, roughly one year's worth of the Design Manual's total maintenance cost.
- The standard slots cleanly into the existing seven-volume Design Manual apparatus. There is no new instrument to defend to a board or to a rating agency.
- It is an unforced political win — technically credible, ADA-defensive, and post-DCA-collision-responsible if built on intelligibility rather than silence.
- It creates a durable institutional artifact that survives the next chair transition and the next CEO transition.

**Against, or at least against a poorly-structured adoption:**

- MII risk is real and not fully scoped in the packet. A standard that flows unrecovered soft cost into the airline rate base during a compressing-coverage window is the wrong story for the wrong quarter.
- Federal-partner enforcement is soft. TSA and CBP will do what they do; a standard MWAA cannot enforce inside its own FIS hall or checkpoints is a standard with a visible gap.
- Political mistranslation risk. "Quiet airport" reads well in a design brief and reads badly in a hearing about the January 2025 collision. Framing discipline is non-optional.
- Tenant coverage. Concessionaires and airline clubs generate the audio that passengers actually notice. A design-side standard that stops at the demising wall is a partial standard.

The CEO's judgment: adopt at the Design Manual tier, tie every mandatory requirement to a measurable performance threshold (STI, LEQ, SNR) rather than to a prescription, and negotiate the tenant and airline coverage separately and slower. The prize is not a quieter airport; the prize is a standards governance win during the largest capital cycle in MWAA history.

## 4. Data points and precedents a Strategist can use

1. **MWAA credit position, May 2025:** Fitch AA−, Moody's Aa3, S&P AA−, all stable. Projected coverage compression from 1.63x (2024) toward ~1.3x over five years; adjusted debt per O&D enplanement peaking near $400 in 2028. Use to frame the "why standards discipline matters now" argument [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/financial-statistics/investor-relations/credit-ratings].

2. **SFO Quiet Airport benchmark:** ~40% reduction in gate-area announcements; ~90 minutes of daily announcement time removed after the 2018/2020 program expansion. The methodology behind the reduction figure is not fully documented in primary sources and should be treated as reported result, not audited result [Source: https://www.internationalairportreview.com/news/127415/san-francisco-airport-quiet-airport-programme/].

3. **ACRP Research Report 175:** the domestic reference standard. Minimum STI of 0.45 in departure lounges, SNR target of 10–15 dB, and a commissioning regime that requires the fire/emergency PA input to be measured for STI at handover. Any MWAA mandatory tier should cite this document as the baseline [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/5].

4. **IEC 60268-16 and EN 54-16:** the international standard framework for STI measurement, with a widely-adopted emergency voice-alarm minimum of STI 0.50, and the categorical bands (Bad <0.30, Poor 0.30–0.45, Fair 0.45–0.60, Good 0.60–0.75, Excellent >0.75). This is the technical vocabulary the Design Manual should adopt [Source: https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26771].

5. **FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (PL 118-63):** AIP authorized at $4B annually through FY2028; PFC cap held at $4.50 (unchanged since 2000); DCA 10 additional daily beyond-perimeter slot exemptions awarded December 2024. Establishes that terminal-quality soft standards will be paid from bonded debt and airline rates, not from a new federal fund [Source: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12791].
