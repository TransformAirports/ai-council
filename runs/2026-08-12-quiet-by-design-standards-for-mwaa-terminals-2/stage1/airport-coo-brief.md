# Airport COO Brief — Quiet-by-Design Standards for MWAA Terminals

**Agent:** airport-coo (operator's chair, 25 years, veteran of the duty desk)
**Run:** quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2
**Date:** 2026-08-10
**Independent brief.** Written without reading any other Stage 1 output.

---

## 1. Key findings — what an experienced operator would recognize as true

1. **The PA is an operational lever, not a nuisance to be minimized.** Strategy decks treat announcements as environmental noise. The duty manager treats them as the only tool that reaches every person in a gate hold room at the same instant when a gate changes at 5:47 AM. A quiet-by-design standard that does not preserve the operator's ability to reach 100 percent of a hold room in under 30 seconds will fail on the first snap gate change and be argued as a change order for the life of the terminal. This is professional judgment; it is also the load-bearing sentence in this brief.

2. **The measurable standards already exist.** IEC 60268-16 defines the Speech Transmission Index and STIPA measurement method [Source: https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26771]. NFPA 72 sets a floor of STI 0.45 or higher at all occupant locations for emergency voice systems, with 0.70 as the design target [Source: https://www.securitysales.com/integration/mass-notification-intelligibility/]. ACRP Research Report 175 recommends a minimum STI of 0.45 and a signal-to-noise ratio of 10–15 dB(A) for public address broadcasts in pier-style departure lounges [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/5]. The COO's problem is not what the number should be. The problem is that we measure it once at commissioning and never again.

3. **Design manuals bind consultants; they do not bind tenants.** MWAA's seven-volume 2020 Design Manual has the force of law on airport property [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/business/airports-authority-design-manual]. But airline club audio, concessions music, gate podium speakers, and airline-installed FIDS overlays are the loudest offenders in every large US hub terminal on the ramp side of any Monday-morning acoustic walkthrough. If quiet-by-design does not reach tenant lease exhibits, airline gate podium equipment standards, and concessions design criteria, the standard controls the shell and leaves the noise sources unregulated. That is not a hypothetical failure mode — it is the failure mode.

4. **IROPS is where quiet standards break.** In the first 90 minutes of a snow event, an ATC ground stop, a security breach, or a Metrorail failure that strands 4,000 employees, the operations center pivots to broadcast-to-many messaging. ACRP Report 153 documents the communication and coordination burden airports carry during IROPS [Source: https://crp.trb.org/acrpwebresource2/guidebook-for-irops-stakeholder-communication-coordination/]. If PA is architected into 200 automatically triggered micro-zones with scripted content, the duty manager cannot override on the timeline required to re-board 26 diverted airplanes. The standard must specify an operator-controlled override mode with pre-authorized authority and audible reach — and it must be tested annually, not documented once.

5. **Accessibility and non-English speakers set the floor, not the ceiling.** ADA Standards §706 requires assistive listening in assembly areas with defined SPL and dynamic-range performance [Source: https://www.corada.com/documents/ada-aba-guidelines-preamble/706]. IAD ran roughly 36 percent international share in 2025 [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md]. Visual-first strategies that work at London City — a small terminal with a fast curb-to-gate journey and a business skew [Source: https://www.londoncityairport.com/at-the-airport/need-to-know/airport-policies/silent-policy] — do not transfer directly to an international hub where the marginal passenger is a first-time traveler navigating in a second language. Redundant channels — audio, visual FIDS, mobile push, and staffed intervention — are the accessibility standard the operator has to defend.

6. **The DCA midair collision is the operational memory that will interrogate this standard.** The February 2026 NTSB final report on PSA/American 5342 identified degraded radio reception and missed advisories among the contributing factors [Source: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/AIR2602.pdf]. Any MWAA standard that reduces the operator's or ARFF's capacity to reach every person in a terminal at the same instant will be interrogated at the board level for exactly that reason. The lesson is not that we should announce more. The lesson is that redundant, tested, and operator-controlled communications survive events that automated single-channel systems do not.

7. **The MWAA Design Manual is the only vehicle that will actually bind future work.** MWAA plans approximately $5.5 billion of new debt between 2025 and 2028 [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md], with the IAD "$20 billion+ transformation" announced July 2026 [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md]. Concourses C and D replacement, Terminal 1 replacement at DCA, and every follow-on IAD element will be scoped, procured, and commissioned inside the next standards window. Standards adopted as a stand-alone policy get argued down in change-order negotiations. Standards adopted as new or amended Design Manual volumes — with IAD- and DCA-specific supplements consistent with MWAA's existing seven-volume architecture — are enforceable in design review and in commissioning.

8. **Lifecycle cost is not "the PA will last 25 years."** DSP-based zoning heads, ambient-sensitive gain control, and STI verification instrumentation drift over time and require re-calibration. NFPA 72's intelligibility requirement is not a one-time commissioning gate — it is a continuing performance requirement. Post-occupancy verification, at defined intervals, is where the standard lives or dies. This is a recurring operating-cost line, and the COO would rather see it funded honestly at Day 1 than deferred into a decade of drift.

## 2. Evidence — sources cited inline

### 2.1 Measurable standards already exist

The Speech Transmission Index is defined internationally in IEC 60268-16:2020, which specifies the STI model, test signals, measurement, and prediction methods for objective rating of speech intelligibility [Source: https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26771]. STIPA — the Speech Transmission Index for Public Address — enables a ~10–20 second single-point measurement per location, making annual re-verification operationally feasible [Source: https://acousplan.com/blog/iec-60268-16-sti-measurement]. IEC's five qualitative bands are Bad (<0.30), Poor (0.30–0.45), Fair (0.45–0.60), Good (0.60–0.75), and Excellent (>0.75) [Source: https://acousplan.com/blog/iec-60268-16-sti-measurement].

NFPA 72 sets a minimum STI of 0.45 (Fair on the Common Intelligibility Scale) at all occupant locations, with a design target of 0.70 across 90 percent of the covered area for mass-notification systems [Source: https://www.securitysales.com/integration/mass-notification-intelligibility/].

ACRP Research Report 175 provides the U.S. airport-specific baseline: minimum STI 0.45 and SNR of 10–15 dB(A) for PA broadcasts in pier-style departure lounges [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/5]. The report specifically addresses commissioning [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/11] and system design [Source: https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24839/chapter/9] for terminal environments — which is the reference document that any MWAA standard should extend and localize, not replace.

### 2.2 MWAA's design apparatus

MWAA maintains a seven-volume 2020 Design Manual, plus Specifications and Estimating and Scheduling Standards, issued by the MWAA Office of Engineering [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/business/airports-authority-design-manual]. Airport-specific supporting volumes exist for IAD [Source: https://www.mwaa.com/sites/mwaa.com/files/2021-09/06_IAD_Vol_3_2020.pdf]. Specifications are edited from MASTERSPEC for MWAA use, and the Design Manual is characterized by MWAA as having the force of law on airport property [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md].

### 2.3 IROPS coordination and communication burden

ACRP Report 153 provides the U.S. domestic reference for IROPS stakeholder communication and coordination, including communication checklists, flow diagrams for integration of communication and collaboration processes, case studies of IROPS response scenarios, tabletop-exercise instructions, and risk-prediction tools [Source: https://crp.trb.org/acrpwebresource2/guidebook-for-irops-stakeholder-communication-coordination/]. The report follows on and expands ACRP Report 65, published February 2012 [Source: https://crp.trb.org/acrp0715/wp-content/themes/acrp-child/documents/051/original/ACRP_65_Guidebook_for_Airport_Irregular_Operations_(IROPS)_Contingency_Planning.pdf].

FAA AC 150/5200-31C requires that the Airport Emergency Plan be functionally oriented, comprehensive in the assignment of responsibilities, and coordinated at all levels [Source: https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/150_5200_31c_consolidated.pdf]. The AEP is the vehicle that scopes an operator's obligation to notify people on the property during an emergency. A quiet-standard that constrains PA reach without accounting for AEP notification obligations is out of compliance in the first exercise cycle.

Guidance for Planning, Design, and Operations of Airport Communications Centers is documented in ACRP Report 182 [Source: https://crp.trb.org/acrp0715/wp-content/themes/acrp-child/documents/208/original/acrp_r182.pdf]. It is the working reference for how operations and communications centers are structured — the room in which the override authority for any zoned PA architecture actually lives.

### 2.4 Peer practice — what is documented, what is branding

- **SFO Quiet Airport Policy:** launched 2018, implemented through 2019–2020, includes announcement localization (final calls only broadcast in adjacent gate areas) and tenant "sound footprint" rules for retail. Reported outcomes: approximately 40 percent reduction in announcements at gate seating areas, and elimination of over 90 minutes of announcements per day in the International Terminal [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/san-francisco-airport-removed-90-minutes-of-daily-noise-travelers-say-it-changed-everything/]. Reported friction: some passengers describe missed hearing of gate changes [Source: https://simpleflying.com/airport-quiet-policy-frustrating-passengers-sfo/]. The measurement methodology behind the 40 percent and 90-minute figures was not verified in primary sources for this brief.

- **London City Airport:** silent policy in which announcements over speakers are used only in bad weather or emergency; passengers rely on information screens throughout the lounge and restaurants [Source: https://www.londoncityairport.com/at-the-airport/need-to-know/airport-policies/silent-policy]. Accessibility caveat noted in third-party accessibility guides — no audible announcements in the departure lounge poses a challenge for passengers with visual impairments [Source: https://www.accessable.co.uk/london-city-airport/access-guides/departure-lounge-london-london-city-airport].

- **Helsinki (Finavia):** "Silent Airport" initiative — no flight or passenger announcements in the terminal; passengers use FIDS, website, and mobile app [Source: https://morepremium.com/helsinki-vantaa-to-be-made-a-quiet-airport/]. Newer facilities include acoustic panelling, natural light, and neutral colour schemes selected after passenger consultations [Source: https://www.futuretravelexperience.com/2013/10/helsinki-airports-relaxation-area-making-airport-enjoyable-part-end-end-journey/].

- **Copenhagen (CPH):** no loudspeaker announcements for "Go to gate" and "Boarding"; information via FIDS and the CPH app [Source: https://www.cph.dk/en/practical/gate-information-and-boarding]. Designated quiet zones inside the terminal [Source: https://www.cph.dk/en/practical/seating-areas].

These four peer airports have profiles fundamentally different from IAD — smaller footprint, shorter curb-to-gate distances, English- or Nordic-language dominance, more homogeneous passenger populations. They are useful precedents for the *policy architecture*; they are not transferrable prescriptions for a hub with 36 percent international share, connecting-passenger flows, and mobile-lounge retirement in progress.

### 2.5 Accessibility floor

ADA Standards §706 requires assistive listening systems in assembly areas to deliver 110 dB minimum and 118 dB maximum SPL with 50 dB dynamic range on the volume control, and receivers must be compatible with headphones and neck loops [Source: https://www.corada.com/documents/ada-aba-guidelines-preamble/706]. Signage under §216.10 must display the International Symbol of Access for Hearing Loss [Source: http://www.ada-compliance.com/ada-compliance/706-assistive-listening-systems]. Any quiet-by-design standard has to reconcile with §706 wherever it applies to hold rooms, gate areas, and processing floors — visual-only channels are not sufficient in an assembly area where audible communication is integral.

### 2.6 The operational memory in the room

The NTSB final report on the January 29, 2025 midair collision between PSA/American 5342 and a U.S. Army UH-60L was released in February 2026 [Source: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/AIR2602.pdf]. Communications-side findings included degraded radio reception that caused a controller advisory to be captured on the helicopter cockpit voice recorder without the word "circling" [Source: https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/ntsb-final-report-causes-of-the-midair-collision-at-reagan-national-airport]. The event is not a PA-standard event; it is an ATC and airspace-design event. But it is the recent operational memory the MWAA Board and executive team carry into any conversation about reducing communications density.

### 2.7 Financial and capital context

MWAA holds Fitch AA−, Moody's Aa3, and S&P AA−, all stable outlook as of May 2025 [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md]. Net revenue debt service coverage is expected to fall from 1.63x in 2024 to roughly 1.3x over the next five years [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md]. The 15-year Airline Use and Lease Agreement effective January 1, 2025 carried an approximately $9 billion capital program at execution [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md]. The IAD "$20 billion+ transformation" was announced July 29, 2026 [Source: outputs/context/airport-context.md].

## 3. The operational case, as the COO sees it

### 3.1 For the thesis

- **Design-in is one to two orders of magnitude cheaper than retrofit.** Ceiling absorption specified at concept design is negligible marginal cost; the same treatment installed after handoff is scaffold, night work, tenant coordination, and a change order. STI verification instrumentation designed into the base building costs little; retrofitted STIPA fixed-position monitoring is a program.
- **Standards written into the Design Manual bind the consultants and contractors before they burn hours on assumptions we disagree with.** The single biggest source of operational pain at handoff is design decisions that were locked before operations reviewed them. Adopting standards while Concourse C/D and DCA Terminal 1 are still in early design is the last cheap window.
- **A tiered standard — mandatory / recommended / context-dependent — is exactly how the Design Manual already speaks.** It fits the vehicle.
- **Post-occupancy STI, ambient LEQ, and reverberation verification give operations a lever with facilities and with tenants that we currently do not have.** Today a tenant with an over-driven club audio system is a customer-relations conversation. With a measurable standard in the lease exhibit, it is a compliance conversation.

### 3.2 Against the thesis, or where it needs work before I sign

- **A standard that reduces PA reach without preserving an operator override will fail on the first snap event.** Non-negotiable.
- **A standard that only touches airport-owned assets misses tenant and airline audio — the loudest offenders.** The lease and design-criteria vehicles have to move together, or the standard controls the wrong surface.
- **A standard that is measured once at commissioning drifts.** The operating budget for STI re-verification, ambient sensor recalibration, and quarterly walkthroughs has to be committed at adoption, not deferred.
- **A single standard applied identically to IAD and DCA breaks somewhere.** IAD gate hold rooms with mobile-lounge feeds today, retirement in progress, connecting international passengers, and long curb-to-gate distances are a different acoustic and communication problem from DCA's slot-controlled, business-domestic, tight-footprint terminals. Airport-specific supplements are not a nice-to-have.
- **Emergency communications reach is a legal and regulatory obligation, not a design preference.** FAA AC 150/5200-31C and §706 do not care about the elegance of a quiet-airport concept. The mandatory tier of any MWAA standard must clear both.

## 4. Specific examples — from the operator's chair

### 4.1 The 5:47 AM gate swap at IAD C24

A B737 inbound to C24 has a jet bridge fault flagged by the ramp. The duty manager needs to re-gate to C22 in the eight minutes before pushback pressure builds behind it. Under today's PA architecture, a manual announcement into the C concourse hold rooms is a keystroke and 10 seconds. Under a poorly-specified quiet standard that automates the script and zones the announcement only into "affected" seating areas as detected by a boarding-status feed, the message never reaches passengers who wandered to the restroom, the coffee shop across the concourse, or the airline club. The result is a delayed pushback and a missed rotation. The standard needs to preserve concourse-wide operator override, with authority delegated to the duty manager on shift — not routed through a system administrator during shift change.

### 4.2 The DCA January 29 aftermath

Within minutes of the January 29, 2025 midair collision, DCA had to hold and re-plan a full terminal of departures, coordinate with ARFF and federal responders on the airfield, and communicate with the traveling public inside the terminal — many of whom had seen the impact from the concourse windows. The AEP-driven notification burden in the first 90 minutes was substantial. Any standard adopted now must survive a tabletop of that scenario before it goes to the Board.

### 4.3 The airline club floor overrun

At most large US hubs, an airline club installed in a mezzanine above a hold room routinely overruns audio into the concourse below because the acoustic separation was value-engineered out of the tenant fit-out. Absent a lease-embedded acoustic isolation requirement (STC and IIC targets, plus SPL bleed measurements at commissioning), the operator has no enforceable remedy. A quiet-by-design standard that does not include tenant lease exhibits is a paper standard.

### 4.4 The concourse retail speaker

Concessions music at retail storefronts along a concourse spine is a persistent SNR problem for gate announcements. SFO's tenant "sound footprint" model [Source: https://viewfromthewing.com/san-francisco-airport-removed-90-minutes-of-daily-noise-travelers-say-it-changed-everything/] is directly transferable and belongs in MWAA's concessions design criteria and tenant work-letter — not just in the Design Manual.

### 4.5 The AeroTrain-extension station handoff

The IAD AeroTrain extension announced as part of the $20B+ program will hand back a series of new station platforms with tunnel-station acoustics, arriving-train noise, and information-system tie-ins. If quiet-by-design STI thresholds and reverberation targets are not written into the program brief before consultant scopes are procured, the platforms will be handed back reverberant and unintelligible. That is a familiar pattern from every APM extension the industry has delivered without an operator-signed acoustic performance requirement.

## 5. What I would tell the Strategist

The thesis is right in direction. It is at risk of being written as a passenger-comfort argument when the load-bearing case is capital cost, tenant enforceability, and IROPS operational reach. Frame it as: *we adopt a Design Manual amendment now, at the last cheap window before Concourses C/D, DCA Terminal 1, and the AeroTrain extension harden, because retrofitting acoustic and PA performance into commissioned terminals costs more and delivers less, and because we can bind tenants at lease renewal only if the measurable standard exists to point at.* Then, and only then, argue the passenger-experience case, because it will land more credibly when the operator has already made the operational and financial case in the same document.

Do not let the peer-airport section become a Copenhagen highlight reel. The right way to use SFO, LCY, HEL, CPH is as documentation that each of them made a *policy architecture choice*, not as case studies whose numbers transfer. IAD is a different animal. DCA is a third animal.

If the standard cannot answer these three questions in mandatory-tier language, it is not ready:

- What is the operator's override authority, how fast does it work, and how is it tested?
- What is the tenant enforcement mechanism, and in which lease exhibit does it live?
- What is the post-occupancy verification cadence, who pays for it, and what happens when a space fails?

Anything else is styling.
