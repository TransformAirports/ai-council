# The Loud Terminal Is the Unintelligible One

**A quiet-by-design standard for MWAA terminals — argued as safety engineering, delivered through the Design Manual.**

*Run: quiet-by-design-standards-for-mwaa-terminals-2 · Strategist draft v1 · 2026-08-10*

---

## Executive Summary

MWAA is about to scope, procure, and commission more than $20 billion of terminal space at IAD and another $2.39 billion at DCA under a new fifteen-year Airline Use and Lease Agreement.[^1] Every square foot will pass through the seven-volume MWAA Design Manual, which the Authority describes as "a mandatory guide with the force of law on the airport property."[^2] The Council's recommendation is narrow, cheap, and time-limited: adopt a tiered quiet-by-design volume into the Design Manual this capital cycle, and exercise it first on the DCA Terminal 1 concourse replacement.

The standard should not be argued as fewer announcements. After the January 29, 2025 midair collision at DCA and the February 2026 NTSB final report, no MWAA board member should sign a policy paraphrasable as "reduced safety broadcasts."[^3] The standard is defensible only if it is engineered as the opposite: a communications-integrity floor that raises the probability that the one announcement that matters — the gate change, the shelter-in-place, the diverted flow during irregular operations — is understood by the passenger who needs it. Intelligibility, not silence, is the variable. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 requires emergency voice systems to achieve a Speech Transmission Index of at least 0.45 at ninety percent of measurement locations within each Acoustically Distinguishable Space, with an average of at least 0.50.[^4] ACRP Report 175 recommends STI in the 0.46–0.53 band with a target of 0.50 for terminal public address, and a signal-to-noise ratio of 10–15 dB(A) in pier-style departure lounges.[^5] IEC 60268-16:2020 supplies the measurement method.[^6] The loud, reverberant terminal is the one that fails these numbers.

Three propositions carry the case. First, the incremental cost is a rounding error against the program — acoustic treatment premiums for the concourse-scale envelope at design time run an order of magnitude cheaper than at retrofit.[^7] Second, the enforcement vehicle already exists: MWAA's Design Manual and MASTERSPEC-derived specifications bind consultants, contractors, and tenants today.[^8] Third, the window is finite. MWAA's debt-service coverage compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× over the next five years while ~$5.5 billion of new debt phases in;[^9] every standard adopted after design freeze becomes a change order.

The mandatory-versus-context split falls out of the evidence. Common to both airports: intelligibility floors, ADS mapping, commissioning under representative traffic, accessibility redundancy, announcement governance with audit logging. Varying by airport: the physical toolkit that hits those numbers inside a Saarinen-era head-house, a slot-controlled DCA peak, and a new-build IAD midfield concourse. The Board decision is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## The Loud Terminal Is the Unintelligible One

Start not at an airport. Start in a hospital intensive-care unit around 2013. Monitoring systems had reached the point where a single Johns Hopkins ICU study found an average of 771 alarms per bed per day.[^10] Clinicians had stopped hearing them. The alarm that mattered sounded like the alarm that did not. The Joint Commission's response was not to reduce alarms in the name of patient comfort. It was National Patient Safety Goal NPSG.06.01.01, phased in 2014 and 2016, which required every accredited hospital to inventory its alarms, rank them, govern them, and prove it did so.[^11] The insight was that indiscriminate broadcast is itself the safety failure.

Cross to IAD at 5:30 p.m. on a weekday in September 2025, in the international connecting bank. A gate change at Concourse C paged overhead. A TSA advisory. Two boarding calls at adjacent gates. A courtesy page for a lost party. A concessions employee overhead reaching a colleague. On the record, the announcement summary looks like operations serving the passenger. In the acoustic reality of a reverberant holdroom shared with two adjacent gates, it is the paradigm case of the alarm no one hears. IAD moved 29.01 million passengers in 2025, 10.53 million of them international — the highest 2025 growth rate among the fifty largest U.S. airports.[^12] DCA moved 24.89 million, down 5.4% in the wake of the January collision and the 43-day federal shutdown.[^13] Every one of those passengers arrived expecting the announcement that matters to reach them. The question is whether the buildings the announcement travels through were specified to let it.

MWAA is now about to specify — from the studs up — more than $20 billion of new terminal space. On July 29, 2026, the President, the U.S. Transportation Secretary, United's CEO, and MWAA jointly announced a Dulles transformation of more than 5 million square feet: Concourses C and D replaced, AeroTrain extended, mobile lounges retired, Saarinen's Main Terminal preserved.[^14] The announcement summary describes concourses, baggage systems, and Customs and Border Protection facilities. It does not mention a single acoustic or public-address specification. Meanwhile, under the fifteen-year Airline Use and Lease Agreement that took effect January 1, 2025, MWAA carries approximately $6.99 billion of committed capital at IAD and $2.39 billion at DCA, the latter including the eventual replacement of the Terminal 1 main concourse.[^15] Every one of those square feet will be scoped, procured, and commissioned inside the next standards window. Standards adopted late are argued as change orders.

The obvious objection surfaces first. After NTSB AIR-26/02, no MWAA board member wants a policy that could be paraphrased on cable news as "airport cuts safety announcements." That instinct is correct. This article agrees — and that agreement is the argument. The trap in the "silent airport" literature is the equation of quietness with subtraction. What the evidence base actually supports is the inverse. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 sets an intelligibility floor for emergency voice systems that the code writers scoped to Acoustically Distinguishable Spaces because they knew a terminal is not one acoustic environment.[^4] The way you fail that floor is not by being too quiet. It is by being loud, reverberant, and cross-broadcast — precisely the condition an unspecified terminal defaults to. High ambient noise pushes STI down the qualitative bands defined by IEC 60268-16 (Bad, Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent).[^6] The louder the terminal, the less intelligible the emergency announcement. Every acoustic and PA-zoning requirement in the recommended standard flows from that inversion.

The peer set frequently held up as models is, on close reading, a category error — and that is liberating. London City Airport has operated a silent policy since 2008, extended in 2016.[^16] Amsterdam Schiphol, Helsinki-Vantaa (since 2015), and Copenhagen each describe reduced-announcement operating models with information moved to flight-information displays and airport apps.[^17] What none of them has published, in any source this Council found, is measured intelligibility, missed-boarding, or channel-redundancy data. They are single-terminal, short-haul, high-English-proficiency operations with traveler mixes that were quieter in the first place. They are policy analogues for MWAA — not design precedents. IAD's 36%-plus international share and DCA's slot-constrained peaks are not their problems. MWAA does not need to import their brand. It needs to out-engineer them.

SFO is the closest North American precedent, and it shows both the model and the failure mode at once. SFO reports that its Quiet Airport program removed roughly 90 minutes of daily terminal announcement time, cut individual paging from 492 to 261 occurrences per day, and reduced overall onsite noise by more than 40%.[^18] Every one of those figures is SFO's own; the Council could not identify a published measurement methodology behind any of them in the industry record. Trade press repeats the numbers verbatim. In the same record are documented passenger complaints and missed-flight anecdotes.[^19] The operating model — zoned delivery, discontinued cross-terminal final calls, gate-area retention — is well-described and worth borrowing. The outcome numbers are not audited evidence, and MWAA should quote them as SFO reports them, not as facts. The lesson is not "don't do this." The lesson is that announcement reduction without intelligibility engineering and channel redundancy is subtraction, not design.

The people who most need to hear are the test the recommendation has to pass. 28 CFR 35.160 obligates MWAA, as a public entity, to ensure effective communication with people with disabilities.[^20] The 2010 ADA Standards §219 and §706 require assistive-listening systems in each assembly area where audible communication is integral to use.[^21] Airlines operating at IAD and DCA are subject to 14 CFR Part 382, which imposes accessible-information requirements at gate, ticket, and customer-service counters, and mandates accessible airport kiosks at up to 25% of installed machines under the December 2016 rulemaking.[^22] IAD and DCA, as Part 139 certificated airports, carry emergency-communication requirements at the airfield boundary.[^23] IAD's Saarinen Main Terminal is listed on the National Register (1978), meaning Section 106 consultation attaches to changes with federal involvement.[^24] Read together, these floors say the same thing: any regime that reduces broadcast without specifying redundant visual, digital, staffed, and hearing-loop channels compounds exclusion. A standard that mandates those redundancies binds MWAA harder than current practice, not softer. If MWAA were seeking ambience, it would not be volunteering for measurable compliance.

Governance is the technology. The Airports Council International – North America has framed the newer generation of automated announcement platforms in terms MWAA's Design Manual can actually enforce: centralized management, replay and audit logs of every broadcast, elimination of manual-initiation errors, and templated multi-language delivery.[^25] That reframing matters. It converts "announcement policy" — a customer-experience conversation that dies in a change-management meeting — into operational integrity with an enforcement trail an internal auditor and a fire marshal can both read. This is the Joint Commission move, applied to airports: inventory, rank, govern, log. It is also what makes the tenant question tractable. At preferential-use gates, the podium PA is effectively airline-owned. United operates approximately 50 jetbridge gates and moves about 67% of IAD traffic; American holds roughly 53% of DCA market share.[^26] A standard that cannot reach tenant audio governs half the soundscape. A standard that specifies platform behavior — audit logs, override protocols, tier discipline — reaches through the tenant technology standards and the AUL to the microphone at the podium.

The window and the vehicle exist together, and both are finite. MWAA's Design Manual is the seven-volume mandatory guide MWAA already updates annually, with airport-specific supporting volumes for IAD and DCA.[^2] A quiet-by-design amendment is not a new bureaucracy; it is a chapter added to an apparatus that already binds consultants, contractors, and — through tenant technology standards and MASTERSPEC-derived specifications — the airlines and concessionaires whose systems set the soundscape today. What the Council did not verify is what that Manual currently says about acoustics, PA zoning, STI, or ambient-noise thresholds. That verification is step one; the shape of the amendment (new volume versus insertion into Volume 2) turns on it. The financial case does not rest on new capital. It rests on rework avoidance. Design-time acoustic ceiling treatment runs on the order of $5–$15 per square foot in new construction and $18–$45 per square foot as retrofit — a 2× to 4× premium once finishes are installed.[^7] Against the announced program, an order-of-magnitude range for the full quiet-by-design stack — ceiling treatment premium, FIDS redundancy, mobile notification platform — sits between 0.06% and 0.20% of the IAD program.[^7] MWAA's coverage window makes the case sharper, not softer: DSCR compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× through 2029 while approximately $5.5 billion of new debt phases in and adjusted debt per O&D enplanement peaks near $400 in 2028.[^9] In that window, the easy Board sell is the specification that prevents late-stage redesign. The hard sell is the recovery-eligible retrofit. The recommendation is the first one.

So the standard writes itself as a two-tier document. Mandatory at both airports: an Acoustically Distinguishable Space map as a design deliverable; intelligibility floors per NFPA 72 Chapter 24 (STI ≥0.45 at 90% of points, average ≥0.50) and ACRP 175 (target 0.50, band 0.46–0.53, SNR 10–15 dB(A)); measurement per IEC 60268-16:2020, with the standard's explicit exclusion of fluctuating noise honored — commissioning under representative traffic, not at 06:00 in an empty building;[^27] accessibility floors per 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, and 14 CFR Part 382; hearing-conservation compliance per 29 CFR 1910.95 (85 dB(A) 8-hour TWA);[^28] and announcement governance with audit logging, tier discipline, and template control. Common in principle but tiered by context: PA zoning depth, sensory-quiet zone scope (ANSI/ASA S12.60 references NC 27 / 35 dB(A) as a low-stimulation benchmark),[^29] tenant-audio enforcement mechanics, and the physical toolkit that hits the numbers inside a listed Saarinen head-house versus a new-build midfield concourse versus a Pelli-era DCA terminal.

Exercise it first where the friction is lowest. The DCA Terminal 1 main concourse replacement is smaller, less politicized, and less encumbered by the July 2026 political stack around Dulles. Adopting the standard there — as a standing requirement, not a pilot — gives the Office of Engineering the reference application that Concourses C and D and every follow-on program element will inherit. Concourse E, opening this fall at IAD with fourteen new United gates,[^30] is the natural place to capture the first commissioned STI and ambient-noise baseline under representative traffic. That baseline becomes the number every future MWAA facility is measured against.

The counter-case deserves an honest hearing. IEC 60268-16 explicitly excludes fluctuating noise from its measurement model, and terminals are the paradigm fluctuating-noise environment; a common standard built on this arithmetic can be sold to a Board as more rigorous than it is.[^27] The most-cited North American precedent has no published measurement methodology; borrowing its language without demanding its data inherits the flaw. The European peer set is structurally unlike either MWAA airport. The Design Manual pathway is slower than the case for standards implies. And the Council did not verify what the current Manual already says, or whether the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest provisions permit MWAA to move on tenant audio without airline consent.[^15] Each of these is a real constraint. None is fatal. The response to a measurement standard that excludes fluctuating noise is to specify measurement under representative traffic as an MWAA requirement — not to abandon measurement. The response to unverified peer numbers is to cite the operating model and not the outcome. The response to Design Manual latency is to start now, before design freeze on the projects the money is already committed to. The response to the AUL uncertainty is to write the verification first and negotiate the tenant enforcement scope second.

Return to the opening image. In 2013, the Joint Commission did not tell hospitals to reduce alarms. It told them to prove they knew which ones mattered, that the mattering ones would be heard, and that the machinery for governing the rest was on the record. Ten years later, the ICUs are not silent. They are audible in the right places, at the right times, to the right people. That is what a quiet-by-design standard buys an airport. The board question is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## Airport Decision

**Owner:** MWAA President and CEO; Office of Engineering (drafting authority); Board of Directors (adoption).

**Decision:** Direct the Office of Engineering to draft a tiered quiet-by-design amendment to the MWAA Design Manual, framed as a communications-integrity and intelligibility standard, in time to govern the scope documents for the DCA Terminal 1 replacement and the IAD Concourse C/D replacement.

**First 90 days:** (1) Verify current acoustic, PA-zoning, STI, and ambient-noise content of the Design Manual and Specifications. (2) Draft mandatory-tier language keyed to NFPA 72 Ch. 24, ACRP 175, IEC 60268-16:2020 (with representative-traffic measurement), 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, 14 CFR Part 382, and 29 CFR 1910.95. (3) Commission a first ADS map and baseline STI/ambient reading at IAD Concourse E under representative traffic within 90 days of opening.

**Cost order of magnitude:** $12M–$40M one-time across the full stack (ceiling treatment premium, FIDS redundancy, mobile-notification platform); the standalone CPE effect approaches zero if the standard rides inside program financing for the announced IAD transformation.[^7]

**Dependencies:** Airline consultation under the 2025 AUL (MII provisions not verified in this run); tenant technology standards for concessions and airline club audio; Section 106 consultation on any Saarinen-adjacent modification; FAA/AHJ concurrence on NFPA 72 edition in force.

**Leading indicator:** DCA Terminal 1 replacement scope documents released with mandatory quiet-by-design language cited by section.

**Failure mode / stop condition:** If the current Design Manual already contains equivalent or stronger acoustic and PA-zoning requirements, the recommendation contracts to a governance-and-verification amendment rather than a new volume. If the AUL's MII text materially constrains tenant-audio enforcement, the tenant tier drops from mandatory to recommended pending renegotiation.

**What would change this recommendation:** Publication of a measured MWAA STI/ambient baseline showing existing terminal spaces already meet ACRP 175 and NFPA 72 targets under representative traffic. Alternatively, MWAA-published PA telemetry showing that announcement density is already below the peer-airport reduced-practice band. Neither is currently on the record.
