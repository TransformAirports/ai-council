## Executive Summary

MWAA holds approximately $6.99 billion of committed capital at IAD and $2.39 billion at DCA under the fifteen-year Airline Use and Lease Agreement that took effect January 1, 2025.[^1] On July 29, 2026, the President, the U.S. Transportation Secretary, United's CEO, and MWAA jointly announced a Dulles transformation described at more than $20 billion — a figure that remains provisional until Board action, program-budget amendment, and airline-consent documents are on the record.[^14] Every square foot of committed and announced work will pass through the seven-volume MWAA Design Manual, which the Authority describes as "a mandatory guide with the force of law on the airport property."[^2] The Council's recommendation is narrow, cheap, and time-limited: adopt a tiered quiet-by-design volume into the Design Manual this capital cycle, and exercise it first on the DCA Terminal 1 concourse replacement — subject to that project's phase status, which the Council did not verify.

The standard should not be argued as fewer announcements. After the January 29, 2025 midair collision at DCA and the February 2026 NTSB final report, no MWAA board member should sign a policy paraphrasable as "reduced safety broadcasts."[^3] The standard is defensible only if it is engineered as the opposite: a communications-integrity floor that raises the probability the announcement that matters — the gate change, the shelter-in-place, the diverted flow during irregular operations — is understood by the passenger who needs it. Intelligibility, not silence, is the variable. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 requires emergency voice systems to achieve a Speech Transmission Index of at least 0.45 at ninety percent of measurement locations within each Acoustically Distinguishable Space, with an average of at least 0.50.[^4] ACRP Report 175 recommends STI in the 0.46–0.53 band with a target of 0.50 for terminal public address, and a signal-to-noise ratio of 10–15 dB(A) in pier-style departure lounges.[^5] IEC 60268-16:2020 supplies the measurement method.[^6] The loud, reverberant terminal is the one that fails these numbers.

Three propositions carry the case. First, design-time acoustic treatment sits roughly two to four times below retrofit at the vendor-benchmark midpoints — meaningful money against a program the Authority is about to specify.[^7] Second, the enforcement vehicle already exists: MWAA's Design Manual and MASTERSPEC-derived specifications bind consultants and contractors today; extending that reach into tenant audio is the design objective of the amendment, not a present-tense claim.[^8] Third, the window is finite. MWAA's debt-service coverage compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× over the next five years while ~$5.5 billion of new debt phases in;[^9] every standard adopted after design freeze becomes a change order or a defended exemption.

The mandatory-versus-context split falls out of the evidence. Common to both airports: intelligibility floors, ADS mapping, commissioning under representative traffic (verification-with-cure-period, not a TCO gate), accessibility redundancy, announcement governance with audit logging. Varying by airport: the physical toolkit that hits those numbers inside a Saarinen-era head-house, a slot-controlled DCA peak, and a new-build IAD midfield concourse. The Board decision is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## The Loud Terminal Is the Unintelligible One

Start not at an airport. Start in a hospital intensive-care unit. Trade-press reporting summarizes a Johns Hopkins ICU observation of 771 alarms per bed per day over twelve days — a figure the Council's research pass could not resolve to a primary study but that recurs in the alarm-fatigue literature the Joint Commission cites as its evidentiary base.[^10] Clinicians had stopped hearing the alarms. The alarm that mattered sounded like the alarm that did not. The Joint Commission's response was not to reduce alarms in the name of patient comfort. It was National Patient Safety Goal NPSG.06.01.01, phased in 2014 and 2016, which required every accredited hospital to inventory its alarms, rank them, govern them, and prove it did so.[^11] The insight was that indiscriminate broadcast is itself the safety failure.

Now picture — as an operating pattern, not a dated observation — the IAD international connecting bank in the late-afternoon peak. A gate change pages overhead in Concourse C. A TSA advisory. Two boarding calls at adjacent gates. A courtesy page for a lost party. A concessions employee overhead reaching a colleague. On the record, the announcement summary looks like operations serving the passenger. In the acoustic reality of a reverberant holdroom shared with two adjacent gates, it is the paradigm case of the alarm no one hears. IAD moved 29.01 million passengers in 2025, 10.53 million of them international — the highest 2025 growth rate among the fifty largest U.S. airports.[^12] DCA moved 24.89 million, down 5.4% in the wake of the January collision and the 43-day federal shutdown.[^13] Every one of those passengers arrived expecting the announcement that matters to reach them. The question is whether the buildings the announcement travels through were specified to let it.

MWAA is now about to specify — from the studs up — an enormous run of new terminal space. On July 29, 2026, the President, the U.S. Transportation Secretary, United's CEO, and MWAA jointly announced a Dulles transformation of more than 5 million square feet: Concourses C and D replaced, AeroTrain extended, mobile lounges retired, Saarinen's Main Terminal preserved.[^14] The announcement summary describes concourses, baggage systems, and Customs and Border Protection facilities. It does not mention a single acoustic or public-address specification. The provisional program figure of $20 billion-plus will settle as Board action and program-budget amendments follow; the committed AUL figures are already firm at approximately $6.99 billion IAD and $2.39 billion DCA, the latter including the eventual replacement of the Terminal 1 main concourse.[^15] Every one of those square feet will be scoped, procured, and commissioned inside the next standards window. Standards adopted late are argued as change orders. And the anchor tenant is not silent about the price: Cranky Flier's coverage of the July 29 announcement described United CEO Scott Kirby as visibly reluctant, and cited an airline briefing pegging fully-loaded IAD transformation CPE at roughly $90.64 per enplanement versus $12.88 today.[^16] That fact does not weaken the case for a Design Manual amendment. It strengthens it: a standard that lives in the Manual survives program shrinkage, re-scoping, or an administration change; a case built on program durability does not.

The obvious objection surfaces first. After NTSB AIR-26/02, no MWAA board member wants a policy that could be paraphrased on cable news as "airport cuts safety announcements." That instinct is correct. This article agrees — and that agreement is the argument. The trap in the "silent airport" literature is the equation of quietness with subtraction. What the evidence base actually supports is the inverse. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 sets an intelligibility floor for emergency voice systems that the code writers scoped to Acoustically Distinguishable Spaces because they knew a terminal is not one acoustic environment.[^4] The way you fail that floor is not by being too quiet. It is by being loud, reverberant, and cross-broadcast — precisely the condition an unspecified terminal defaults to. High ambient noise pushes STI down the qualitative bands defined by IEC 60268-16 (Bad, Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent).[^6] The louder the terminal, the less intelligible the emergency announcement. Every acoustic and PA-zoning requirement in the recommended standard flows from that inversion.

The peer set frequently held up as models is, on close reading, a category error — and that is liberating. London City Airport has operated a silent policy since 2008, extended in 2016.[^17] Helsinki-Vantaa (since 2015) and Copenhagen each describe reduced-announcement operating models with information moved to flight-information displays and airport apps.[^18] These three are small, single-terminal, point-to-point operations with traveler mixes that were quieter in the first place. Amsterdam Schiphol is different: a genuinely large intercontinental transfer hub whose reduced-announcement regime is anchored on FIDS density and a passenger base that expects an app-plus-screens channel.[^18] What none of the four has published, in any source this Council found, is measured intelligibility, missed-boarding, or channel-redundancy data. They are policy analogues for MWAA — not design precedents. Schiphol's difference from IAD is not traffic mix; it is passenger expectation and system density. Copying any of the peers' surface artifacts into IAD's Saarinen-adjacent geometry and connection-dominant international flow produces the opposite of the peers' outcome. MWAA does not need to import their brand. It needs to out-engineer them.

SFO is the closest North American precedent, and it shows both the model and the failure mode at once. SFO reports that its Quiet Airport program removed roughly 90 minutes of daily terminal announcement time, cut individual paging from 492 to 261 occurrences per day, and reduced overall onsite noise by more than 40%.[^19] Every one of those figures is SFO's own; the Council could not identify a published measurement methodology behind any of them in the industry record. Trade press repeats the numbers verbatim. In the same record are documented passenger complaints and missed-flight anecdotes.[^20] The operating model — zoned delivery, discontinued cross-terminal final calls, gate-area retention — is well-described and worth borrowing. The outcome numbers are not audited evidence, and MWAA should quote them as SFO reports them, not as facts. The lesson is not "don't do this." The lesson is that announcement reduction without intelligibility engineering and channel redundancy is subtraction, not design.

The people who most need to hear are the test the recommendation has to pass. 28 CFR 35.160 obligates MWAA, as a public entity, to ensure effective communication with people with disabilities.[^21] The 2010 ADA Standards §219 and §706 require assistive-listening systems in each assembly area where audible communication is integral to use.[^22] Airlines operating at IAD and DCA are subject to 14 CFR Part 382, which imposes accessible-information requirements at gate, ticket, and customer-service counters, and — under the December 12, 2016 kiosk amendment — requires that airline-installed automated airport kiosks at U.S. airports with 10,000-plus enplanements be an accessible model **until at least 25%** of installed machines at each airport are accessible.[^23] IAD and DCA, as Part 139 certificated airports, carry emergency-communication requirements at the airfield boundary.[^24] IAD's Saarinen Main Terminal was determined eligible for the National Register in 1978 as part of the Dulles Airport Historic District; eligibility, not listing, is the current federal status, and Section 106 consultation attaches to eligible properties.[^25] ACRP Report 239 (2023) treats digital-first substitution as a partial rather than a sufficient channel for travelers with disabilities and older adults.[^26] Read together, these floors say the same thing: any regime that reduces broadcast without specifying redundant visual, digital, staffed, and hearing-loop channels compounds exclusion. A standard that mandates those redundancies binds MWAA harder than current practice, not softer. If MWAA were seeking ambience, it would not be volunteering for measurable compliance.

Governance is the technology. An ACI-NA-published industry article frames the newer generation of automated announcement platforms in terms MWAA's Design Manual can actually specify: centralized management, replay and audit logs of every broadcast, elimination of manual-initiation errors, and templated multi-language delivery.[^27] The endorsement is directional, not authoritative — the framework earns its place in the amendment because it is sound engineering, not because ACI-NA published it. That reframing matters. It converts "announcement policy" — a customer-experience conversation that dies in a change-management meeting — into operational integrity with an enforcement trail an internal auditor and a fire marshal can both read. This is the Joint Commission move, applied to airports: inventory, rank, govern, log. It is also what makes the tenant question tractable — but only tractable, not yet closed. At preferential-use gates, the podium PA is effectively airline-owned. United operates approximately 50 jetbridge gates and moves about 67% of IAD traffic; American holds roughly 53% of DCA market share.[^28] A standard that cannot reach tenant audio governs half the soundscape. The Council's proposition is not that today's Design Manual already reaches the podium microphone; it is that the amendment, once verified against the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest provisions and the tenant-technology-standards apparatus, should be written to reach it. That verification is step one.

The window and the vehicle exist together, and both are finite. MWAA's Design Manual is the seven-volume mandatory guide MWAA already updates annually, with airport-specific supporting volumes for IAD and DCA.[^2] A quiet-by-design amendment is not a new bureaucracy; it is a chapter added to an apparatus that already binds consultants and contractors and — through tenant technology standards and MASTERSPEC-derived specifications, to the extent the AUL and lease agreements permit — the airlines and concessionaires whose systems set the soundscape today. What the Council did not verify is what that Manual currently says about acoustics, PA zoning, STI, or ambient-noise thresholds. That verification is step one; the shape of the amendment (new volume versus insertion into Volume 2) turns on it. The financial case does not rest on new capital. It rests on rework avoidance. Commercial pricing guides put new-construction acoustic ceiling treatment in the $5–$15 per square foot range and retrofit acoustic panel treatment in the $18–$45 per square foot range — different products from different vendors, both non-airport, both medium-confidence directional benchmarks that airside badging, escort, and night-work premiums would raise further. Taken as separate ranges rather than a derived ratio, they point in the same direction: design-time treatment is roughly two to four times below retrofit at the midpoints.[^7] Against the committed IAD AUL program of ~$6.99 billion, an order-of-magnitude range for the full quiet-by-design stack — ceiling treatment premium, FIDS redundancy, mobile notification platform — of $12–$40 million sits between roughly 0.17% and 0.57% of the program (0.06%–0.20% against the provisional $20 billion-plus figure, if that announcement resolves at scale).[^7] MWAA's coverage window makes the case sharper, not softer: DSCR compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× through 2029 while approximately $5.5 billion of new debt phases in and adjusted debt per O&D enplanement peaks near $400 in 2028.[^9] In that window, the easy Board sell is the specification that prevents late-stage redesign. The hard sell is the recovery-eligible retrofit. The recommendation is the first one.

So the standard writes itself as a two-tier document. Mandatory at both airports: an Acoustically Distinguishable Space map as a design deliverable; intelligibility floors per NFPA 72 Chapter 24 (STI ≥0.45 at 90% of points, average ≥0.50) and ACRP 175 (target 0.50, band 0.46–0.53, SNR 10–15 dB(A)); measurement per IEC 60268-16:2020, with the standard's explicit exclusion of fluctuating noise honored — commissioning under representative traffic, verification-with-cure-period rather than a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy gate;[^29] accessibility floors per 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, and 14 CFR Part 382; hearing-conservation compliance per 29 CFR 1910.95 (85 dB(A) 8-hour TWA);[^30] and announcement governance with audit logging, tier discipline, and template control. Common in principle but tiered by context: PA zoning depth, sensory-quiet zone scope (ANSI/ASA S12.60 references NC 27 / 35 dB(A) as a low-stimulation benchmark),[^31] tenant-audio enforcement mechanics, and the physical toolkit that hits the numbers inside a listed Saarinen-eligible head-house versus a new-build midfield concourse versus a Pelli-era DCA terminal.

Exercise it first where the friction is lowest. Subject to Terminal 1 project phase — which the Council did not verify and which is on the 90-day list — the DCA Terminal 1 main concourse replacement is the natural choice: smaller, less politicized, and less encumbered by the July 2026 political stack around Dulles. If Terminal 1 has already moved past scope definition, the reference application shifts to IAD C/D program elements still in programming. Adopting the standard on the first eligible project — as a standing requirement, not a pilot — gives the Office of Engineering the reference application every follow-on program element will inherit. Concourse E, opening this fall at IAD with fourteen new United gates,[^32] is the natural place to capture the first commissioned STI and ambient-noise baseline under representative traffic. That baseline becomes the number every future MWAA facility is measured against.

The counter-case deserves an honest hearing. The strongest version is not the one about SFO methodology or European peers. It is the contrarian's redundancy argument and its smaller alternative. The redundancy claim is that NFPA 72, ADA §219, and ACRP 175 already set the intelligibility and assistive-listening floors that matter most, so a new MWAA standard will primarily regulate the discretionary space above the floor. This is half-right. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 binds *emergency* voice systems; it does not require ADS mapping as a general-terminal design deliverable, does not govern routine PA, and does not compel governance of manual-initiation error. ADA §219 sets receiver counts and hearing-aid compatibility; it does not set an intelligibility floor for daily PA. ACRP 175 is research guidance, not code. The marginal content the MWAA amendment adds — an ADS map required at each design milestone, commissioning under representative traffic, audit-logged announcement governance, channel-redundancy floors at Part 382-touching accessibility levels, and a tenant-audio boundary tied to the AUL — is exactly what those existing floors do not require. The contrarian's alternative — a mandatory 30%-design Communications-Environment Design Review with no new bi-airport thresholds — is worth adopting *inside* the amendment as the enforcement checkpoint, not as a substitute for the specification. Review without a specification produces a review of nothing.

The premature-specification objection is more serious, and the v1 draft did not treat it seriously enough. Rigid overlays written before program-brief maturity are themselves a change-order driver, and STI commissioning gates on the critical path of Concourse C/D or DCA Terminal 1 in this coverage window would be a bond-rating conversation, not a comfort conversation. The response is not to abandon specification; it is to write the commissioning language as verification-with-cure-period, with airline-recovery exposure explicitly excluded, and to make the ADS map a design-milestone deliverable rather than a TCO condition. That is the version above.

Three smaller objections remain. IEC 60268-16 explicitly excludes fluctuating noise from its measurement model, and terminals are the paradigm fluctuating-noise environment; the response is to specify measurement under representative traffic as an MWAA requirement, not to abandon measurement.[^29] The most-cited North American precedent has no published measurement methodology; borrowing its language without demanding its data inherits the flaw. The Design Manual pathway is slower than the case for standards implies, and the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest provisions may constrain tenant-audio enforcement — the reason step one is verification, not drafting.

Return to the opening image. In 2013, the Joint Commission did not tell hospitals to reduce alarms. It told them to prove they knew which ones mattered, that the mattering ones would be heard, and that the machinery for governing the rest was on the record. Ten years later, the ICUs are not silent. They are audible in the right places, at the right times, to the right people. That is what a quiet-by-design standard buys an airport. The board question is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## Airport Decision

**Owner:** MWAA President and CEO; Office of Engineering (drafting authority); Board of Directors (adoption).

**Decision:** Direct the Office of Engineering to draft a tiered quiet-by-design amendment to the MWAA Design Manual, framed as a communications-integrity and intelligibility standard, in time to govern the scope documents for the first eligible major terminal package — the DCA Terminal 1 replacement if its phase status permits, otherwise the earliest IAD C/D program element still in programming.

**First 90 days:** (1) Verify current acoustic, PA-zoning, STI, and ambient-noise content of the Design Manual and Specifications. (2) Verify DCA Terminal 1 project phase and identify the first eligible IAD C/D program element as a fallback pilot. (3) Verify the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest and consultation provisions as they apply to acoustic, PA, and tenant-audio content, and confirm the tenant-technology-standards apparatus that would carry the tenant tier. (4) Draft mandatory-tier language keyed to NFPA 72 Ch. 24, ACRP 175, IEC 60268-16:2020 (with representative-traffic measurement and verification-with-cure-period commissioning), 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, 14 CFR Part 382, and 29 CFR 1910.95. (5) Commission a first ADS map and baseline STI/ambient reading at IAD Concourse E under representative traffic within 90 days of opening.

**Cost order of magnitude:** $12M–$40M one-time across the full stack (ceiling treatment premium, FIDS redundancy, mobile-notification platform); the standalone CPE effect approaches zero if the standard rides inside program financing for the committed AUL program.[^7]

**Dependencies:** Airline consultation under the 2025 AUL (MII provisions not verified in this run); tenant technology standards for concessions and airline club audio; Section 106 consultation on any Saarinen-adjacent modification (property is Register-eligible, not listed); FAA/AHJ concurrence on NFPA 72 edition in force.

**Leading indicator:** First eligible major terminal-package scope documents released with mandatory quiet-by-design language cited by section.

**Failure mode / stop condition:** If the current Design Manual already contains equivalent or stronger acoustic and PA-zoning requirements, the recommendation contracts to a governance-and-verification amendment rather than a new volume. If the AUL's MII text materially constrains tenant-audio enforcement, the tenant tier drops from mandatory to recommended pending renegotiation. If DCA Terminal 1 is past scope-definition when the amendment is ready, the reference application shifts to the earliest eligible IAD C/D program element still in programming.

**What would change this recommendation:** Publication of a measured MWAA STI/ambient baseline showing existing terminal spaces already meet ACRP 175 and NFPA 72 targets under representative traffic. Alternatively, MWAA-published PA telemetry showing that announcement density is already below the peer-airport reduced-practice band. Neither is currently on the record.
