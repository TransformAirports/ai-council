## Executive Summary

MWAA holds approximately $6.99 billion of committed capital at IAD and $2.39 billion at DCA under the fifteen-year Airline Use and Lease Agreement effective January 1, 2025.[^1] On July 29, 2026, the President, the U.S. Transportation Secretary, United's CEO, and MWAA jointly announced a Dulles transformation described at more than $20 billion — provisional pending Board action, program-budget amendment, and airline-consent documents.[^14] Every square foot of committed and announced work will pass through the seven-volume MWAA Design Manual, described by the Authority as "a mandatory guide with the force of law on the airport property."[^2]

The Council's recommendation is narrow: a tiered communications-integrity standard added to the Design Manual this capital cycle, issued by the Office of Engineering under delegated standards-setting authority, taken to the Board as an information item at the next Business Administration Committee, and escalated to a Board resolution only if AAAC consultation surfaces material airline objection or an AUL cost-center reclassification. Reference application, subject to phase verification: the DCA Terminal 1 concourse replacement; fallback, the earliest IAD C/D program element still in programming. Concourse E, opening this fall, carries the first commissioned STI and ambient baseline under representative traffic.

Adopt the internal engineering name "quiet-by-design." Use the external policy name — "communications-integrity standard" — in every board and press document. The framing dies on cable news the first Friday after any DCA incident otherwise. After the January 29, 2025 midair collision at DCA and the February 2026 NTSB final report, no board member should sign a policy paraphrasable as "reduced safety broadcasts."[^3] The standard is defensible only as the opposite: a floor that raises the probability the announcement that matters — the gate change, the shelter-in-place, the IROPS diversion — reaches the passenger who needs it. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 requires a Speech Transmission Index of at least 0.45 at 90% of measurement locations within each Acoustically Distinguishable Space, average 0.50.[^4] ACRP 175 targets 0.50 in the 0.46–0.53 band with a signal-to-noise ratio of 10–15 dB(A) in pier-style departure lounges.[^5] IEC 60268-16:2020 supplies the measurement method.[^6] The reverberant, cross-broadcast terminal is the one that fails these numbers.

Three propositions carry the case. First, design-time acoustic treatment sits roughly two to four times below retrofit at commercial-benchmark midpoints — meaningful money against a program MWAA is about to specify.[^7] Second, the enforcement vehicle already exists: the Design Manual and MASTERSPEC-derived specifications bind consultants and contractors today; extending that reach into tenant audio is the design objective, contingent on the 2025 AUL verification.[^8] Third, the window is narrow, and directional. MWAA debt-service coverage compresses from 1.63× in 2024 to roughly 1.3× through 2029 as ~$5.5 billion of new debt phases in;[^9] added specification cost inside programs already in design would cut against DSCR — which is exactly why the amendment must be scoped to work still in programming or early-scope, not to programs already in design or procurement. That distinction is the difference between the easy Board sell and the wrong one. The Board question is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## The Loud Terminal Is the Unintelligible One

Start not at an airport. Start in a hospital intensive-care unit. Trade-press summaries of a Johns Hopkins ICU observation put alarm rates at 771 per bed per day over twelve days — a figure this Council could not resolve to a primary study but that recurs in the alarm-fatigue literature the Joint Commission cites as its evidentiary base.[^10] Clinicians had stopped hearing the alarms. The alarm that mattered sounded like the alarm that did not. The Joint Commission's response was not to reduce alarms in the name of patient comfort. It was National Patient Safety Goal NPSG.06.01.01, phased in 2014 and 2016, which required every accredited hospital to inventory its alarms, rank them, govern them, and prove it did so.[^11] Indiscriminate broadcast was itself the safety failure.

Picture — as an operating pattern, not a dated observation — the IAD international connecting bank in the late-afternoon peak. A gate change pages overhead in Concourse C. A TSA advisory. Two boarding calls at adjacent gates. A courtesy page. A concessions employee overhead reaching a colleague. On paper the summary reads like operations serving the passenger. In the acoustic reality of a reverberant holdroom shared with two adjacent gates, it is the alarm no one hears. IAD moved 29.01 million passengers in 2025, 10.53 million international — the highest 2025 growth rate among the fifty largest U.S. airports.[^12] DCA moved 24.89 million, down 5.4% in the wake of the January collision and the 43-day federal shutdown.[^13] Every one of those passengers arrived expecting the announcement that matters to reach them. The question is whether the buildings the announcement travels through were specified to let it.

MWAA is about to specify — from the studs up — an enormous run of new terminal space. On July 29, 2026, the President, the U.S. Transportation Secretary, United's CEO, and MWAA jointly announced a Dulles transformation of more than 5 million square feet: Concourses C and D replaced, AeroTrain extended, mobile lounges retired, Saarinen's Main Terminal preserved.[^14] The announcement summary describes concourses, baggage systems, and CBP facilities. It does not mention a single acoustic or public-address specification. The provisional $20 billion-plus figure will settle as Board action follows; the committed AUL figures are already firm at approximately $6.99 billion IAD and $2.39 billion DCA, the latter including the eventual replacement of the Terminal 1 main concourse.[^15] Every one of those square feet will be scoped inside the next standards window. The anchor tenant is not silent about the price: Cranky Flier's coverage described United CEO Scott Kirby as visibly reluctant, and cited an airline briefing pegging fully-loaded IAD transformation CPE at roughly $90.64 per enplanement versus $12.88 today.[^16] That fact strengthens the case for a Design Manual amendment. A standard that lives in the Manual survives program shrinkage, re-scoping, or an administration change. A case built on program durability does not.

The obvious objection surfaces first. After NTSB AIR-26/02, no board member wants a policy paraphrased as "airport cuts safety announcements." That instinct is correct — and it is the argument. The trap in the silent-airport literature is the equation of quietness with subtraction. What the evidence base actually supports is the inverse. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 sets an intelligibility floor for emergency voice systems scoped to Acoustically Distinguishable Spaces because a terminal is not one acoustic environment.[^4] You fail that floor by being loud, reverberant, and cross-broadcast — precisely the condition an unspecified terminal defaults to. High ambient noise pushes STI down the qualitative bands defined by IEC 60268-16.[^6] The louder the terminal, the less intelligible the emergency announcement. Every acoustic and PA-zoning requirement in the recommended standard flows from that inversion.

The peer set commonly cited is, on close reading, a category error. London City has operated a silent policy since 2008, extended in 2016.[^17] Helsinki-Vantaa (since 2015) and Copenhagen each moved information to FIDS and airport apps.[^18] These three are small, single-terminal, point-to-point operations, quieter to begin with. Amsterdam Schiphol is different: a genuinely large intercontinental transfer hub whose reduced-announcement regime is anchored on FIDS density and a passenger base that expects an app-plus-screens channel.[^18] None of the four has published measured intelligibility, missed-boarding, or channel-redundancy data. They are policy analogues — not design precedents. MWAA does not need to import their brand. It needs to out-engineer them.

SFO is the closest North American precedent, and it shows both the model and the failure mode. SFO reports that its Quiet Airport program removed roughly 90 minutes of daily terminal announcement time, cut individual paging from 492 to 261 occurrences per day, and reduced onsite noise by more than 40%.[^19] Every figure is SFO's own; no published measurement methodology appears in the industry record this Council could identify. In the same record are documented passenger complaints and missed-flight anecdotes.[^20] The operating model — zoned delivery, discontinued cross-terminal final calls, gate-area retention — is worth borrowing. The outcome numbers are not audited evidence, and MWAA should quote them as SFO reports them, not as facts. The lesson: announcement reduction without intelligibility engineering and channel redundancy is subtraction, not design.

The people who most need to hear are the test the recommendation must pass. 28 CFR 35.160 obligates MWAA to ensure effective communication with people with disabilities.[^21] The 2010 ADA Standards §219 and §706 require assistive-listening systems in each assembly area where audible communication is integral to use.[^22] Airlines at IAD and DCA are subject to 14 CFR Part 382, which — under the December 12, 2016 kiosk amendment — requires airline-installed automated kiosks at U.S. airports with 10,000-plus enplanements to be an accessible model **until at least 25%** of installed machines at each airport are accessible.[^23] IAD's Saarinen Main Terminal was determined eligible for the National Register in 1978 as part of the Dulles Airport Historic District; eligibility, not listing, is the federal status, and Section 106 attaches to eligible properties.[^25] Section 106 consultation with the Virginia SHPO and ACHP runs six to eighteen months, and further if adverse effect requires a Memorandum of Agreement — a schedule reason to keep the amendment's first applications outside the eligible-district envelope and to treat any later application inside it as a formal Section 106 precedent step. ACRP Report 239 (2023) treats digital-first substitution as partial, not sufficient, for travelers with disabilities and older adults.[^26] A regime that reduces broadcast without specifying redundant visual, digital, staffed, and hearing-loop channels compounds exclusion. A standard that mandates those redundancies binds MWAA harder than current practice, not softer.

Governance is the technology. An ACI-NA-published industry article frames the newer generation of automated announcement platforms in terms the Design Manual can specify: centralized management, replay and audit logs of every broadcast, elimination of manual-initiation errors, templated multi-language delivery.[^27] The endorsement is directional; the framework earns its place because it is sound engineering. This is the Joint Commission move applied to airports: inventory, rank, govern, log — with a standing Announcement Governance Committee (co-chaired Engineering/Operations/Communications/IT, rotating air-carrier member) reporting annually to the Business Administration Committee of the Board. Enforcement mechanics matter as much as the technology: an annual acoustic inspection cycle keyed to lease renewal, 30/60/90-day cure notices, escalation to lease-management action only for pattern noncompliance. Without those mechanics, airlines and concessionaires read the standard as paper and comply nominally.

That reframing makes the tenant question tractable, but not closed. At preferential-use gates the podium PA is effectively airline-owned. United operates approximately 50 jetbridge gates and moves about 67% of IAD traffic; American holds roughly 53% of DCA market share.[^28] A standard that cannot reach tenant audio governs half the soundscape. The proposition is not that today's Design Manual reaches the podium microphone; it is that the amendment, once verified against the 2025 AUL's majority-in-interest provisions and the tenant-technology-standards apparatus, should be written to reach it. The tenant question consequently runs on a critical-path airline-consultation lane — confidential station-manager briefings in weeks one through three, formal AAAC briefing at the next scheduled meeting, thirty-day comment window on the draft, written MWAA position on cost-recovery categorization by week twelve — not on a footnote.

The vehicle exists; the window is narrow. The Design Manual is a seven-volume mandatory guide MWAA updates annually.[^2] The financial case rests on rework avoidance, not new capital. Commercial pricing guides put new-construction acoustic ceiling treatment in the $5–$15 per square foot range and retrofit panel treatment in the $18–$45 per square foot range — different products from different non-airport vendors, both medium-confidence directional benchmarks. Taken as separate ranges, they point in the same direction: design-time treatment sits roughly two to four times below retrofit at the midpoints.[^7] An order-of-magnitude range for the full quiet-by-design stack — ceiling treatment premium, FIDS redundancy, mobile notification platform — of $12–$40 million sits between roughly 0.17% and 0.57% of the committed IAD program.[^7] The DSCR window is a scoping constraint, not a general urgency argument: added specification cost inside programs already in design cuts the wrong way. Route the amendment to work still in programming; leave programs in design or procurement to the follow-on Governance-only tier.

The standard writes itself as a two-tier document. Mandatory at both airports: an Acoustically Distinguishable Space map as a design deliverable; intelligibility floors per NFPA 72 Chapter 24 (STI ≥ 0.45 at 90% of points, average ≥ 0.50) and ACRP 175 (target 0.50, band 0.46–0.53, SNR 10–15 dB(A)); measurement per IEC 60268-16:2020, with the standard's explicit exclusion of fluctuating noise honored — commissioning under representative traffic, verification-with-cure-period rather than a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy gate;[^29] accessibility floors per 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, and 14 CFR Part 382; hearing-conservation compliance per 29 CFR 1910.95 (85 dB(A) 8-hour TWA);[^30] announcement governance with audit logging; concessionaire televisions and gate-lounge audio — the loudest ambient contributors most standards miss — kept inside scope (no ambient television audio in holdrooms except captioned displays; concessionaire audio contained within roughly ±3 dB of the lease line, exact figure to be set in Design Manual verification; closed-captioning required); and the load-bearing paragraph most silent-airport writing omits — an IROPS override. During declared irregular operations, the Airport Duty Manager holds a named override authority to exceed announcement-density templates; every deviation is captured in the audit log; a post-event reconciliation report goes to the Announcement Governance Committee. A standard that does not survive the first Wednesday afternoon of gate holds will not be believed for the Wednesday afternoon it was written for. Common in principle but tiered by context: PA zoning depth, sensory-quiet zone scope (ANSI/ASA S12.60 references NC 27 / 35 dB(A) as a low-stimulation benchmark),[^31] tenant-audio enforcement mechanics, and the physical toolkit that hits the numbers inside a Saarinen-eligible head-house versus a new-build midfield concourse versus a Pelli-era DCA terminal.

Exercise it first where the friction is lowest. Subject to Terminal 1 project phase — which the Council did not verify — the DCA Terminal 1 main concourse replacement is the natural choice: smaller, less politicized, and less encumbered by the July 2026 political stack around Dulles. If Terminal 1 has moved past scope definition, the reference application shifts to the earliest IAD C/D program element still in programming, and Concourse E — opening this fall with fourteen new United gates[^32] — carries the first commissioned STI and ambient baseline under representative traffic. That baseline becomes the number every future MWAA facility is measured against.

The counter-case deserves an honest hearing. The strongest version is the contrarian's redundancy argument: NFPA 72, ADA §219, and ACRP 175 already set the floors that matter most, so a new MWAA standard mostly regulates the discretionary space above the floor. This is half-right. NFPA 72 Chapter 24 binds *emergency* voice; it does not require ADS mapping as a general-terminal design deliverable, does not govern routine PA, does not compel governance of manual-initiation error. ADA §219 sets receiver counts, not intelligibility floors for daily PA. ACRP 175 is research guidance, not code. The marginal content the MWAA amendment adds — an ADS map at each design milestone, commissioning under representative traffic, audit-logged governance, channel-redundancy floors at Part 382-touching accessibility levels, an IROPS override, TV and concessions media limits, and a tenant-audio boundary tied to the AUL — is exactly what those existing floors do not require. The contrarian's alternative — a mandatory 30%-design Communications-Environment Design Review with no new bi-airport thresholds — belongs inside the amendment as the enforcement checkpoint, not as a substitute. Review without a specification is a review of nothing.

The premature-specification objection is more serious. Rigid overlays written before program-brief maturity are themselves a change-order driver, and STI commissioning gates on the critical path in this coverage window would be a bond-rating conversation, not a comfort conversation. The response is verification-with-cure-period commissioning, airline-recovery exposure explicitly excluded, and the ADS map as a design-milestone deliverable rather than a TCO condition. Three smaller objections remain: IEC 60268-16 excludes fluctuating noise from its measurement model[^29] — specify measurement under representative traffic rather than abandon measurement; the most-cited North American precedent has no published methodology — borrow its operating model, not its numbers; the Design Manual pathway is slower than urgency suggests — which is why nothing about the case allows waiting for the next coverage cycle.

Return to the opening image. In 2013, the Joint Commission did not tell hospitals to reduce alarms. It told them to prove they knew which ones mattered, that the mattering ones would be heard, and that the machinery for governing the rest was on the record. Ten years later the ICUs are not silent. They are audible in the right places, at the right times, to the right people. That is what a communications-integrity standard buys an airport. The Board question is not whether to fund a program. It is whether to write the paragraph.

## Airport Decision Card

**Owner and instrument.** MWAA President and CEO sponsors. Vice President for Engineering drafts and issues the amendment under the delegated standards-setting authority the Office of Engineering exercises for annual Design Manual revisions. The Board receives the amendment as an information item at the next scheduled Business Administration Committee (target: November 2026 committee cycle, confirm on the day the meeting notice publishes). A formal Board resolution is initiated only if AAAC consultation surfaces material airline objection or if the cost-center analysis under the 2025 AUL characterizes any element that requires MII treatment.

**External framing convention.** Internal engineering name: "quiet-by-design." External policy name in all Board papers, press releases, DOT correspondence, and airline briefings: "MWAA Communications-Integrity Standard." MWAA Communications drafts and enforces the convention.

**Airline-consultation lane (parallel to drafting, on the critical path).**
- Weeks 1–3: confidential briefings to United and American executive station managers and their capital counsels.
- Week 4: formal Airport Airlines Affairs Committee (AAAC) briefing at the next scheduled meeting.
- Weeks 4–8: thirty-day comment window on the draft amendment language.
- Week 10: written MWAA response letter cataloging airline concerns.
- Week 12: written MWAA position on cost-recovery categorization keyed to specific 2025 AUL cost centers (Terminal Rentals, Landing Fees, Airline Support Areas, etc.).
- No amendment language enters any scope document until the Week 12 position letter is on the record.

**First 180 days, branched.**
- **Days 1–30 — verification.** (a) Current acoustic, PA-zoning, STI, and ambient-noise content of the Design Manual and Specifications; (b) DCA Terminal 1 project phase; (c) 2025 AUL MII and consultation text as it applies to acoustic/PA/tenant-audio content; (d) tenant-technology-standards apparatus reach; (e) NFPA 72 edition in force at each AHJ (Loudoun/Fairfax Fire Marshals for IAD, DC Fire Marshal for DCA); (f) Section 106 posture with Virginia SHPO and ACHP for any Saarinen-adjacent scope; (g) FAA Part 139 emergency-communications compliance baseline.
- **Days 30–60 — drafting.** Mandatory-tier language keyed to NFPA 72 Ch. 24, ACRP 175, IEC 60268-16:2020 (representative-traffic measurement, verification-with-cure-period), 28 CFR 35.160, ADA §219/§706, 14 CFR Part 382, 29 CFR 1910.95. Acoustic engineering support via task order under an existing on-call A/E IDIQ if available; new Brooks-Act Qualifications-Based Selection if not.
- **Days 60–90 — stakeholder.** Airline consultation lane above; Fire Marshal briefings at each AHJ; DOT Office of Public Affairs coordination given the July 29 co-brand; Airports Council International-North America and Airlines for America briefings.
- **Days 90–120 — branched application.** **Branch A** (Terminal 1 in scope-definition): draft DCA Terminal 1 pilot application language. **Branch B** (Terminal 1 past scope-definition): qualify the earliest eligible IAD C/D program element still in programming. Under either branch: instrument Concourse E baseline STI and ambient measurement.
- **Days 120–180 — adoption and baseline.** Board information item at Business Administration Committee; publish amendment in the annual Design Manual revision; commission first ADS map and baseline STI/ambient reading at Concourse E under representative traffic within 90 days of opening.

**Procurement path (per line item).**
| Line item | Vehicle | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Acoustic engineering support | Task order under existing on-call A/E IDIQ if available; new Brooks-Act QBS if not | Multi-month if new solicitation; identify vehicle in Week 1 |
| Concourse E baseline measurement | Task order under existing commissioning contract if scope permits | Otherwise scoped as an add to the acoustic-engineering task order |
| Mobile notification platform | MWAA IT governance path; evaluate against any active enterprise-notification contract | Cyber review required; potential AAAC briefing depending on cost recovery |
| FIDS redundancy | Rides inside identified existing capital program element | Confirm program element in Week 1 |
| Governance-platform license | Standard IT procurement | Include cyber review |

**Cost and funding source.**
- Capital stack $12M–$40M one-time, allocated roughly 60/40 IAD/DCA proportional to committed program footprint. Capital-vs-operating split refined during scoping.
- Airline recoverability characterized in the Week 12 position letter under the 2025 AUL cost-center matrix. Directional expectation, subject to that letter: ceiling treatment premium airline-recoverable inside Terminal Rentals; mobile notification platform non-recoverable MWAA IT; commissioning verification airline-recoverable inside program financing.
- Funding candidates: program contingency inside the committed AUL work; PFC amendment for the mobile notification platform; General Airport Revenue Bonds only if a stand-alone capital element requires it.
- Five-year operating tail: platform license, audit-log staffing, and commissioning verification estimated at ~$0.8M–$2.5M/year across both airports.
- FTE: 0.5–1.0 across Engineering, Operations, and IT at each airport. Confirm whether new duties fall inside existing position descriptions or require reclassification and bargaining review with the represented workforce.

**Ownership after adoption.** Engineering: standard content and revision. Operations at each airport: commissioning acceptance under representative traffic and IROPS override authority (Airport Duty Manager). IT: platform and audit-log stewardship. Communications: external framing convention. Standing Announcement Governance Committee (co-chaired Engineering/Operations/Communications/IT, rotating air-carrier member): audit-log review, template control, IROPS post-event reconciliation, annual public report to the Business Administration Committee of the Board.

**Tenant-audio enforcement architecture.** Annual acoustic inspection cycle keyed to lease renewal or Tenant Technology Standards audit; violation notice with 30/60/90-day cure; escalation to lease-management action only for documented pattern noncompliance. Framework drafted before AUL text is closed so it can inform, rather than lag, the AUL consultation.

**Dependencies.** 2025 AUL MII and cost-recovery text (verification step 1c); tenant technology standards apparatus (1d); Section 106 consultation with Virginia SHPO and ACHP on any Saarinen-adjacent application, 6–18 months and longer with MOA (1f); Fire Marshal AHJ concurrence at each airport (1e); FAA Part 139 emergency-communications compliance (1g); represented-workforce bargaining posture on any new duties; DOT Office of Public Affairs coordination given the July 29 co-brand.

**Leading indicators.**
1. Amendment language published in the next Design Manual annual revision and cited by section in the first eligible major terminal-package scope documents.
2. Commissioned STI and ambient readings at Concourse E under representative traffic within 90 days of opening, benchmarked against ACRP 175 and NFPA 72.
3. Missed-boarding, service-recovery, and ADA-effective-communication complaint rates at amendment-governed facilities, benchmarked against non-amendment facilities.

**Failure mode / stop conditions.**
- If the current Design Manual already contains equivalent or stronger acoustic and PA-zoning content, the recommendation contracts to a governance-and-verification amendment rather than a new volume.
- If the 2025 AUL MII text materially constrains tenant-audio enforcement, the tenant tier drops from mandatory to recommended pending renegotiation.
- If DCA Terminal 1 is past scope-definition, the reference application shifts to the earliest eligible IAD C/D program element still in programming.
- If the Concourse E baseline shows existing MWAA design already meets ACRP 175 and NFPA 72 under representative traffic, mandatory application contracts to governance and IROPS behavior; the acoustic performance section becomes recommended pending measured cause.
- If the first commissioning cure-period cost exceeds an MWAA-set per-project threshold (indicative $500,000), Engineering issues a hold on further application pending amendment language revision.

**What would change this recommendation.**
- Publication of a measured MWAA STI/ambient baseline showing existing spaces already meet the ACRP 175 and NFPA 72 targets under representative traffic.
- MWAA-published PA telemetry showing announcement density already below the peer-airport reduced-practice band.
- Publication of a peer-airport primary measurement methodology (e.g., SFO Airport Commission publishing measured intelligibility data), which would permit direct-copy operating patterns rather than fresh MWAA engineering.
- AAAC consultation outcome in which airlines commit to a shorter-form standard as a negotiation concession, which would reduce the amendment's marginal content.
- FAA publication of Part 139 amendments touching PA and communications, which would move requirements outside MWAA's discretion.
