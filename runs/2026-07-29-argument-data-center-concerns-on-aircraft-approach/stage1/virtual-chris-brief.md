## The Lattice — What This Thesis Activates

**1. FAA Airport District Office ↔ Uncoordinated Proposal ↔ Master Plan Timing.**
The memo notes the project has not been coordinated with the FAA ADO. The MWAA Board of Directors approved an updated master plan on July 16, 2025 (per Simple Flying, Virginia Mercury, MWAA.com), and that plan is now under FAA review. Any project that surfaces a compliance question — especially one the ADO has never seen — can complicate or delay master plan approval. The ADO's first look at this data center should not be through a noncompliance finding.

**2. FAA AIM § 7-6-16 ↔ Cooling Tower Thermal Plumes ↔ Approach Corridor Safety.**
The Aeronautical Information Manual Section 7-6-16 (titled "Avoid Flight in the Vicinity of Exhaust Plumes — Smoke Stacks and Cooling Towers") is directly applicable. The AIM defines data-center-class exhaust plumes as sources of turbulence, vertical shear, reduced visibility, oxygen depletion, engine particulate contamination, and icing. It states explicitly: these hazards "are most critical during low altitude flight in calm and cold air, especially in and around approach and departure corridors or airport traffic areas." The AIM further specifies that "significant turbulent effects of an exhaust plume can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the height of the top of the stack or cooling tower." A large-scale data center in the 19R/1L approach corridor is precisely the scenario this section was written to prevent.

**3. FAA AC 150/5190-4B (effective September 16, 2022) ↔ Incompatible Land Use Categories ↔ Electronic Interference.**
AC 150/5190-4B explicitly categorizes as incompatible: "airspace obstructions and hazards to safe navigation such as tall structures, light, glare, electronic/radio, smoke, steam, or other atmospheric interference emanating from nearby land uses." Data centers generate all five of these: operational lighting (light/glare), cooling tower emissions (steam/thermal plume), emergency diesel generators (smoke), and high-density server farms (radio frequency). FAA land-use compatibility guidance (FAA APL document III.B) further specifies that "high-energy use facilities that may affect navigational aids (NAVAIDs) are an electronic interference land use consideration for airports."

**4. AC 150/5200-33C ↔ Stormwater Retention ↔ Compounding Wildlife Hazard.**
The current version (33C) of the FAA wildlife hazard AC is direct: stormwater detention basins that retain standing water attract birds and wildlife into approach and departure airspace. The proposal includes a stormwater retention facility adjacent to the data center. This is not an incidental risk — the AC specifically addresses detention basins, requiring designs that "remain totally dry between rainfalls" and specifying that wet-bottom facilities require special mitigation. A wet stormwater facility in the approach corridor stacks a wildlife hazard on top of the light, EMI, and thermal plume risks.

**5. AC 150/5300-13B ↔ Runway Protection Zone Standards ↔ Future Capacity.**
AC 150/5300-13B (Airport Design, updated with Change 1 on August 16, 2024) establishes the Runway Protection Zone (RPZ) as a trapezoidal area protecting people and property at runway ends. While RPZ rules enumerate specific prohibited uses (residences, fuel storage, smoke-generating activities, places of public assembly), the broader principle — protecting approach areas from any development that introduces hazard or encumbrance — governs this site. Dulles's design integrates 11,000+ acres with 8,000-foot buffers from runway endpoints to the property perimeter precisely to preserve approach surface integrity. This buffer is not surplus land; it is the strategic reserve.

**6. 2025 Dulles Master Plan ↔ Fifth Runway ↔ Approach Surface Lock-In.**
MWAA's current master plan envisions a fifth runway (designated Runway 12R-30L, ~10,500 feet, east-west orientation), growth to 90 million annual passengers by 2080–2090, and interim milestones of 38 million passengers with 154 gates by 2040. The plan is under FAA review as of mid-2025. Any permanent non-aeronautical structure in the approach zone of 19R/1L forecloses options that the master plan has not foreclosed — including potential future instrument procedure refinement, runway end safety area improvements, and navigational aid siting. A data center lasts 30–40 years; the master plan runs 65. The sequencing is backwards.

**7. 2024 FAA Reauthorization Act (Section 743) ↔ ALP Review Authority ↔ FAA's Retained Safety Jurisdiction.**
Section 743 of the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 limited FAA's authority over non-aeronautical ALP changes — but preserved full FAA jurisdiction for projects that "(1) materially impact the safe and efficient operation of aircraft at, to, or from the airport; (2) adversely affect the safety of people or property on the ground as a result of aircraft operations; or (3) adversely affect the value of prior Federal investments to a significant extent." The data center triggers all three clauses. The statute's three exceptions are not obscure carve-outs; they are the exact language Jen quotes in her memo. She is not citing personal judgment — she is reciting federal law.

**8. AIP Grant Assurances ↔ Future Funding Risk ↔ Real Dollar Exposure.**
FAA enforcement under AIP grant assurances (codified via 14 CFR Part 16) can include corrective-action orders, withholding of future AIP grants, and grant-repayment requirements. MWAA receives tens of millions in AIP funding annually. Grant Assurance 21 (Compatible Land Uses) explicitly restricts "use of land adjacent to or in the immediate vicinity of the airport to activities and purposes compatible with normal airport operations, including landing and takeoff of aircraft." The data center is adjacent to an active instrument approach path. The grant assurance risk is not hypothetical — FAA Order 5190.6C (effective February 20, 2026) is the current compliance framework, and Chapter 20 addresses compatible land use in detail.

## Three Pattern Matches

### 1. The Yellowstone Wolf Effect — Systems Behave Differently When You Remove a Key Node

In the 1990s, removing wolves from Yellowstone appeared to be a local wildlife management decision. Within years, elk overgrazing had destabilized river banks, altered stream flow, and changed vegetation patterns across the entire ecosystem — what ecologists call a "trophic cascade." The airport's 8,000-foot approach buffer is a system node, not a surplus real estate decision. Placing a data center there doesn't just affect that parcel; it potentially constrains what instrument approaches can be certified, what navigational aids can be sited, how approach lighting can be configured, and whether future runway capacity can be authorized at that runway end. You don't feel the cascade until it's already happening.

*Mapping:* Wolves = approach surface buffer. Elk = competing development pressure. River stability = instrument approach certification. The cascade runs from data center approval → approach procedure limitations → capacity ceiling → competitive disadvantage against peer airports. The causal chain is long, which is why it's easy to miss.

### 2. The 1956 Interstate Highway Act — Short-Term Federal Money Forecloses Long-Term Options

The Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956 funded the interstate system at 90% federal / 10% state cost-share. States rushed to route highways through urban cores because the federal match was irresistible. Fifty years later, cities spent billions removing highways that had severed neighborhoods and blocked waterfronts. The federal money was real; so were the unintended foreclosures on future choices. A data center proposal that generates near-term lease revenue from a piece of approach-zone land is structurally identical: the revenue is real, and so is the 30-to-40-year foreclosure of aeronautical options that MWAA will eventually need.

*Mapping:* Federal highway money = data center lease revenue. Urban core routing = approach zone siting. Highway removal cost = future cost of relocating a data center or reclassifying an instrument approach. The pattern predicts that the organization that says "yes" to the short-term revenue will spend multiples dealing with the consequences.

### 3. The Chernobyl Operator's Paradox — Each Violation Is Survivable; All of Them Together Are Not

The operators at Chernobyl Reactor 4 on April 26, 1986 were not reckless; they were conducting an authorized safety test. Each individual deviation from protocol — reduced power level, disabled safety systems, modified reactor configuration — was survivable in isolation. The catastrophe emerged from the compound interaction of simultaneous, apparently manageable deviations during a non-standard operating condition. The data center proposal presents a structurally identical compound risk: approach lighting interference + thermal plume turbulence + electromagnetic interference with navigational aids + wildlife attractant from stormwater + backup generator smoke — none of which have been formally assessed — stacked in the most safety-critical corridor of the airfield. The relevant question is not whether any individual effect is dangerous in isolation. The question is what happens when a widebody on a Category I ILS approach to 1L encounters two or three simultaneously in calm, cold air at night. The FAA has not evaluated this. Neither has MWAA.

*Mapping:* Individual safety deviations = individual data center risk factors (light, plume, EMI, wildlife, generator smoke). Compound interaction during non-standard conditions = compound risk during calm-air nighttime instrument approach. RBMK reactor design flaw = the absence of a formal FAA ADO safety review before any approval was considered.

**1. Bring the ADO in Before This Memo Is Formally Responded To (Action within weeks)**
Jen's memo notes the project has not been coordinated with the FAA ADO. The first step is not to wait for a project proponent to contest the memo — it is to initiate ADO coordination now, so the FAA's view is on the record before any internal escalation. Under the 2024 Reauthorization guidance (October 2024 FAA memo on ALP approvals), FAA has a 45-day window to object to certain non-aeronautical projects. Getting the ADO's analysis in writing before any board-level discussion converts an internal planning opinion into an external regulatory determination — which is a categorically different kind of "no."
*First step:* Planning team files a formal coordination request with the ADO, submitting the project description, location relative to approach surfaces, and the specific FAA guidance implicated.

That reframe turns an adversarial conversation with a project proponent into a collaborative one: MWAA works with the developer to identify alternative sites on airport property that serve their needs without encumbering the approach corridor. This positions MWAA as a sophisticated landlord rather than a regulator, and preserves a revenue relationship.
*First step:* Planning team maps the alternative sites consistent with the master plan designation and prepares a brief for the project proponent.

**3. Use the Master Plan FAA Review as Protective Cover (Action within 60 days)**

Introducing an incompatible development into a live master plan review is exactly the kind of complication MWAA should avoid.
*First step:* MWAA Planning notifies the FAA project manager for the master plan review of the data center proposal and requests informal guidance on compatibility.

**5. Build the Compounding Risk Analysis the ADO Will Eventually Require (Action within 60 days)**

Commissioning that analysis now — before the ADO requests it — gives MWAA the findings rather than leaving the proponent to hire their own consultant. It also ensures the analysis covers the compound risk scenario (thermal plume + EMI + light + wildlife) rather than addressing each hazard in isolation, which is where the real exposure is.
