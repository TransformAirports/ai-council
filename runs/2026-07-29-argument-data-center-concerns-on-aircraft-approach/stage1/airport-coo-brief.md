### Bottom line

Do not advance this proposal. Five independent regulatory obligations converge on this site, none of which requires the structure to penetrate an approach surface to apply. This project has not been coordinated with the FAA Airport District Office, rests on a 2025 internal review that did not address federal land use obligations, and cannot proceed to design without a formal ALP amendment — an amendment the FAA is almost certain to condition or deny.

---

### Why it holds

**Runway Protection Zone prohibition.** AC 150/5300-13B (2022) categorically prohibits enclosed, occupied structures within the Runway Protection Zone. A data center is an occupied, enclosed structure. This is not a height question — it is a use prohibition. Beyond the RPZ, this site sits within both the RWY 1L approach surface and the RWY 19R departure surface, where the same AC directs airport sponsors to protect against any constraint on future runway extension or design-standard compliance.

**Approach lighting interference.** Precision instrument approaches to RWY 1L depend on the approach lighting system as the primary visual reference the moment a crew breaks out of clouds. AC 150/5340-30J designs ALS on the assumption that the system is the dominant light source in the pilot's forward field. Data centers generate continuous, uncontrolled facility lighting — perimeter security, rooftop equipment, access roads — that cannot be suppressed during Category I, II, or III operations. No FAA waiver process eliminates this conflict.

**Thermal plumes.** FAA AIM § 7-6-16 explicitly instructs pilots to avoid "cooling towers" because "significant turbulent effects of an exhaust plume can extend to heights of over 1,000 feet above the top of the stack or cooling tower." Data center air-cooled condenser arrays discharge air at 14 to 25°F above ambient. This is not speculative — the AIM names the hardware class. Placing cooling towers inside the final approach corridor of an ILS runway introduces a FAA-documented turbulence source in the most critical phase of flight.

**Wildlife attractants.** The associated stormwater retention facility creates standing water within the approach/departure surface. AC 150/5200-33C requires detention basins near airports to remain completely dry between rainfall events, with concrete lining where standing water is anticipated. This site plan does not satisfy that standard.

**NAVAID and ILS compatibility.** Data centers house large UPS arrays, power conditioning equipment, and diesel generators — all recognized sources of electromagnetic emissions. FAA Order 6750.16E (ILS Siting Criteria) identifies industrial power infrastructure as a category requiring formal compatibility evaluation. No such evaluation has been completed.

**Grant Assurance exposure.** Assurances 19 (Operation and Maintenance), 20 (Hazard Removal and Mitigation), 21 (Compatible Land Use), and 29 (Airport Layout Plan) apply independently. Assurance 29 is the gating obligation: this area is not designated for non-aeronautical development in the current master plan. Proceeding without an FAA-approved ALP amendment puts MWAA in immediate assurance violation.

---

### Strongest objection

The project team will argue that the structure does not penetrate the approach surface — no obstruction, no FAA jurisdiction — and that Section 743 of the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 limits FAA review of non-aeronautical development.

Both fail for IAD. Section 743 explicitly preserves FAA jurisdiction, without exception, over federally acquired airport land. IAD was built by the federal government; the United States holds underlying title under the 1987 lease to MWAA. The three-pronged test that overrides Section 743's narrowing — material impact on safe and efficient aircraft operations, safety risk to people or property from aircraft operations, adverse effect on federal investment value — is satisfied by lighting, plume, NAVAID, and wildlife concerns here, each independently. FAA will assert jurisdiction within the 45-day notice window.

---

### What to do now

1. **Halt project advancement** until FAA ADO formal coordination is complete.  
2. **File FAA Form 7460-1** to initiate the obstruction and airspace impact study.  
3. **Commission a NAVAID compatibility study** against FAA Order 6750.16E ILS siting criteria.  
4. **Require written Grant Assurance responses** from the proponent for Assurances 19, 20, 21, and 29, reviewed by airport compliance counsel before any further project action.  
5. **Initiate ALP amendment** under FAA ARP SOP 2.00. The 2025 land use review does not substitute for this process.  
6. **Reject the stormwater retention design** or require redesign to fully satisfy AC 150/5200-33C.

### Where the original memo was weakest
- It did not address Section 743 of the 2024 FAA Reauthorization Act — the likely counter-argument — and therefore missed the IAD-specific rebuttal (federally acquired land).
- The thermal plume argument read as opinion; AIM § 7-6-16 converts it to an FAA-documented operational hazard.
- The NAVAID concern was acknowledged but unsupported; Order 6750.16E provides the applicable standard.
- Grant Assurance 22 (Economic Nondiscrimination) is the weakest citation and may invite pushback — focus on 19, 20, 21, and 29.

### Key quantitative anchor
Data center air-cooled condenser discharge temperature: **14 to 25°F above ambient** (FAA Technical Guidance Assessment Tool — Thermal Exhaust Plume Impact). AIM § 7-6-16 establishes that effects from cooling towers can extend **over 1,000 feet above the source**. On a 3° glide slope at a point 1 nm from threshold, an aircraft is approximately 310 feet AGL — well within the documented plume hazard zone.

### Counterevidence acknowledged
No FAA case specifically addresses a data center near an ILS approach at a major hub airport in the public record. The closest precedents are:
- Industrial plume studies at power plants (FAA Technical Guidance, 2013)
- Landfill/wildlife cases at general aviation airports (AOPA, GAO review)
- Buckeye Municipal Airport (AZ) — large data center park adjacent to airport, no public FAA objection documented, but that airport has no precision approach; the safety triggers differ fundamentally

The absence of a direct precedent is not a weakness — it is an argument for extra caution, not for reduced review. The regulatory framework is unambiguous even without a named enforcement case.
