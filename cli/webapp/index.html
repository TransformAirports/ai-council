Frame the question

Topics produce summaries. Theses produce arguments.

Subject and angle, under ~8 words — it becomes the filename and the cover.

The most-read sentence of the run — every agent starts here.
The four tests
  • Dinner-party test — could someone informed say "I don't think that's true"?
  • Verb test — claim verbs (fails, will strand, should be), not survey verbs (examines, explores).
  • Number test — at least one quantity, date, or named case an agent can check.
  • Two-sided test — can you state the strongest opposing position in one sentence?
Name what to compare, where to look, or what would settle it. 5–8 items is the sweet spot.
Patterns that work
  • Failure taxonomy — "catalog 6–10 deployments that failed, and the decision that doomed each."
  • Named benchmarks — "how do high performers (FRA, AMS, ATL) actually do this?"
  • The hidden number — "stated read rates vs. real performance after 2–3 years."
  • Decision framework — end with "decision framework, not preferences."
  • What actually happens — "…and why does it differ from what enterprise IT recommends?"
  • Tie the last item to the decision you actually face.
Ask: if this report went wrong, what would it sound like? Name those failure modes.
Examples from real runs
  • "Vendor capability lists. Anything that ends with 'deploy a platform.'"
  • "Promises about computer-vision sortation that haven't proven out at scale."
  • "A polemic that ignores the genuine reasons for the status quo."
  • Keep it to 2–4 lines — a ten-line list constrains the argument into blandness.
